## Executive Summary

**Yes, a few studies show the choice reversal the forecasting page asks for, though the best are single studies, and most run through memory or comparison mode rather than through the simulation biases the page names.** The cleanest *forecast-driven* case is Hsee & Zhang (2004) Study 3. Of those choosing between recalling a failure while eating a 15 g chocolate and recalling a success while eating a 5 g one, 65% took the large chocolate. Randomly assigned experiencers of each option were happier with the success/small-chocolate option (M = 2.41 vs −0.71). So were the self-choosers themselves (2.61 vs 0.09). A separate prediction group's forecasts matched the choices (66%), and choosers' forecasts inflated the chocolate's size effect that experience did not show. No independent replication of this choice study exists. The 2021 replication covered only the two misprediction studies, and its support was "mixed". The strongest designs by construction run through *remembered* utility. Kahneman et al. (1993) had 22 of 32 people choose to repeat a cold-water trial that contained the other trial's pain plus 30 more seconds of it. In Redelmeier, Katz & Kahneman's (2003) randomised trial (n = 682), adding a milder ending improved patients' memories of colonoscopy and raised later return for a repeat colonoscopy (OR 1.41). Immune neglect, the page's own mechanism, has between-sample demonstrations: Gilbert & Ebert (2002) and Kermer et al. (2006). Kermer et al. *do* contain a choice measure, contrary to the 10-12 review's open question, but in a separate 21-person pretest. On loss aversion, Charpentier et al. (2016) found neither expected nor experienced feelings asymmetric between gains and losses. The extra weight on losses appeared only in how feelings were weighted at choice, and only when gains and losses were evaluated together. That cuts against the inference the pending Item 1 refine task draws from Kermer et al. For [P-VS1](/positions/value-in-selection/#p-vs1), the demonstrated reversals make its "fallible driver" cost concrete without discriminating between the horns. None of these studies measured present anticipatory affect at choice, which is [P-VS1](/positions/value-in-selection/#p-vs1)'s actual input. Gilbert & Wilson (2000) name a failure mode specific to a *felt* currency: incidental feeling misread as feeling about the option. **Recommendation**: a short concepts page `concepts/miswanting` of 1,500–1,900 body words, plus a zero-word pointer swap on the forecasting page, which is 116 words over its hard limit.

## Terminology First: Whose "Wanting"

A Map page titled "miswanting" will collide with the corpus's established usage unless it fixes the mapping in its opening paragraph. Gilbert & Wilson define wanting as forecast: "Liking has to do with how a thing makes us feel, and wanting is, simply enough, a prediction of liking" **[page image, p. 178; also verbatim in the 2006 reprint's OpenAlex abstract]**. Miswanting is the mismatch: "When wanting and liking are uncoordinated in this way we may say that a person has *miswanted*" **[page image, p. 179]**. On the [shared terminology map](/topics/wanting-liking-and-the-value-in-mechanism-fork/#shared-terminology) that places:

- their **wanting** on the *predicted utility* row, the opposite of Berridge's incentive-salience "wanting". [affective-forecasting-gap](/concepts/affective-forecasting-gap/) L70 already quotes Berridge (2023) that desire "can decouple completely from memories and from learned predictions of outcome value".
- their **"gut reactions"** (one of three ingredients of a wanting, below) on the *present anticipatory affect* row, which is [P-VS1](/positions/value-in-selection/#p-vs1)'s currency.
- their **liking** on *experienced utility*, consciously felt. It is not Berridge's objective "liking".

So miswanting, in the Map's vocabulary, is a misranking of predicted utility, with or without its felt vehicle, against experienced utility. The page must say so before using the word.

## What Counts as a Demonstrated Mischoice

A single choice has no within-person counterfactual, so every design approximates one. The designs below are ranked by how directly they show that *this chooser's* choice was worse *by this chooser's own later experience*, and whether a *forecast* is shown to carry the error.

| Design | What it shows | Examples below |
|---|---|---|
| (i) Experience both options, then choose which to repeat | A choice of a dominated option. The error is in memory, since the choice follows experience | Kahneman et al. 1993 |
| (ii) Randomised manipulation of memory, real later choice | Memory causally moves choice | Redelmeier et al. 2003 |
| (iii) Choose for oneself, then experience; compare choosers of each option | A choice–experience inconsistency in the same people, but the comparison groups self-select | Hsee & Zhang 2004, Condition 1 |
| (iv) Choose for others; others randomly assigned to each option | A cleaner comparison, but the forecast is about other people | Hsee & Zhang 2004, Condition 2 vs 3–4 |
| (v) One sample states a preference; another is randomly assigned to experience the options | An inconsistency across samples | Gilbert & Ebert 2002; Kermer et al. 2006 |
| (vi) Manipulate the decision process; measure choice and later satisfaction | The manipulation changes both the choice and the experienced outcome | Wilson et al. 1993; Yang et al. 2011 |

Only Hsee & Zhang measured forecasts, choices and experiences together *and* showed the forecast predicting the choice. No study here manipulated a forecast while holding the other inputs fixed. That is the dissociation [affective-forecasting-gap](/concepts/affective-forecasting-gap/)'s battery says is needed, so the forecast's causal role is inferred everywhere.

## Key Sources

### Gilbert & Wilson (2000), "Miswanting: Some problems in the forecasting of future affective states"
- **Citation**: In J. P. Forgas (Ed.), *Feeling and Thinking: The Role of Affect in Social Cognition* (pp. 178–197). Cambridge University Press. Reprinted in S. Lichtenstein & P. Slovic (Eds.), *The Construction of Preference* (2006), pp. 550–564, https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9780511618031.031.
- **Verification**: the 2000 printing was read in full as a scan from Harvard DASH (http://nrs.harvard.edu/urn-3:HUL.InstRepos:14549983). Pages 178 and 197 bound the chapter, and the book's title page reads *Feeling and Thinking*. DASH's own citation transposes the title to "Thinking and feeling", and that transposition should not be copied. There is no DOI or Crossref record for the 2000 volume. Its title was confirmed at Open Library.
- **Key points**:
  - Wanting has three ingredients: "the particular details that we imagine when we consider a future event, our beliefs about the ways in which people like us are likely to react to such events, and the "gut reactions" we experience when we imagine the event" **[page image, p. 182]**. Each ingredient has its own failure: imagining the wrong event, using the wrong theory, and misinterpreting feelings. Focalism and immune neglect are treated as further sources of long-run miswanting.
  - **The felt-anticipation passage** bears on [P-VS1](/positions/value-in-selection/#p-vs1): "Our affective reactions to imaginary events are, in a sense, experiential previews of our affective reactions to the events themselves, and they figure prominently in our predictions of future liking" **[page image, p. 182, zoom-checked]**. And its failure: "The crux of the problem is that the feelings we experience when we imagine a future event are not necessarily or solely caused by that act of imagination" **[page image, p. 182, zoom-checked]**. Also: "Because we cannot always tell if the feelings we are having as we imagine an event are being caused solely by that imagining, we may use these feelings as proxies for future liking, and hence, miswant" **[page image, p. 183]**.
  - Neither channel dominates: "It seems, then, that feelings sometimes serve us better than theories, and theories sometimes serve us better than feelings" **[page image, p. 184]**.
  - On immune neglect and choice: "Immune neglect can cause us to miswant by causing us to fear and avoid outcomes that will not, in the long run, hinder our happiness" **[page image, p. 192]**. The chapter's evidence for avoidance-by-immune-neglect is Gilbert & Jenkins (1998, unpublished), the photography study later published as Gilbert & Ebert (2002).
- **Tenet alignment**: Neutral. The chapter is a functional account. Its "gut reactions" ingredient is the one place in this literature where the forecast's *felt* vehicle is named as an input, which is congenial to [P-VS1](/positions/value-in-selection/#p-vs1)'s framing and also names that framing's distinctive risk.

### Hsee & Zhang (2004), "Distinction bias: Misprediction and mischoice due to joint evaluation"
- **Citation**: *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 86(5), 680–695. https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.86.5.680 (Crossref-verified).
- **Verification**: full text read (`bear.warrington.ufl.edu` course copy of the APA PDF).
- **Mechanism**: choices and predictions are made in *joint evaluation* (JE), with the options side by side, while experience usually occurs in *single evaluation* (SE). Attribute differences that are only quantitative look larger in JE than they feel in SE. Differences that are qualitative, such as good versus bad, are predicted accurately. Abstract: the work "identifies a new source of failure to make accurate affective predictions or to make experientially optimal choices" **[verbatim]**.
- **Study 3 (Task–Reward)**: 243 students at a university in eastern China, in four between-subject conditions. Option A paired recalling a failure with a 15 g Dove chocolate. Option B paired recalling a success with a 5 g one, eaten only during the task.
  - Choice: 65% chose A, both choosing for themselves and choosing for unidentified others (χ²(1, N = 55) = 5.25 as reported).
  - Forecast: choosers for others predicted the larger chocolate would bring more happiness (2.67 vs 0.47, t(54) = 6.69). Randomly assigned experiencers found no difference (1.65 vs 1.94, t(63) < 1). The task effect was predicted accurately.
  - Experience: pure experiencers were happier with B (2.41 vs −0.71, t(63) = 7.51). Self-choosers were too (2.61 vs 0.09, t(50) = 5.61). "In the choice phase, most of them opted for the negative-task/large-reward option, but they ended up being less happy than the minority of people who chose the positive-task/small-reward option" **[verbatim]**. The authors report no standardised effect sizes. Approximate d ≈ 1.9 and ≈ 1.6 were computed here from t and estimated group sizes.
  - Forecast drives the choice: an extra condition (N = 33) asked only which option would make others happier. 66% predicted A, matching the choice data, so "the choice–experience inconsistency in this study is chiefly a result of JE/SE misprediction, not a result of inconsistency between choice and predicted experience" **[verbatim]**.
  - Knowledge and adaptation were ruled out: SE predictions made just before the task got the order right (pure experiencers −0.65 vs 2.52; self-choosers 0.24 vs 2.44).
- **Authors' own caveats**: the self-chooser comparison "is a less direct test of our theory but is nevertheless intriguing" **[verbatim]**, because self-choosers had seen both options and the groups self-selected. Footnote 6 addresses a strength-of-preference alternative explanation and argues against it. The second, audiovisual "strategic replication" (72 dB/ugly adult vs 78 dB/cute baby) is cited as "Hsee & Zhang (2003). Inconsistency between choice and consumption experience. Unpublished manuscript."
- **Hsee & Zhang's distinction**, worth carrying onto a Map page: "Substantive inconsistencies refer to findings that people fail to choose what is best for them; they show that decisions are suboptimal" **[verbatim]**. A bare JE/SE preference reversal is only an *internal* inconsistency. [preference-void](/voids/preference-void/) already holds the internal reversal (Hsee et al. 1999), and Study 3 is the substantive step it lacks.
- **Tenet alignment**: Neutral. JE is a property of any comparator, felt or unfelt.

### Anvari, Olsen, Hung & Feldman (2021), replication of Hsee & Zhang Studies 1–2
- **Citation**: *Journal of Experimental Social Psychology*, 92, 104052. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jesp.2020.104052 (Crossref-verified) **[abstract only]**.
- **Key points**: 824 MTurk participants. "In Study 1 we replicated the original findings" for the book-sales scenarios. "Study 2 failed to find support for the original findings", for the word-copying scenarios. Conclusion: "Taken together, the present paper provides mixed support for distinction bias" **[verbatim, abstract]**. The FORRT replication atlas lists no other replication. **Study 3, the mischoice study, has not been independently replicated** as far as these checks reach.

### Morewedge, Gilbert, Myrseth, Kassam & Wilson (2010), "Consuming experience: Why affective forecasters overestimate comparative value"
- **Citation**: *Journal of Experimental Social Psychology*, 46(6), 986–992. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jesp.2010.07.010 (Crossref-verified) **[abstract only, from the Harvard DASH record]**.
- **Key points**: five experiments. Forecasters overvalue comparison "because forecasters do not realize that comparison requires cognitive resources, and that experiences consume more cognitive resources than do forecasts" **[verbatim]**. This gives a mechanism for the JE/SE gap. It is a misprediction result with no choice measure.

### Yang, Hsee, Liu & Zhang (2011), "The supremacy of singular subjectivity"
- **Citation**: *Journal of Consumer Psychology*, 21(4), 393–404. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jcps.2011.05.006 (Crossref-verified) **[abstract only; samples not read]**.
- **Key points**: "consumers can make better decisions (i.e., choose what yields a better consumption experience) if objective specifications are removed and direct comparison is inhibited than if not" **[verbatim]**. This is the debiasing direction: an intervention changes choice toward the experientially better option. It fills the forecasting page's "Debias forecast (checked)" row, which every column predicts will change, so it does not discriminate between the models.

### Kahneman, Fredrickson, Schreiber & Redelmeier (1993), "When more pain is preferred to less: Adding a better end"
- **Citation**: *Psychological Science*, 4(6), 401–405. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.1993.tb00589.x (Crossref-verified).
- **Verification**: full text read (UZH-hosted JSTOR copy).
- **Design**: 32 subjects, within-subject. Short trial: 60 s at 14 °C. Long trial: the same 60 s, then 30 s more while the water warmed to about 15 °C. Subjects then chose which trial to repeat.
- **Result**: "most subjects (22 of 32, or 69%) preferred to repeat the long trial (z = 2.15, p < .05 by sign test)". "Note that this proportion would be zero if subjects acted to minimize their exposure to pain" **[verbatim]**. Among the 21 who reported a decrement of 2 or more points over the final 30 s, 17 (81%) chose the long trial. The 11 with little or no decrement split 6:5 for the short trial.
- **Bearing**: the plainest *demonstrated* mischoice in the set, with a real choice of an option that contains the alternative plus more pain. The error is remembered utility, the first arrow of the forecasting page's pipeline (experience → memory → forecast → selection), and the forecast arrow is not involved. "Dominated" assumes the temporal-integration standard the forecasting page already marks as Kahneman, Wakker & Sarin's *normative* theory. The only "replication" found was a student lab report with N = 12, which is not citable.

### Redelmeier, Katz & Kahneman (2003), "Memories of colonoscopy: a randomized trial"
- **Citation**: *Pain*, 104(1), 187–194. https://doi.org/10.1016/S0304-3959(03)00003-4 (Crossref-verified) **[abstract only]**.
- **Key points**: 682 consecutive outpatients, randomised. Half had a short extension at the end of the procedure, which made the final moments less painful (1.7 vs 2.5) and the remembered experience less unpleasant (4.4 vs 4.9, P = 0.006). Over a median 5.3 years of follow-up, return for a repeat colonoscopy averaged 50.4% and "were slightly higher (odds ratio=1.41, P=0.038) for those who underwent the longer procedure" **[verbatim]**.
- **Bearing**: the only *randomised* manipulation here that moves a real, consequential later choice. It moves that choice toward the option with more total discomfort, via memory. Clinically the shift is desirable, so "mischoice" applies only against the total-utility standard.

### Schurr, Rodensky & Erev (2014), "The effect of unpleasant experiences on evaluation and behavior"
- **Citation**: *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization*, 106, 1–9. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jebo.2014.05.012 (Crossref-verified) **[abstract read only through a summarising fetch of the Warwick repository record, so not quoted]**.
- **Key points (paraphrase)**: evaluations of unpleasant experiences overweight the rare peak, while repeated decisions from experience *underweight* it, in ongoing rather than planning decisions. **Counterweight**: the peak-end distortion behind the cold-pressor choice does not govern all experience-based choice.

### Wirtz, Kruger, Scollon & Diener (2003), "What to do on spring break?"
- **Citation**: *Psychological Science*, 14(5), 520–524. https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-9280.03455 (Crossref-verified) **[abstract only; N not read]**.
- **Key points**: "remembered experience, but neither on-line nor anticipated experience, directly predicted the desire to repeat the experience" **[verbatim]**. The measure is an intention to repeat, not a choice.

### Gilbert & Ebert (2002), "Decisions and revisions: The affective forecasting of changeable outcomes"
- **Citation**: *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 82(4), 503–514. https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.82.4.503 (Crossref-verified).
- **Verification**: full text read (Gilbert lab PDF; poor OCR in places, so only the abstract is quoted).
- **Study 1** (photography course): 55 participants were included. Experiencers who could exchange their chosen print came to like it less than those whose choice was final (Time × Condition, F(1, 22) = 5.68, p = .03, r = .45). Forecasters predicted no difference (all Fs(1, 27) < 1.41). On the 9th-day measure, after the exchange window had closed, the interaction was marginal (F(1, 23) = 4.05, p = .06).
- **Study 2b**: 89 Harvard undergraduates read the Study 2a protocol, a poster choice. 66.3% preferred to be assigned to the changeable version (Z = 3.07, p = .002), and 84.3% thought the average student would.
- Abstract: "The results demonstrate that errors in affective forecasting can lead people to behave in ways that do not optimize their happiness and well-being" **[verbatim]**.
- **Bearing**: the clearest *immune-neglect* route to choice. People do not foresee that irreversibility helps them come to like what they chose, so they choose reversibility. The design is between-sample, with stated preference in one sample and experience in another. The experiencer samples are small. No replication was found in one search.

### Kermer, Driver-Linn, Wilson & Gilbert (2006), "Loss aversion is an affective forecasting error"
- **Citation**: *Psychological Science*, 17(8), 649–653. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2006.01760.x (Crossref-verified).
- **Verification**: full text read (Gilbert lab PDF). This settles the 10-12 review's open question about whether either study measured a choice.
- **Choice measure, in a separate sample**: a pretest gave 21 participants $5 and the option of a 50–50 gamble to win another $5 or lose $0–$5. "Most participants (16 of 21, 76%) were unwilling to risk losing $3". The mean amount risked was $1.86 against $3 (t(20) = 3.59, d = 1.61) **[verbatim for the quoted phrase]**.
- **Main Study 2** (N = 51, within-participants, no real choice): participants forecast that the $3 loss would affect them more than the $5 gain (ds > 0.61). Those who lost felt happier than they had predicted (d = 1.18), and winners felt as happy as predicted. Footnote 3: the last 27 were told they could decline the gamble, but under dissonance-style encouragement "No participant declined" **[verbatim]**, so this sample's choice data carry no information.
- **Authors' conclusions**: "there was no evidence that losing actually had a greater emotional impact than winning" **[verbatim]**. They also concede a design feature: "By endowing people with $5, we did provide them with an easy way to rationalize a loss" **[verbatim]**. Their closing claim is that this "may lead people to make decisions that maximize neither their wealth nor their happiness" **[verbatim]**.
- **Bearing**: Kermer et al. are a between-sample choice–experience inconsistency, design (v), and not the "inferred" reversal the 10-12 review describes. They are also not a same-person demonstrated reversal. Their 76% refusal and their losers' less-than-predicted distress come from different people.

### Charpentier, De Neve, Li, Roiser & Sharot (2016), "Models of affective decision making: How do feelings predict choice?"
- **Citation**: *Psychological Science*, 27(6), 763–775. https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797616634654 (Crossref-verified).
- **Verification**: full text read (Europe PMC XML, PMC4904352, author manuscript). Its two replication-and-extension studies are in the Supplemental Material, which was not read.
- **Design**: 59 recruited and 56 analysed. A feelings task collected expected feelings before outcomes and experienced feelings after them, in separate blocks, across gain and loss amounts. A separate gambling task of 288 to 322 choices, each between a 50–50 gamble and a sure option, covered mixed, gain-only and loss-only gambles at the same amounts. Feeling functions fitted to the ratings were then used to predict the choices.
- **Results**:
  - Feeling-based choice models "predicted choice better than all value-based comparison models".
  - Feelings themselves showed no gain/loss asymmetry: "the absolute impact of losses and gains on ratings of feelings relative to a zero outcome revealed no difference, F (1, 55) = 0.01" **[verbatim]**.
  - An impact bias grew with the stake: curvature was smaller for experienced than expected feelings (t(55) = 3.31, d = 0.442). It is not reported as sign-specific.
  - At choice, feelings about losses carried more weight than feelings about gains, both for expected feelings (d = 0.406) and experienced feelings (d = 0.392). But "only when potential losses and gains are evaluated simultaneously (i.e., in the same choice) are feelings about losses weighted more strongly than feelings about gains" **[verbatim]**.
  - Abstract: "loss aversion in choice was explained by an asymmetry in how feelings about losses and gains were weighted when making a decision, not by an asymmetry in the feelings themselves" **[verbatim]**.
- **Bearing**: the most direct evidence in this set that reported feelings predict choice beyond monetary value, which is congenial to a feelings-as-input reading. It places the loss tilt on the [weighting page](/concepts/negative-valence-asymmetry-and-the-selection-weighting-function/)'s *selection-policy* row, not on the predicted-utility row that Kermer et al. support. It also makes the tilt joint-evaluation-specific, the same signature as distinction bias and Yechiam & Hochman's format dependence. Linking these is this note's inference. **The Map cites Charpentier et al. nowhere**: 0 hits across `obsidian/`.

### Wilson, Lisle, Schooler, Hodges, Klaaren & LaFleur (1993), "Introspecting about reasons can reduce post-choice satisfaction"
- **Citation**: *Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin*, 19(3), 331–339. https://doi.org/10.1177/0146167293193010 (Crossref-verified) **[abstract only]**.
- **Key points**: "Those instructed to think about their reasons chose a different type of poster than control subjects and, when contacted 3 weeks later, were less satisfied with their choice" **[verbatim]**. The percentages that circulate (about 95% vs 64% choosing an art poster) come from secondary summaries only and should not be cited. Gilbert & Wilson (2000, pp. 183–184) read this as cognitive reactions overruling better affective ones.

### Gilbert, Gill & Wilson (2002), "The future is now: Temporal correction in affective forecasting"
- **Citation**: *Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes*, 88(1), 430–444. https://doi.org/10.1006/obhd.2001.2982 (Crossref-verified).
- **Verification**: full text read (Gilbert lab PDF).
- **Field study**: 135 grocery shoppers were recruited and 111 analysed. They were made sated (a muffin, about 100 g) or left hungry, and given their own shopping list or not. Shoppers without a list bought a larger share of unplanned food items when hungry (51%) than when sated (34%), F(1, 109) = 5.8, p < .02. Shoppers with a list did not differ (36% vs 28%, F(1, 109) = 1.2). All shoppers in a session shared a condition.
- **Bearing**: the nearest field approximation to the forecasting page's battery row "shift present affect; forecast held fixed". The list fixes the plan, and hunger shifts the "proxy reactions". It does not isolate felt affect: hunger also moves incentive salience and learned value, and later experience was not measured, so it shows a changed choice without showing a mischoice.

### Nordgren, van Harreveld & van der Pligt (2009), "The restraint bias"
- **Citation**: *Psychological Science*, 20(12), 1523–1528. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2009.02468.x (Crossref-verified) **[abstract only]**.
- **Key points**: "Recovering smokers with more inflated impulse-control beliefs exposed themselves to more temptation, which led to higher rates of relapse 4 months later" **[verbatim]**. The forecast error concerns self-control rather than affect, and the design is correlational. It is relevant to [cross-state-void](/voids/cross-state-void/), which already cites the Nordgren programme.

### Benjamin, Heffetz, Kimball & Rees-Jones (2012), "What do you think would make you happier? What do you think you would choose?"
- **Citation**: *American Economic Review*, 102(5), 2083–2110. https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.102.5.2083 (Crossref-verified).
- **Verification**: the AER abstract was read. The 83% figure is verbatim from the earlier NBER Working Paper 16489 (October 2010), titled "Do People Seek to Maximize Happiness? Evidence from New Surveys". The published text was not checked for the same figure.
- **Key points**: "On average, predicted SWB and choice coincide in our data 83 percent of the time", ranging "from well below 50 percent to above 95 percent" **[verbatim, WP]**. AER abstract: "While choice and predicted SWB rankings usually coincide in our data, we find systematic reversals". Choice is better explained by also adding "predicted sense of purpose, control over one's life, family happiness, and social status" **[verbatim]**. The choices were hypothetical.
- **Bearing**: choice mostly tracks the hedonic forecast but departs from it systematically, toward non-hedonic evaluative considerations. That direction is what [P-VS4](/positions/value-in-selection/#p-vs4) pluralism would predict of a plural evaluative currency. It also bears on [P-VS1](/positions/value-in-selection/#p-vs1)'s micro-valence shift condition.

### Hsee, Zhang, Yu & Xi (2003), "Lay rationalism and inconsistency between predicted experience and decision"
- **Citation**: *Journal of Behavioral Decision Making*, 16(4), 257–272. https://doi.org/10.1002/bdm.445 (Crossref-verified) **[abstract only]**.
- **Key points**: "people are more likely to favor a rationalistically-superior option when they make a decision than when they predict experience" **[verbatim; the source's U+2010 hyphen normalised]**. Here choice departs from the forecast, the second kind of failure. The often-cited chocolate study (Hsee 1999, *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review* 6(4), 555–561, https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03212963, Crossref-verified) was **not reached**: Springer served HTML and SSRN was blocked.

### Hsee & Hastie (2006), "Decision and experience: Why don't we choose what makes us happy?"
- **Citation**: *Trends in Cognitive Sciences*, 10(1), 31–37. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2005.11.007 (Crossref-verified; PMID 16318925) **[abstract only; the SSRN full text was blocked]**.
- **Key points**: reviews findings "that show people systematically fail to predict or choose what maximizes their happiness" **[verbatim]**. Secondary summaries report a two-part taxonomy of prediction biases and failures to follow predictions. That is plausible but unverified here.

### Mellers, Schwartz & Ritov (1999), "Emotion-based choice"; Mellers & McGraw (2001)
- **Citations**: *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General*, 128(3), 332–345, https://doi.org/10.1037/0096-3445.128.3.332; *Current Directions in Psychological Science*, 10(6), 210–214, https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-8721.00151 (both Crossref-verified) **[abstract only]**.
- **Key points**: subjective expected pleasure theory, in which "People are assumed to choose the option with greater subjective expected pleasure" **[verbatim]**. It is the formal precedent for a choice rule over anticipated feeling, and for [P-VS1](/positions/value-in-selection/#p-vs1), the closest published model of its selection law at the behavioural grain.

### Sharot, De Martino & Dolan (2009), "How choice reveals and shapes expected hedonic outcome"
- **Citation**: *Journal of Neuroscience*, 29(12), 3760–3765. https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.4972-08.2009 (Crossref-verified) **[abstract only]**.
- **Key points**: choosing between similarly valued options raised the expected pleasure of the chosen one and lowered the rejected one, tracked in the caudate. "This finding suggests that the physiological representation of a stimulus' expected hedonic value is altered by a commitment to it" **[verbatim]**. **Caveat**: the rate–choose–rate paradigm carries the artifact identified by Chen & Risen (2010, *JPSP* 99(4), 573–594, https://doi.org/10.1037/a0020217, Crossref-verified; not read beyond metadata). **Bearing**: if it survives, the felt forecast at choice is partly *produced* by the choosing, so it is not a pure input.

### Lerner, Small & Loewenstein (2004), "Heart strings and purse strings" (lead)
- **Citation**: *Psychological Science*, 15(5), 337–341. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.0956-7976.2004.00679.x (Crossref-verified) **[abstract only; replication status not established after one search]**.
- **Key points**: incidental disgust eliminated the endowment effect, and incidental sadness reversed it. The authors conclude that "emotions of the same valence can have opposing effects on such decisions" **[verbatim]**.
- **Bearing**: it operationalises the "shift present affect" row with incidental emotion, but the choice moves with the *appraisal*, not the valence. If it holds, a battery row framed as "shift valence" is mis-specified. That fits [P-VS1](/positions/value-in-selection/#p-vs1)'s micro-valence shift condition (Shuman, Sander & Scherer 2013).

## Major Positions

### Miswanting / affective-forecasting programme
- **Proponents**: Gilbert, Wilson, Ebert, Kermer, Morewedge.
- **Core claim**: wanting forecasts liking. The forecast fails through imagined detail, lay theory and misread feeling, and over time through focalism and immune neglect, and these failures cause suboptimal choice.
- **Relation to tenets**: neutral on the horn. It names a felt ingredient ("gut reactions") without claiming that it selects.

### Evaluability / joint-versus-separate evaluation
- **Proponents**: Hsee, Zhang, Yang, Hastie.
- **Core claim**: choosing is comparing, and experiencing usually is not, so choosers overweight distinctions that experience does not register. Hsee & Zhang (2010, *Perspectives on Psychological Science* 5(4), 343–355, https://doi.org/10.1177/1745691610374586, abstract read) generalise this as "general evaluability theory", claimed to unify findings "ranging from duration neglect to affective forecasting errors".
- **Relation to tenets**: mechanism-neutral. Any selector that compares options is in JE.

### Experienced utility and memory
- **Proponents**: Kahneman, Redelmeier, Fredrickson; Wirtz et al.
- **Core claim**: choice consults remembered utility, which is peak-end-weighted and duration-blind, so people can choose dominated experiences.
- **Relation to tenets**: neutral. This is the training record a felt or an unfelt forecaster would share.

### Feelings as choice inputs with a separate weighting stage
- **Proponents**: Mellers; Charpentier and Sharot.
- **Core claim**: anticipated feelings predict choice, beyond value in Charpentier et al., but the decision stage weights them asymmetrically and context-dependently.
- **Relation to tenets**: the closest behavioural analogue of [P-VS1](/positions/value-in-selection/#p-vs1)'s currency, with the important split that what is *felt* is symmetric while the *policy* adds the tilt.

## Key Debates

### What standard makes a choice a "mischoice"?
- **Sides**: total-utility integration (Kahneman), immediate SE happiness ratings (Hsee & Zhang), satisfaction weeks later (Wilson et al.), and revealed preference, on which choice defines welfare and "mischoice" is ill-formed. The revealed-preference side was not researched in this pass.
- **Core disagreement**: each demonstration is a reversal *relative to a chosen standard*. That is the forecasting page's "which self's valence" question ([transformative-experience-void](/voids/transformative-experience-void/)) arising at small scale. The colonoscopy case shows that the standards can disagree about whether the reversal is bad.
- **Current state**: open. Any Map page should name the standard for each case.

### Where does loss aversion's tilt sit: forecast or weighting?
- **Sides**: Kermer et al. (2006) put it in the forecast: predicted losses outweigh predicted gains, and experience is roughly symmetric. Charpentier et al. (2016) put it in the weighting at choice: expected and experienced feelings are both symmetric, and losses are weighted more only in mixed gambles.
- **Core disagreement**: the two designs differ. Kermer et al. used a single $5-endowed coin flip, with a framing the authors concede eased rationalising a loss. Charpentier et al. used many small-stakes probabilistic trials with block-separated feeling ratings.
- **Current state**: unresolved, and the [weighting page](/concepts/negative-valence-asymmetry-and-the-selection-weighting-function/) currently cites only Kermer et al. and McGraw et al. on this row.

### Do feelings drive choice?
- **For**: Charpentier et al. (feeling models beat value models); Benjamin et al. (83% coincidence); Mellers et al. (subjective expected pleasure).
- **Against or qualifying**: lay rationalism (choice is "colder" than forecast); Benjamin et al.'s systematic non-hedonic departures; Harris (2012) self-estimated dread uncorrelated with timing choices (in the sibling note [anticipatory-feeling-and-time-preference-2026-10-05](/research/anticipatory-feeling-and-time-preference-2026-10-05/)); Sharot et al. (the forecast is partly produced by the choice).
- **Current state**: ratings predict choice well. No study here measures the *felt* state at the moment of choosing.

### On [P-VS1](/positions/value-in-selection/#p-vs1) (input: the felt anticipation at the moment of choice)
1. **The cost is now demonstrated rather than hypothetical.** A selector whose input is a fallible forecast would choose the dominated cold-pressor trial and the large-chocolate option, as people did.
2. **The demonstrations do not discriminate between the horns.** Comparison mode is a property of any comparator. Peak-end memory is a property of the record a learned-value chooser and a felt forecaster share. Immune neglect is a property of the forecast's content. The value-blind rival predicts every reversal in the table, so the reversals enter [P-VS2](/positions/value-in-selection/#p-vs2)'s battery as non-discriminating results. They sit with the "Debias forecast (checked)" row, which Yang et al. (2011) fill.
3. **No study here measured [P-VS1](/positions/value-in-selection/#p-vs1)'s actual input.** Hsee & Zhang's predictions were rating-scale *anticipated* emotion, a cool judgement, and not *anticipatory* emotion felt at choice. Charpentier et al.'s expected feelings were rated in a separate task. The forecasting page's own warning applies: a cool judgement with flat present affect "gives this reading no felt input". Whether the felt vehicle carried the same misranking is unmeasured. That is a real gap in the literature, and it should be stated as such.
4. **A failure specific to a felt currency.** Gilbert & Wilson's misattribution, in which feelings at imagination are "not necessarily or solely caused by that act of imagination", is the risk a *felt* selector carries. At the behavioural grain the rival has an analogue in state-dependent incentive salience (Gilbert, Gill & Wilson's hunger), so this is a description of [P-VS1](/positions/value-in-selection/#p-vs1)'s cost rather than evidence against it. Incidental-emotion paradigms such as Lerner et al. are the existing operationalisation of the battery's "shift present affect" row. If Lerner et al.'s appraisal-specific result holds, the row should be framed by appraisal, not valence, which feeds [P-VS1](/positions/value-in-selection/#p-vs1)'s micro-valence shift condition.
5. **Choice is not denominated in hedonic forecast alone.** Benjamin et al.'s 83% coincidence, with departures toward purpose, control and status, is evidence about the currency's *dimensionality*. It is congenial to [P-VS4](/positions/value-in-selection/#p-vs4) pluralism and is not a datum for or against value-sensitivity.
6. **No band change is warranted.** [P-VS1](/positions/value-in-selection/#p-vs1) stays held on prior commitments, and nothing here adjudicates the horn. A calibration-history note would be optional. The positions register cannot take edits length-neutrally, so any note needs a dated "Updated" entry.

## Gaps in Research

- **No study found that measures present anticipatory affect at the moment of choice together with later experience.** This is the measurement [P-VS1](/positions/value-in-selection/#p-vs1) needs, and it was not specifically searched beyond the queries above, so absence here is not proof of absence.
- Hsee & Zhang's Study 3 has no independent replication. Its own replication is an unpublished 2003 manuscript.
- Gilbert & Ebert: small experiencer samples and a marginal 9th-day effect. One search found no replication, and the Study 2a figures were not extracted.
- Kahneman et al. (1993): no peer-reviewed direct replication was located. Schurr et al. (2014) was read only through a summarising fetch.
- Wilson et al. (1993), Yang et al. (2011), Wirtz et al. (2003) and Nordgren et al. (2009): samples and effect sizes not read, abstracts only.
- Hsee (1999), the chocolate study, was not reached. Hsee & Hastie (2006) was abstract only.
- Charpentier et al.'s two supplemental replication-and-extension studies were not read.
- Lerner et al. (2004): replication status unknown.
- The revealed-preference and behavioural-welfare objection to the concept of "mischoice" was not researched.
