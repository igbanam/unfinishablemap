## Executive Summary

The Map leans on dissociation arguments across a cluster (23 live files use "double dissociation"), and its hub page reads the dissociation pattern as revealing "which components are separable—and therefore which are architecturally distinct". No Map page examines that step. Cognitive neuropsychology has debated it for forty years, and the result is a graded verdict rather than a refutation. **Selective deficits establish differential vulnerability robustly. They support functional differentiation only as inference to the best explanation relative to a model class. They identify discrete components (modules, channels) only given bridge assumptions (Caramazza's fractionation, modularity, transparency and universality; Farah's "locality") that are contested, and that are weakest exactly where the Map uses them: for consciousness, report and awareness.** The classical methodologist himself, Shallice, wrote that "The presence of dissociations in no way entails the existence of isolable subsystems", and conceded that in his book's chapters on consciousness and central systems "the methodological scruples of the earlier sections are abandoned". Connectionist demonstrations (Plaut 1995), single-system and backup-strategy counterexamples (Chater 2003; Dunn & Kirsner 1988), diaschisis and lesion-network mapping (Carrera & Tononi 2014; Boes et al. 2015), degeneracy (Price & Friston 2002) and Bayesian lesion models (Parr, Rees & Friston 2018; Fotopoulou 2014) each attack a different bridge assumption. The defenders (Coltheart 2017; Bullinaria & Chater 1995; Machery 2012; Davies 2010) hold that the inference survives, but as abduction. **Verdict: `article`** at `topics/logic-of-dissociation-inference` (topics 349/370 by `count_section_files` today). The host pages cannot absorb this material: the hub is at 3,972 of 4,000 body words and `clinical-dissociation-as-systematic-evidence` is already over the hard threshold. The step is upstream of every interface-versus-production question the cluster asks, and the Map can bring something of its own to it: the modular reading is, in Shallice's words, "a simple explanation of the findings", so the Map's own Occam's-limits tenet tells against taking it at face value.

## Key Sources

### Plaut (1995), "Double dissociation without modularity: Evidence from connectionist neuropsychology"
- **URL**: https://ni.cmu.edu/~plaut/papers/pdf/Plaut95JCEN.modularity.pdf (doi 10.1080/01688639508405124)
- **Type**: Paper, *Journal of Clinical and Experimental Neuropsychology* 17(2):291–321
- **Key points**:
  - Damage to a connectionist network that reads via meaning and "yet has no separable components" (Plaut & Shallice 1993) yields a concrete/abstract word-reading double dissociation.
  - The abstract: "The functional specialization in the network that gives rise to the double dissociation is not transparently related to the network’s structure, as modular theories assume."
  - Quantitatively equivalent lesions (same site, same severity) produce qualitatively different outcomes, which "raises specific concerns about the interpretation of single-case studies".
  - The paper does not say dissociations are uninformative: "the occurrence of the double dissociation does “throw useful light” on the functional organization of the network", given specific assumptions about the computational principles governing it. The conclusion, verbatim: "cognitive neuropsychologists cannot assume that the cognitive system must be modular in order to account for observed dissociations among brain-damaged patients."
  - Plaut explicitly frames this as a violation of "Caramazza’s (1986) “transparency” assumption".
  - Quotes Shallice 1988 (p. 256) on the two conditions a valid distributed-memory double dissociation must meet, and (p. 258) that a dissociation pattern "might throw no useful light on what function is responsible for the specialisation" though "determining the degree of specialisation within a system is a useful guide to system architecture".
- **Tenet alignment**: Neutral on dualism. It bears on the Map's *use* of dissociations; it does not touch the tenets themselves.
- **Quote**: "cognitive neuropsychologists cannot assume that the cognitive system must be modular in order to account for observed dissociations among brain-damaged patients."

### Shallice (1991), "Précis of *From neuropsychology to mental structure*"
- **URL**: https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/123356/1/download1.pdf (doi 10.1017/S0140525X0007059X)
- **Type**: Book précis, *Behavioral and Brain Sciences* 14(3):429–438. It summarises Shallice (1988), the field's standard methodology text.
- **Key points**:
  - Shallice defends the method. He lists artefacts (resource differences among tasks, premorbid individual differences, reorganisation of function) and holds that "strong" and "classical" dissociations minimise them.
  - On reorganisation: "Classical dissociations are less prone to this problem as there is no reason a reorganised system should produce the same quantitative level of performance as the original one."
  - A *strong* dissociation is one where performance on the better task "is still below the normal range even though the contrast between the better and worse performed tasks is striking".
  - The classical solution to difficulty artefacts "was to seek a complementary dissociation and thereby demonstrate a double dissociation (e.g., Teuber 1955)".
  - The key concession, verbatim: "Dissociations have often been treated as evidence of isolable subsystems (e.g., Fodor 1983; Shallice 1979), a simple explanation of the findings. The presence of dissociations in no way entails the existence of isolable subsystems ... Dissociations can arise from damage to other types of architecture." The book's ch. 11 considers "continuous processing spaces, overlapping processing regions and coupled systems". Dissociations "can also occur from impairments to different levels or modes of operation of the same subsystem".
  - On transparency: theory-generation works "only if the relation between damage to a subcomponent and impaired task performance is fairly transparent; Gregory (1961) has pointed out that this is often not the case in machines."
  - On subtraction: "certain observed impairments cannot be understood merely by subtracting one or more components from the normal system". Letter-by-letter reading is "clearly a compensatory procedure".
  - **On consciousness specifically**: chapters 12–16 cover knowledge, visual attention, supervisory operations, memory and consciousness. Of them he says, "On the whole, the methodological scruples of the earlier sections are abandoned, and a dissociation is assumed to represent the operation of a damaged subsystem." His ch. 16 then gives "A functionalist account" said to explain "the counterintuitive neuropsychological findings related to consciousness that have been described such as blindsight, aspects of prosopagnosia, and the split-brain syndrome."
- **Tenet alignment**: Shallice's own conclusion is functionalist and modular. Do not recruit him as an anti-modularist. The point the Map can use is narrower: the field's leading methodologist marks the consciousness domain as the one where the method runs without its safeguards.
- **Quote**: "The presence of dissociations in no way entails the existence of isolable subsystems"

### Caramazza (1986); Coltheart (2017); Shallice (2015): the bridge assumptions
- **URLs**: doi 10.1016/0278-2626(86)90061-8; doi 10.1080/02643294.2017.1324950; doi 10.1080/02643294.2015.1131677
- **Type**: Papers (abstracts read)
- **Key points**:
  - Caramazza: "given certain assumptions, only the single-case method allows valid inferences about the structure of cognitive systems from the analysis of impaired performance". Group studies average over different lesions.
  - Coltheart 2017 names Caramazza's four assumptions as "fractionation, modularity, transparency and universality" and concludes "all four remain defensible today".
  - Shallice 2015 treats the same four as "bridge principles" and discusses one potential pitfall per axiom for single-case methods. He argues that single-case and case-series/group methods should cross-check each other.
  - "Subtractivity" (impaired performance = normal system minus damaged parts) appears in the literature as a further named assumption, usually credited to Saffran (1982) and listed by Coltheart (2001). **Neither source was seen**; use Shallice 1991's "subtraction approach" wording, or mark the label as standard but unverified here.
- **Tenet alignment**: Neutral.
- **Quote** (Coltheart 2017 abstract): "all four remain defensible today"

### Farah (1994), "Neuropsychological inference with an interactive brain: A critique of the 'locality' assumption"
- **URL**: doi 10.1017/S0140525X00033306
- **Type**: BBS target article, 17(1):43–61 (abstract read)
- **Key points**: Cognitive neuropsychologists "generally assume that the effects of brain damage are local, that is, that the nondamaged components of the architecture continue to function as they did before the damage". This "locality" assumption "is probably not correct in general". Inferences can instead proceed on assumptions that processing "is graded, distributed, and interactive". Three worked dissociations: living-things knowledge, disengagement of visual attention, and covert face recognition.
- **Tenet alignment**: Directly relevant to the interface reading. The filter image of "channel broken, broadcast intact" is a locality claim made across the interface.
- **Quote**: "the nondamaged components of the architecture continue to function as they did before the damage"

### Gregory: the radio counterexample
- **URL**: https://www.webofstories.com/play/17294 (Richard Gregory, story 42, "Gadgets and understanding the brain", recorded June 2006)
- **Type**: Interview transcript
- **Key points**: The canonical critique of lesion-to-function inference is itself a radio analogy. Gregory: "If you’ve got a radio and you take part of it out, remove it, the radio might howl, it might go whooo like this but it doesn’t mean that that part in the radio was a suppressor for howling." Removing a part leaves "a modified total system, simply a different system but it’s working with all those other parts without that one and each of the remaining parts work differently because they’re in a different internal environment." He says he raised this in his "first paper"; Shallice 1991 cites "Gregory (1961)" for the point.
- **Tenet alignment**: Neutral. The use for the Map is that its hub page argues with a radio analogy (L60) that presupposes exactly the transparency Gregory's radio denies.
- **Quote**: as above. **Quote only the 2006 interview.** The 1961 chapter ("The brain as an engineering problem", in Thorpe & Zangwill (eds.), *Current Problems in Animal Behaviour*) was not seen, and its bibliographic details here come from memory, not a publisher.

### Chater (2003), "How much can we learn from double dissociations?"
- **URL**: https://www.dectech.co.uk/publications/LinksNick/FoundationsTheoryAndMethodology/how%20much%20can%20we%20learn%20from%20double%20dissociations.pdf (doi 10.1016/S0010-9452(08)70093-5)
- **Type**: Discussion piece, *Cortex* 39(1):167–169 (the Cortex double-dissociation forum)
- **Key points**: Three single-system constructions:
  - two tasks sharing nearly all machinery except "one infinitesimally small component" each (the prawn and peanut allergy case);
  - shared components used to different degrees (javelin and shot-put blisters);
  - identical components but different backup strategies (blackboard writing versus fruit-picking after hand or leg injury). This shows that "the double dissociation may arise purely from the differences between backup strategies".
  - He extends the point to fMRI "hot spots".
  - His conclusion is not nihilist: "even a single dissociation may be a powerful constraint – if whole classes of cognitive model cannot reproduce that single dissociation."
- **Tenet alignment**: Neutral. It supplies the model-class framing the Map needs: dissociations rule out model classes, and do not read off parts.
- **Quote**: "At this high level of generality, it seems then that double dissociation cannot be used as an inference principle for uncovering the modularity of the cognitive system."

### Bullinaria & Chater (1995), "Connectionist modelling: Implications for cognitive neuropsychology"
- **URL**: doi 10.1080/01690969508407095
- **Type**: Paper, *Language and Cognitive Processes* 10(3–4):227–264 (abstract read)
- **Key points**: **The counterweight to Plaut.** Small networks show double dissociation without modularity. In "sufficiently larger and more distributed networks ... double dissociations are not observed", and such double dissociations "are merely artefacts of scale". In large distributed systems, damage typically yields only a single dissociation. Hence "connectionism appears to create no additional problems for the traditional neuropsychological inference."
- **Tenet alignment**: Neutral. On their reading, a robust double dissociation in a large system *is* evidence of some separation. The new article must present this side.
- **Quote**: "such double dissociations are merely artefacts of scale"

### Van Orden, Pennington & Stone (2001), "What do double dissociations prove?"
- **URL**: doi 10.1207/s15516709cog2501_5 (Wiley full text blocked: 403/Cloudflare)
- **Type**: Paper, *Cognitive Science* 25(1):111–172. **Abstract only, via OpenAlex, possibly truncated.**
- **Key points**: They read the practice as presupposing what it claims to show: "the practice of revealing dissociations is predicated on modularity being true (T. Shallice, 1988)". Using reading modules as the case, they argue that modularity "fails to converge on a fixed set of exclusionary criteria that define pure cases". Competing modular theories therefore force "perennial quests for purer cases" and "unending fractionation into more modules".
- **Tenet alignment**: Relevant to Occam's limits: a simplicity-driven decomposition that never converges.
- **Quote**: the predication sentence above. It is **their characterisation of Shallice**, which Shallice 1991's "in no way entails" passage arguably resists. Present it as Van Orden et al.'s charge.

### Dunn & Kirsner (1988), "Discovering functionally independent mental processes: The principle of reversed association"
- **URL**: doi 10.1037/0033-295x.95.1.91
- **Type**: Paper, *Psychological Review* 95(1):91–101 (abstract read)
- **Key points**: The dissociation logic "is flawed in two ways". First, "it is possible to posit single processes that mimic both single and double dissociation". Second, it rests on "an assumption of selective influence that most, if not all, psychologists would now reject as false". They propose *reversed association* (any non-monotonic relation between two tasks) as a sounder test of the number of independent processes. This is the ancestor of state-trace analysis. Their 2003 *Cortex* paper "What can we infer from double dissociations?" (39(1):1–7) opened the Cortex forum; **not seen**.
- **Tenet alignment**: Neutral.
- **Quote**: "it is possible to posit single processes that mimic both single and double dissociation"

### Davies (2010), "Double dissociation: Understanding its role in cognitive neuropsychology"
- **URL**: doi 10.1111/j.1468-0017.2010.01399.x
- **Type**: Paper, *Mind & Language* 25(5):500–540 (abstract read). This is the philosophical treatment.
- **Key points**:
  - "arguments from double dissociation to separate modules work by inference to the best, not the only possible, explanation".
  - Connectionism broadened "the range of potential explanations of double dissociation". That makes the competition between explanations visible.
  - Lesion-location findings "may enhance the value of double dissociation in shifting the balance of support between cognitive theories".
- **Tenet alignment**: The IBE framing pulls Occam's-limits into play. Which explanation counts as "best" depends on simplicity and prior-plausibility weightings.
- **Quote**: "arguments from double dissociation to separate modules work by inference to the best, not the only possible, explanation"

### Machery (2012), "Dissociations in neuropsychology and cognitive neuroscience"
- **URL**: doi 10.1086/668002
- **Type**: Paper, *Philosophy of Science* 79(4):490–518 (abstract read)
- **Key points**: He compares dissociation inferences with cognitive neuroscience's function-to-structure inferences and argues "the former have a poorer epistemic standing than the latter". **Machery is a relative defender of dissociations**: imaging inferences fare worse.
- **Tenet alignment**: Neutral.

### Price & Friston (2002), "Degeneracy and cognitive anatomy"
- **URL**: doi 10.1016/S1364-6613(02)01976-9 (PubMed 12413574)
- **Type**: Paper, *Trends in Cognitive Sciences* 6(10):416–421 (abstract read)
- **Key points**: Multiple sufficient systems for one task ("degeneracy") cannot be detected by lesion studies "because their performance might not be impaired when only one degenerate system is damaged". **Cuts the other way**: the *absence* of a dissociation does not show the absence of components.
- **Quote**: "Nor can it be detected by neuropsychological studies of patients because their performance might not be impaired when only one degenerate system is damaged."

### Carrera & Tononi (2014), "Diaschisis: past, present, future"; Boes et al. (2015), "Network localization of neurological symptoms from focal brain lesions"
- **URLs**: doi 10.1093/brain/awu101; doi 10.1093/brain/awv228 (PubMed 26264514)
- **Type**: Review; empirical paper (abstracts read)
- **Key points**:
  - "Diaschisis" was "coined by von Monakow in 1914" for changes "distant to a focal brain lesion". Its connectional form now "seems to relate more consistently to the clinical findings", especially in motor and attentional networks after stroke.
  - Boes et al. found that heterogeneous lesions with little overlap that produce the same symptom share connectivity: "heterogeneous lesions producing similar symptoms share functional connectivity to specific brain regions involved in symptom expression". Syndromes tested: peduncular hallucinosis, central post-stroke pain, auditory hallucinosis and subcortical aphasia.
- **Tenet alignment**: Neutral. These are the anatomical face of non-locality: symptom ≠ site.
- **Quote** (Boes): as above.

### Parr, Rees & Friston (2018), "Computational neuropsychology and Bayesian inference"
- **URL**: https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fnhum.2018.00061/full (doi 10.3389/fnhum.2018.00061)
- **Type**: Narrative review, *Frontiers in Human Neuroscience* 12:61
- **Key points**:
  - Opens with the lesion-deficit problems: statistical dependencies between lesions from vascular anatomy, distributed networks, and remote effects. "Damage to one part of the brain may give rise to abnormal cognition indirectly – through its influence over a distant region (Price et al., 2001; Carrera and Tononi, 2014)."
  - Recasts syndromes: "neuropsychological deficits can be thought of as false inferences that arise due to aberrant prior beliefs (that are poor fits to the real world)". This is "Bayes optimal pathology", in which "any given neuropsychological disorder could be characterized by the set of prior beliefs that would make a patient’s behavior appear Bayes optimal".
  - Builds a "taxonomy of pathological priors", related "to failures of neuromodulation and to the notion of a ‘disconnection’ syndrome".
- **Tenet alignment**: Physicalist framework. **In scope only as a lesion model**: it replaces "component removed" with "parameter (prior or precision) altered in one hierarchical system", a non-subtractive model of what a lesion does.

## Major Positions

### Classical modular inference (Caramazza 1986; Shallice 1988; Coltheart 2017)
- **Core claim**: Given fractionation, modularity, transparency and universality, the pattern of selective impairment in single cases reveals the normal architecture. Double dissociations (Teuber 1955) control for difficulty artefacts. Classical and strong dissociations, plus convergence with normal-subject experiments, control the rest.
- **Relation to site tenets**: The Map's dissociation cluster tacitly adopts this position and then adds one more transparency step: from functional component to *interface channel*. The position's own defenders treat the step from dissociation to module as defeasible. Shallice suspends his scruples for consciousness.

### Non-modular and graded critique (Plaut 1995, 2003; Farah 1994; Van Orden et al. 2001; Dunn & Kirsner 1988; Chater 2003)
- **Core claim**: Double dissociations arise in systems without separable components: distributed networks, single processes with non-linear performance functions, shared machinery with tiny specialisations, and differential backup strategies. Lesion effects are non-local. Dissociations constrain model classes; they do not read off parts.
- **Relation to site tenets**: This undercuts both the Map's channel vocabulary *and* the functionalist modular reading it contrasts with. Neither metaphysics is favoured. The Map loses the claim that the architecture is "earned robustly", and gains a cleaner statement of what the clinical evidence can carry.

### Abductive middle (Davies 2010; Machery 2012; Bullinaria & Chater 1995)
- **Core claim**: The inference is inference to the best explanation. It is strengthened by lesion location, large-system modelling and convergence, and it remains better grounded than imaging's function-to-structure inferences.
- **Relation to site tenets**: This is the grade the new article should assign. IBE carries simplicity weightings, so the Map's Occam's-limits tenet applies directly.

### Network and parameter models of lesions (Carrera & Tononi 2014; Boes et al. 2015; Price & Friston 2002; Parr, Rees & Friston 2018; Fotopoulou 2014)
- **Core claim**: A lesion is a perturbation of a network (diaschisis, network localisation) or a change of prior or precision parameters in one hierarchical generative model. It is neither the removal of a box nor the cutting of a wire. Degeneracy hides components; remote effects manufacture them.
- **Relation to site tenets**: This is the strongest competitor to *subtractive* reading in general. Under the scope guard it enters only as a lesion model, never as a PP-versus-dualism argument (that is `topics/predictive-processing-and-dualism`).

## Key Debates

### Can non-modular systems produce genuine double dissociations?
- **Sides**: Plaut (1995, 2003) says yes, with large-lesion, multi-instance analyses. Bullinaria & Chater (1995) say that in large distributed networks the effect disappears and is a scale artefact. Shallice (1988, as quoted by Plaut) laid down two validity conditions for a distributed-memory demonstration.
- **Core disagreement**: Whether the demonstrations meet Shallice's conditions at realistic scale.
- **Current state**: Unresolved in principle. Davies (2010) reframes the question as competition between explanations rather than as a test for modules.

### Single cases or groups?
- **Sides**: Caramazza (1986) holds that only single-case designs license valid inference. Plaut (1995) answers that equivalent lesions can produce opposite dissociations, so a single case can mislead. Shallice (2015) concludes that the methods must cross-check each other.
- **Current state**: Shallice (2015) argues that single-case and case-series/group inferences should support each other, while the single or multiple case study "remains a critical part" of the method.

### Deduction or abduction?
- **Sides**: On one side stands the folk reading: "the way it breaks reveals its structure" (the Map's L60). On the other, Shallice 1991 ("in no way entails"), Davies 2010 (IBE) and Chater 2003 (model-class constraint).
- **Current state**: Among the sources read here, defenders and critics alike treat the inference as abductive; none defends the deductive reading.

### Does locality hold?
- **Sides**: Farah (1994), Gregory, Carrera & Tononi (2014) and Boes et al. (2015) say no. Coltheart (2017) holds that the classical assumptions remain defensible.
- **Current state**: Connectional diaschisis and lesion-network mapping (Carrera & Tononi 2014; Boes et al. 2015) strengthen the anatomical case against locality. Functional locality is still argued case by case.

## What the Step Licenses: A Three-Tier Reading

This is the synthesis the article should develop. It is the note's own construction from the sources above and is not attributable to any one of them.

1. **Differential vulnerability (observed).** Capacity A can be lost while B is spared, and in a second case the reverse. The tier needs only good measurement. It is strongest for *classical* dissociations, where the spared task is within the normal range, and for double dissociations on *commensurable* measures. The Map's cluster earns this tier robustly for most of its cases.
2. **Functional differentiation (abductive, model-relative).** No member of a specified class of models can produce the pattern without some functional distinction (Chater 2003; Davies 2010). This is earned to the extent that rivals have actually been modelled and failed: single process with non-linear performance, resource or difficulty artefact, criterion shift (Phillips 2021 for blindsight), backup strategy, degeneracy, diaschisis, and precision or prior change. Where no rival has been tried, the tier is asserted, not earned.
3. **Discrete components and channels (requires bridge assumptions).** This tier requires transparency, locality, subtractivity and universality. It is weakest exactly where measures are reports of awareness, because report-based measures are where criterion and confidence effects enter. It is also where Shallice concedes the scruples lapse. Every "channel" claim in the Map's cluster lives here.

**The interface-versus-production question sits above tier 3.** The hub page's balanced verdict ("Both readings predict the same dissociation phenomenology") is therefore correct but incomplete. Both readings presuppose tier 3. If tier 3 is not earned, the six-channel table is a modelling choice, and the honest grade for "the channel architecture" drops from "earned robustly" to "one admissible decomposition among several".

**What survives for the Map** (the article should say this plainly, so the page does not overcorrect):

- Tier 1 is untouched and is what most dissociation arguments actually need. Experience depends on differentiated brain function.
- The *metaphysical* compatibility grade the 10-16 refinements installed is unaffected. It was already the lower bound.
- Degeneracy cuts both ways (Price & Friston 2002). A failure to find a dissociation does not show unity.
- Robust double dissociations in large systems may still indicate *some* separation (Bullinaria & Chater 1995).
