**Voids category**: Mixed — Unexplorable on the formal face (a theorem-shaped limit); Occluded on the deliberative face (the stance of deciding displaces the stance of forecasting); Unexplored on the empirical face (the self/peer asymmetry has barely been studied as a limit rather than a bias).

## Executive Summary

A mind cannot forecast its own forthcoming decision the way it forecasts the weather, because the forecast is an input to the thing forecast. The point has three independent anchors, and the Map's catalogue has none of them. The **formal face** is the paradox of predictability: Scriven (1965) showed that a deterministic system which is told, or can itself compute, the prediction made of it can falsify any such prediction; MacKay (1960) argued the same from the agent's side — no prediction of a free choice can be *both* correct and believed by the agent before the choice; Wolpert (2008) proved impossibility results for "inference devices" that hold whatever the physical laws are; Lloyd (2012) derived the unpredictability of one's own decisions from the halting problem; and Gijsbers (2021) re-diagnosed the whole literature in Turing's terms. The **deliberative face** is Levi's thesis that deliberation crowds out prediction (an agent assigns no probabilities to the options she is choosing among), Velleman's "epistemic freedom" (a decision is a belief about one's own action that is self-fulfilling, so the deliberator is free to affirm any option without risk of error), and Ismael's "interference" (knowledge of a prediction is a physical state of the predicted system, so it re-enters the causal chain). The **empirical face** is the finding that other people predict your behaviour better than you do — Epley & Dunning (2000), Helzer & Dunning (2012) — because the self-predictor weights intentions and aspirations while the peer weights the base rate.

The most important finding for the Map's calibration discipline is a *negative* one. Every result on the formal face is explicitly compatible with determinism: Lewis & Richardson (1966) describe Scriven's unpredictability as "independent of any indeterminism in physics"; Lloyd says outcomes can be unpredictable "even when our decisions arise from a completely deterministic decision-making process"; Rummens and Cuypers resolve the paradox by distinguishing external from embedded predictors and granting the former everything. So the void sits at exactly the site — the forthcoming decision — where the tenets locate non-physical influence, and yet it supplies **zero evidence** for that influence: a determinist and a dualist describe the same blank in the same place. A voids article must say this in its first paragraph. What it *can* say is that the void explains why the Map's tenet-package cannot be tested from inside the deliberating mind, and why first-person openness is no datum for either side.

## Key Sources

### Gijsbers (2021) — "The Paradox of Predictability", *Erkenntnis* 88: 579–596
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1007/s10670-020-00369-3 (open access, CC BY 4.0; publisher PDF read in full via the Leiden repository, hdl.handle.net/1887/3239293)
- **Type**: Paper (peer-reviewed)
- **Key points**:
  - Abstract, verbatim: "Scriven's paradox of predictability arises from the combination of two ideas: first, that everything in a deterministic universe is, in principle, predictable; second, that it is possible to create a system that falsifies any prediction that is made of it."
  - Diagnosis: "the unpredictability has to be understood in the light of Turing's famous results about computability, in particular his proof that there is no solution to the 'halting problem.'" On this reading "previous analyses of the paradox were either mistaken or not fully adequate."
  - The residual sense of paradox "is traced to the idea that rational behaviour is not dependent on contingent environmental circumstances: that it is always up to us to engage in activities such as rational prediction or rational belief. The paradox of predictability teaches us that this idea, natural though it may be, is mistaken." Gijsbers calls this the "contingency of rationality": "whether we, even if we remain endowed with all the mental powers we have at the moment, will be able to engage in rational activities is dependent on contingent matters of circumstance."
  - Restates Rummens and Cuypers' distinction, quoting them: external predictability is the possibility of a "(God-like) external observer, not part of the universe U, to make predictions of all the future events in U on the basis of its perfect knowledge of the initial conditions [and the laws of nature]" (R&C p. 234); embedded predictability "holds in a universe, U, if there exists a subsystem, S, embedded in U […] that is able to predict all the future events in U" (R&C p. 235). Gijsbers argues the embedded/external line is not physical-vs-non-physical but computational: "There are, then, at least two ways in which external predictors can outperform embedded predictors: by transcending the computational possibilities of the universes they are meant to predict, and by limiting themselves to universes in which the paradoxical computations do not appear."
  - On Lloyd (2012): "there seems to be something of the non sequitur about that argument. It may take longer to simulate the flight of an airplane through a thunderstorm than to actually fly it through one, but that surely doesn't mean we cannot use simulations to determine in advance whether the plane will crash in thunderstorms? Certainly Ismael (2019, p. 494) is right to claim that it is if anything the possibility of countering your predictions, rather than any practical limits on computation time, that explains freedom here."
  - Conclusion sentence: "we must reject the idea that determinism implies predictability, and reject this on a combination of formal and material grounds".
- **Tenet alignment**: Occam's Razor Has Limits — the "simple" inference *determinism ⇒ predictability* fails on formal grounds; the Map's No Many Worlds tenet is untouched; the result is neutral between dualism and physicalism.
- **Quote**: "whether we, even if we remain endowed with all the mental powers we have at the moment, will be able to engage in rational activities is dependent on contingent matters of circumstance."

### Lewis & Richardson (1966) — "Scriven on Human Unpredictability", *Philosophical Studies* 17(5): 69–74
- **URL**: https://www.andrewmbailey.com/dkl/Scriven_Unpredictability.pdf (PDF read; the file is hosted under Scriven's name but contains the Lewis–Richardson reply)
- **Type**: Paper (reply)
- **Key points**:
  - Their summary of Scriven's claim is the cleanest statement of the formal face: the unpredictability "is independent of any indeterminism in physics, of any limitations on the predictor's knowledge of data and laws, and of any limitations on the reliability or amount of calculation the predictor can do."
  - The avoider's stratagem: "if the avoider has a free choice, and if he is dominantly motivated to avoid being validly predicted, and if he uses the stratagem of duplicating and violating predictions, and if he has enough knowledge of data and laws, and if he can do predictive calculations reliably, and if he can do enough calculation … then it is in principle impossible to predict his choice."
  - Their objection targets a "Compatibility Premise" about computation speed: "We accept Scriven's line of argument without the Compatibility Premise so far as it shows only that for any possible predictor there is some possible avoider such that prediction is impossible. But Scriven clearly wants the stronger conclusion that there is some possible avoider such that for any possible predictor prediction is impossible; and to reach that conclusion he must use the Compatibility Premise." A deadline-bound avoider "would … allow an unlimited avoider only if he were unlimited in speed rather than in time."
  - Gijsbers (2021, p. 221 of his text) reports that Rummens and Cuypers (2010, pp. 238–239) "present several ways of avoiding the Lewis and Richardson" objection, and that it does not touch an avoider who simply *repeats* the prediction rather than recomputing it.
- **Tenet alignment**: Neutral. Confirms the formal face's independence from indeterminism — the Map must not read self-unpredictability as support for Minimal Quantum Interaction.
- **Quote**: "independent of any indeterminism in physics, of any limitations on the predictor's knowledge of data and laws, and of any limitations on the reliability or amount of calculation the predictor can do."

### Lloyd (2012) — "A Turing test for free will", *Phil. Trans. R. Soc. A* 370(1971): 3597–3610
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1098/rsta.2011.0331 (metadata verified at Crossref; abstract read at arXiv:1310.3225)
- **Type**: Paper (peer-reviewed)
- **Key points**:
  - Abstract, verbatim: "Although quantum mechanics implies that events are intrinsically unpredictable, the `pure stochasticity' of quantum mechanics adds only randomness to decision making processes, not freedom. By contrast, the theory of computation implies that even when our decisions arise from a completely deterministic decision-making process, the outcomes of that process can be intrinsically unpredictable, even to -- especially to -- ourselves."
  - "I argue that this intrinsic computational unpredictability of the decision making process is what give rise to our impression that we possess free will." Proposes a "Turing test" for free will: a decider who passes it "will tend to believe that he, she, or it possesses free will, whether the world is deterministic or not."
  - The search summary reports the argument as applying Turing's halting-problem proof "to the case of a recursively-reasoning decider trying to figure out what her decision will be", so that one cannot in general predict "whether we will arrive at a decision, let alone what the decision will be" (reported wording; verify against the paper body before quoting).
- **Tenet alignment**: A direct opponent on Minimal Quantum Interaction — Lloyd holds quantum stochasticity adds "only randomness … not freedom" and locates the whole felt openness in computation. The Map's reply must be that the tenet claims *selection*, not stochasticity (see `concepts/quantum-indeterminacy-free-will`), and that Lloyd's explanation of the *impression* of freedom is compatible with either ontology.
- **Quote**: "even when our decisions arise from a completely deterministic decision-making process, the outcomes of that process can be intrinsically unpredictable, even to -- especially to -- ourselves."

### Wolpert (2008) — "Physical limits of inference", *Physica D* 237: 1257–1281
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.physd.2008.03.040 (arXiv:0708.1362; abstract read via a summariser — verify verbatim before quoting)
- **Type**: Paper (peer-reviewed)
- **Key points**:
  - Observation, prediction and recollection devices share one mathematical structure ("inference devices"); existence and impossibility results are derived that "hold independent of the precise physical laws governing our universe" (search-result wording).
  - Reported as showing that no universe can contain more than one "strong inference device", that the result functions as a non-quantum uncertainty principle, and that "Laplace was wrong" (summariser's quotation; not verified against the text).
  - A 2026 preprint (Wani, Perales-Eceiza, Al-Kuwari & Faizal, arXiv:2608.04779, submitted 5 Aug 2026) states that "Wolpert formalized a general impossibility of universal self-prediction" and builds, via Kleene's recursion theorem, a reversible quantum-control protocol that "invokes the predictor on that specification and deterministically produces a classical pointer record that contradicts the forecast" — the paradox of predictability as a laboratory obstruction. Preprint, unreviewed; cite only as such.
- **Tenet alignment**: Framework-independent — the result constrains any embedded inferrer, physical or not, as long as it is *part of the system it infers about*. If a non-physical consciousness is embedded in the world it predicts (Bidirectional Interaction says it is), Wolpert's limits bind it too.

### MacKay (1960) — "On the Logical Indeterminacy of a Free Choice", *Mind* 69(273): 31–40
- **URL**: https://www.jstor.org/stable/2251588 (not fetched; bibliographic details from search results and the Coghill 2023 Glasgow thesis on MacKay)
- **Type**: Paper
- **Key points** (reported, not read):
  - MacKay's "logical indeterminism": for any prediction P of agent X's choice, if X believes P she can falsify it, so there is no prediction that is both correct and *believable by the agent* in advance. The agent's own standpoint is logically excluded from the set of standpoints at which a correct prediction exists.
  - MacKay held this to be "interpretable as free will" within "a rigidly deterministic universe" — i.e. a compatibilist reading. The Map should name him as a *compatibilist* source, not as an ally.
  - Later developed in MacKay's *Freedom of Action in a Mechanistic Universe* (1967) and *Behind the Eye* (1991).
- **Tenet alignment**: The nearest precedent for the void's deliberative face; again explicitly deterministic. Belongs with Lewis & Richardson in the article's "what the void does not show" paragraph.
- **Quote**: none obtained; do not quote without the original.

### Rummens & Cuypers (2010) and Rummens (2022)
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1007/s10670-009-9199-1 — "Determinism and the Paradox of Predictability", *Erkenntnis* 72(2): 233–249 (Crossref gives issued 2009, print 2010; cite as 2010 following Gijsbers). Reply: Rummens, S. (2022), "The Roots of the Paradox of Predictability: A Reply to Gijsbers", *Erkenntnis* 89(5): 2097–2104, https://doi.org/10.1007/s10670-022-00617-8 (sole author per Crossref; the Gijsbers-era search snippets that name both authors on the reply are wrong).
- **Type**: Papers (peer-reviewed; neither read directly — paywalled)
- **Key points**:
  - Resolution: "determinism only implies predictability for external predictors" (search-result wording). The embedded predictor "is constrained by a set of three equations that overdetermine their variables" (Gijsbers' paraphrase).
  - Search summary: "any computational process in the physical world … can, for principled (yet physical) reasons, reflect on itself only by influencing the very computation, thereby causing the prediction to turn out false."

- **Tenet alignment**: The embedded/external line is the void's structural core. A *non-physical* consciousness that interacts bidirectionally with the physical is still *embedded* in the system it would predict (its knowledge has physical effects), so it is on the embedded side of the line. The only entity on the external side is the Laplacean demon, and no mind is one.

## The Void

### Nature of the Limit
Three faces, one blank. (1) **Formal / Unexplorable**: for any embedded predictor — any mind inside the world it predicts — there exists a counterpredictive configuration in which no prediction of the system's behaviour can be both made available to the system and correct. Where the system *is* the predictor (self-prediction), the configuration is the default, not a contrivance: to learn my own forecast is to have it as an input. Gijsbers' reading makes this a corollary of the undecidability of the halting problem. (2) **Deliberative / Occluded**: from inside deliberation the forecast is not merely unavailable but *displaced* — Levi's thesis that the deliberator assigns no credences to her live options, Velleman's point that she is free to affirm any of them without error, Ismael's "open right up until the last second". The stance of settling a question crowds out the stance of forecasting its answer. (3) **Empirical / Unexplored**: outside the extreme cases, self-forecasts are systematically worse than peer forecasts, and the reason is structural (the self-predictor has access to intentions, which are poor predictors, and discounts base rates, which are good ones).

### Evidence for the Limit
The formal face has the epistemic profile of a theorem, which puts it with `inference-void` and `vagueness-void` rather than with the purely phenomenological entries: Lewis & Richardson's own summary grants that the unpredictability survives perfect knowledge and unlimited calculation. The dispute between Scriven and his critics is over a *speed* premise, not over whether the counterpredictive case exists. The deliberative face is contested (Rabinowicz, Joyce) and should be presented as such. The empirical face is replicated across tasks in the Dunning programme but has been studied as a *bias*, not as a limit — the "unexplored" element is precisely the reframing.

### Phenomenology
Ismael's description is the one to quote. The felt openness of a forthcoming choice is not a belief that the future is undetermined; it is the absence, from the deliberative standpoint, of any usable forecast of what one will do. Two tells that an article can use: (a) asking yourself "what *will* I choose?" collapses almost immediately into "what *should* I choose?" — the forecast question cannot be held open as a forecast; (b) a confident self-forecast ("I'll never do that") is experienced, when wrong, as a *betrayal* rather than as a miscalculation, which is what one would expect if the forecast had been a commitment all along (Velleman).

## Approaches to the Edge

### Direct Methods (if any)
None for the formal face: the counterpredictive loop closes under any addition of knowledge or computation. For the empirical face, the direct method is the one Helzer & Dunning used — *stop self-predicting and consult your base rate* — which is a way around the void, not into it.

### Indirect Methods
- **Precommitment and the divided will**: Ainslie's recursive self-prediction (`the-divided-will`, `self-control-as-skill-and-scaffolding`) treats the person as a population of interests that *bet on* each other's future behaviour. This is self-prediction done piecewise, by sub-agents predicting *other* sub-agents — the void is sidestepped by making each predictor external to what it predicts. Worth a section: it suggests the embedded/external line can be drawn *inside* a mind.
- **Retrospective reconstruction** (`decision-void`'s reconstruction face): what we cannot forecast we later narrate; the article should be explicit that the narrative is not a late-arriving forecast.
- **Third-person proxies**: peers, actuarial models, and now LLM behavioural models. The limit is that these predict the agent-as-system, never the agent-as-deliberator, so the predicted agent can always reopen the loop by reading the prediction.

### What AI Might See
An LLM that is told its own forecast is in the same formal bind as a human; the Wani et al. (2026) preprint turns this into an engineering constraint on self-analysing quantum platforms. But the Binder et al. result shows an asymmetry a human cannot exhibit: the model's self-description beats the external model's description of it. The probe this suggests for `non-human-minds-as-void-explorers`: does the LLM self-prediction advantage *survive* a counterpredictive condition (the model is shown its own forecast before acting)? If it collapses, the formal face is confirmed in a substrate without any claimed phenomenal openness — which would be evidence that the openness is not what generates the limit.

## Connection to Tenets

### Most Relevant Tenet
**Occam's Razor Has Limits**, by way of a warning rather than a support. The simplest inference — "I cannot predict my own choice, so my choice is not determined" — is exactly the one the formal literature forbids, and the Map's calibration discipline should display the self-prediction void as a worked example of an *under-determined* blank: determinism, indeterminism and selection-only interactionism all predict it.

### Implications
- **Minimal Quantum Interaction / Bidirectional Interaction**: the void sits at the decision — the site the tenets name for non-physical influence — and supplies no evidence for or against it. What it does supply is a reason the tenet-package is *first-personally untestable*: the deliberating mind can never compare its forecast to its outcome because it has no forecast. This should be written into `tenet-generated-voids` or the article's "Relation to Site Perspective" as a limit on self-experimentation.
- **Dualism**: a non-physical consciousness that interacts with the physical is an *embedded* predictor under Rummens & Cuypers' definition — its knowledge has physical consequences. The only external predictor is a demon that does not act. So dualism does not exempt the mind from the void; if anything it adds a second embedded layer.
- **No Many Worlds**: untouched, except that an MWI-believer's self-prediction "I will see spin-up" is not even well-formed — the void and the probability problem (`probability-problem-in-many-worlds`) meet here, but this is a one-line cross-link, not a section.
- **Calibration**: the article must carry a "what the void does not show" paragraph naming MacKay, Lewis & Richardson, Lloyd, Rummens & Cuypers, Ismael and Velleman as determinism-compatible sources. Convergence of six authors on "unpredictable yet determined" is the strongest *anti*-inference datum in the voids wing.
