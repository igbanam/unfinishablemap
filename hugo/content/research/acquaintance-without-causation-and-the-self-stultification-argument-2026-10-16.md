## Executive Summary

**The tension the reviewer flagged is real, and on the knowing side it does not dissolve.** The Map states the premise its self-stultification case rests on as a claim about *knowledge*: whether "knowledge of one's experience requires *causal traffic* from experience to belief" (tenets L101, `^tenet-3-epiphenomenalism`; repeated at hub `concepts/self-stultification` L149). Its acquaintance pages deny that premise for present experience. Acquaintance has no intermediary (`phenomenal-acquaintance` L70). It gives "*apodictic* evidence for the existence of experience" (L132). Constitutive observation is "in part, to be undergoing it" (`constitutive-vs-referring-observation` L43). And acquaintance answers the justification charge "however they were caused" (`metaproblem` L80). Chalmers built the epiphenomenalist's reply from exactly these materials. In 1996 he argued that a causal theory of knowledge "is not appropriate for our knowledge of consciousness" (p. 193). In 2003 he went further and *assumed* epiphenomenalism to show that constitution, "tighter than any causal connection", underwrites phenomenal knowledge (preprint p. 37). The wing never states this reply. The hub's Yetter-Chappell section comes nearest.

**The cited source for the causal premise does less than the tenets block implies.** De Brigard (2014) is the one source the tenets block cites for the causal-theory side. He *concedes* that a counterfactual condition "is all you need — and have — in the case of the knowledge of our own sensations" (pp. 123–124). His first counterargument rests on parsimony, which Tenet 5 discounts. His comparison class is the identity theory, not interactionism. And he sets Chalmers's version aside as "beyond the scope of this paper" (p. 129, n. 8).

**The two commitments can be stated as one thesis, but only by moving the premise.** The proposed statement (the Map's synthesis, not a quoted position): *experience is present to its subject without causal mediation, and that presence is evidence; but whatever the subject builds on the evidence counts as built* from *it only if the features that make experience evidence do causal work in producing it.* What gets built on evidence includes beliefs beyond the direct phenomenal belief, inferences, memories, perceptual beliefs and reports. On this statement the argument concedes the **presence** channel. It keeps two others. One is **use**: inference, memory, and perceptual justification, which Chalmers 2003 explicitly leaves open (p. 48). The other is **voice**: report and assertion, which is where the SEP's own disjunction and the hub's "cannot be rationally *asserted*" land. So the reviewer's resolution is right about knowing but too narrow on the other side. Report is one of two surviving channels, and against Chalmers's property dualist the use channel carries at least as much weight as report. Even Jackson (1982, p. 133) allowed that awareness of qualia seems to need qualia to make "a difference to other mental states". So the originator of the common-cause reply put causation into the use channel himself.

**The relocated premise costs interactionism something too.** At use and voice, what the argument needs is that experience be efficacious *in virtue of* its evidential (phenomenal) features. Bare interactionism, on which consciousness causes but not in virtue of what it presents, does no better than epiphenomenalism here. Robinson's interactionist diagram (SEP §2.4) and Mørch's phenomenal-powers point (already on the Map at `inheritance-problem-for-the-psychophysical-law` L70) both show this. The Map passes only through Tenet 3's content-expressing clause ("toward patterns that express its content"). That clause is a posit the interface argument leaves open (`^tenet-3-standing`).

## The Tension, Stated from the Map's Own Pages

**The premise side.** The tenets block holds that "Whether the self-stultification objection then bites depends on whether knowledge of one's experience requires *causal traffic* from experience to belief—the causal-theory-of-knowledge premise De Brigard (2014) presses" (tenets L101). The hub ends its Coincidence Response with the same premise: "What this reply still owes is an account of how a lawful parallel yields *knowledge* of experience without causal traffic from it" (L149). The hub's classic formulation turns on "5. A belief causally disconnected from rational evaluation cannot be rationally justified." (L95).

**The acquaintance side.**
- `phenomenal-acquaintance` L70: "No inference, representation, or intermediary stands between subject and quality." It adds: "This differs from perception of external objects, where causal chains mediate between perceiver and world."
- `phenomenal-acquaintance` L132: acquaintance "supplies *apodictic* evidence for the existence of experience".
- `constitutive-vs-referring-observation` L43: "*To be acquainted with phenomenality is, in part, to be undergoing it.*"
- `metaproblem-of-consciousness-under-dualism` L80: "A natural thought is that direct acquaintance with experience justifies our problem intuitions however they were caused." Then: "Acquaintance answers the charge that our beliefs are unjustified; the coincidence argument declines to press that charge."
- `phenomenal-acquaintance` L122 (phenomenal intentionality): "Acquaintance is the mechanism: acquaintance with the qualitative character of thinking about coffee is what makes the thought genuinely *about* coffee". This is a *constitutive* role for experience in content, and it is exactly the role Chalmers 2003 uses on the epiphenomenalist's behalf.

**How much the Map's own text already dissolves.** It dissolves part of the tension. Two Map sentences already separate presence from what is built on it. `constitutive-vs-referring-observation` L47 says "The constitutive claim concerns seemings, not the beliefs and reports a seeming usually produces." `phenomenal-acquaintance` L130 says "The resolution lies in distinguishing the relation from judgements based on it." So the Map can say, without contradiction, that acquaintance supplies evidence non-causally while belief is formed downstream. But "downstream" and "produces" then carry the whole argument. They amount to a causal theory of *basing*, and the Map has stated that theory nowhere. Chalmers 2003 replaces causal basing with constitution for exactly the class of belief at issue (below). So the Map cannot rest on the downstream reading without arguing against constitution as a basing relation. Its own phenomenal-intentionality commitment makes that argument harder. **Conclusion: the tension is real at the presence channel, and only a restatement removes it.** It does not dissolve on a careful reading, so option (c) is ruled out.

`phenomenal-acquaintance` L146 already points the right way. It locates the causal claim at *reports*: "That acquaintance reliably produces accurate reports suggests the relation is causally efficacious." The optimistic review's Medium opportunity B flags the same paragraph's next sentence as residual "luck" framing.

## Key Sources

### Chalmers, *The Conscious Mind* (1996), ch. 5 "The Paradox of Phenomenal Judgment"
- **URL**: archive.org item `chalmers-the-conscious-mind` (OCR text)
- **Type**: Book chapter (pp. 172–209)
- **Key points**:
  - The paradox is about *explanatory* irrelevance: "More strongly, it seems that consciousness is explanatorily irrelevant to our claims and judgments about consciousness. This result I call the paradox of phenomenal judgment." (p. 177). Causal irrelevance he leaves open: "the question of whether consciousness is causally irrelevant in the production of behavior is a complex metaphysical issue that is best left open" (p. 177). For the self-knowledge arguments he concedes it anyway: "In order to give these arguments their full power, I will temporarily concede the causal irrelevance of experience, in order to see whether the arguments succeed." (p. 191).
  - The interactionist answer is in the chapter, credited to Elitzur: "he argues instead for an interactionist dualism." (p. 177). The Map cites Elitzur 1989 only at `arguments/materialism-argument` L88.
  - He states the causal-theory argument in its strongest form: "If a causal theory of knowledge is correct, it follows that we cannot know anything about our experiences." (p. 193). He also records its origin as an argument *for* materialism: "Shoemaker (1975a) uses arguments like these to argue for materialism about consciousness, and in fact to argue for reductive functionalism." (p. 193).
  - His reply: "A property dualist should argue that a causal theory of knowledge is not appropriate for our knowledge of consciousness, and that the justification of our judgments about consciousness does not lie with the mechanisms by which those judgments are formed." (p. 193). And: "Our knowledge of conscious experience does not consist in a causal relationship to experience, but in another sort of relationship entirely." (p. 193).
  - **The certainty argument** (pp. 194–195) bears directly on the Map. He grants that reliabilism fits epiphenomenalism: "It is entirely compatible with a nonreductive theory of experience that in the actual world, our phenomenal judgments are reliable: at least as a matter of nomic correlation" (p. 194). He then rejects reliabilism and causal accounts alike: "Where there is causation, there is contingency: a causal connection that holds might not have held." He concludes: "But we do know for certain that we are conscious; so a causal account of this knowledge is inappropriate." (p. 195).
  - The positive view: "But intuitively, our access to consciousness is not mediated at all." (p. 196). "it is having the experiences that justifies the beliefs." (p. 196). He names the relation "a relation that we might call “acquaintance.”" (p. 197).
  - Against the objection that a belief in the physical realm must have its justification there: "the justification of my belief accrues not just in virtue of my physical features but in virtue of some of my nonphysical features—namely the experiences themselves." (p. 198). This prefigures Yetter-Chappell's "you are not your brain" move.
  - On the counterfactual objection: "It might still be objected, “But the belief would still have been formed even if the experience had been absent!” To this, the answer is, “So what?”" (p. 198). And: "To say that the experience makes no difference to my psychological functioning is not to say that the experience makes no difference to me." (p. 198).
  - **The concession on the third-person side**, which is where the voice channel lives: "At most this shows that from the third-person point of view, my zombie twin and I are identical, so that you cannot be certain that I am conscious; but we knew this all along." (pp. 198–199).
  - **The concession on memory**, which is where the use channel lives: "there will certainly be a causal connection at the level of psychology" (p. 200). The connection runs between belief states. The experience is not a relatum, and he concludes "So there seems to be good reason to believe that a causal connection to an experience is not required to remember that experience." (p. 201).
- **Tenet alignment**: Conflicts with Tenet 3 as a defence of epiphenomenalism's coherence; aligns with Tenet 1 (it presupposes property dualism).

### Chalmers, "The Content and Epistemology of Phenomenal Belief" (2003)
- **URL**: https://consc.net/papers/belief.pdf (preprint); doi 10.1093/oso/9780199241286.003.0009
- **Type**: Book chapter, in Q. Smith & A. Jokic (eds.), *Consciousness: New Philosophical Perspectives*, OUP, pp. 220–272
- **Key points**:
  - **It supersedes ch. 5.** Footnote 15: "I now think that discussion is at best suboptimal." and "So the present discussion can be viewed in part as a replacement for that chapter." (p. 36). An article should cite 2003 as the canonical reply and 1996 for the paradox's statement and the certainty argument.
  - **Constitution**: in direct phenomenal beliefs, "the content of a phenomenal concept and a corresponding phenomenal belief, is partly constituted by an underlying phenomenal quality" (p. 16).
  - **The Justification Thesis**: "When a subject forms a direct phenomenal belief based on a phenomenal quality, then that belief is prima facie justified by virtue of the subject's acquaintance with that quality." (p. 30).
  - **Constitution takes causation's place in basing, and causation stays for inference.** For an inferred belief, "the belief must be appropriately caused by the justifying belief" (pp. 31–32). For a direct phenomenal belief, "Second, a natural connection: the phenomenal belief must be appropriately constituted by the experience." (p. 32). *Chalmers himself, then, requires causation for inferential justification.* This is the hook for the use channel.
  - **It is run against epiphenomenalism explicitly.** He states Shoemaker's argument as "(1) If phenomenal realism is true, experiences are causally irrelevant to phenomenal beliefs." and "(2) If experiences are causally irrelevant to phenomenal beliefs, phenomenal beliefs are not knowledge." (p. 36). He then sets up the hard case: "I will assume epiphenomenalism, according to which the phenomenal has no effects on the physical domain." He goes on: "The view I have outlined makes it easy to see why this argument fails, even against an epiphenomenalist." Then the key claim: "But if what I have said is correct, the connection between experience and phenomenal belief is tighter than any causal connection: it is constitution. And if a causal connection can underwrite knowledge, a constitutive connection can certainly underwrite knowledge too." (p. 37).
  - The fallback, without constitution: "Even without appealing to constitution, the epiphenomenalist can respond reasonably to this argument by appealing to the notion of acquaintance, and arguing that a subject's acquaintance with experience can non-causally justify a phenomenal belief." With it: "Acquaintance and constitution together enable a theoretical model of the justification of phenomenal belief (as above), a model that is compatible with epiphenomenalism." (p. 37).
  - **Not an endorsement**: "I am not endorsing epiphenomenalism, but I regard it as one of the three serious options that remain once one accepts phenomenal realism and rules out type-B materialism and idealism." (p. 37, n. 16).
  - **Zombies do not share our phenomenal beliefs**: "it is clear that a zombie cannot share a conscious being's phenomenal beliefs." (p. 38). Also "and its corresponding beliefs are not even formed by the same mechanism, since constitution by a phenomenal quality plays a central role in forming a direct phenomenal belief." (p. 39). The reply reaches "I am conscious": "So beliefs involving the standing concept of experience (such as I am conscious) are equally unthreatened by this argument." (p. 40).
  - **Interactionism is named and set aside for the hard case**: "a property dualist could hold that these effects proceed through a fundamental causal connection between the phenomenal and physical domains" (p. 36).
  - **What the reply does not reach**: "This gap between phenomenal knowledge and knowledge of the external world remains as wide as ever, and I have done nothing to close it." (p. 48). Footnote 21's option "(3) extend the account so that perceptual experiences can justify perceptual beliefs directly, through a constitutive connection to perceptual concepts analogous to the connection to phenomenal concepts." is deferred to later work (p. 49).
  - **The certainty argument and its critic**: "I argued in The Conscious Mind (1996) that something like acquaintance is required to secure certainty, and that a mere causal connection or reliable connection cannot do the job." and "In response, a number of philosophers, including Bayne (2001), have argued that acquaintance accounts can be criticized in a similar way." (p. 34, n. 14). Bayne's point, as Chalmers reports it, is that acquaintance alone allows inattention-driven error, so certainty needs background factors that themselves cannot be certified.
- **Tenet alignment**: Neutral on Tenet 3 as metaphysics (it is offered as a defence, not an endorsement); it sharply limits the self-stultification argument as the Map states it.
- **Quote**: "the connection between experience and phenomenal belief is tighter than any causal connection: it is constitution."

### De Brigard, "In Defence of the Self-Stultification Objection" (2014)
- **URL**: https://people.duke.edu/~fd13/2014/De_Brigard_2014_JoCS.pdf
- **Type**: Journal article, *Journal of Consciousness Studies* 21(5–6): 120–130
- **Key points**:
  - Target: "if epiphenomenalism were true, we would not have knowledge of our own sensations" (abstract, p. 120). The opponent is W.S. Robinson, whose strategy is to show "epiphenomenalism is no less stultifying than its contenders, such as dualism, functionalism, or identity theory" (abstract). Robinson is quoted (Robinson 2006 p. 91, via De Brigard p. 121): "It would, therefore, seem that the burden of proof is on opponents of epiphenomenalism to show why that view should be thought to be any more self-stultifying than Cartesian dualism".
  - **Origin**: the objection goes back to "(Shoemaker, 1975; Dennett, 1978)" (p. 121). In Robinson's reconstruction (p. 122) its conclusion is "Any dualism which includes the claim that we have non-inferential knowledge about our (non-physical) sensations is false." The causal premise is (5): "For any subject S and any x, if S non-inferentially knows [de re] something about x, then x must cause some change in S which is causally contributory to S's believing what he does about x." (p. 122). So in this reconstruction the objection is aimed at *dualism* in general, given closure. Interactionism escapes only by denying premise (3), that the causes of physical events are all physical. The interactionist must *keep* premise (5).
  - **The concession**: "I am happy to concede the point Robinson wants to extract from it: namely that thesis (A) is all you need — and have — in the case of the knowledge of our own sensations." (pp. 123–124). Thesis (A) is the counterfactual condition (S would not believe x is F if x were not F); thesis (B) is causation. Of the interactionist's diagram he writes: "thus no physical explanation of the knowledge of F is available to the interactionist." (p. 124).
  - **First counterargument (content)**: the content–object relation "must be, at the very least, nomological" (p. 125). He notes in n. 4 that "not all agree that the nomological connection needs to be causal." (p. 125). The epiphenomenalist could posit a non-causal nomological relation, but "this move requires the postulation of an additional sort of nomological relation the identity theorist need not be committed to" (p. 126). **This is a parsimony argument, and the Map's Tenet 5 discounts it.** Also, a *constitutive* connection (Chalmers 2003) is stronger than nomological, so the argument leaves the acquaintance reply untouched.
  - **Comparison class**: "I did not discuss functionalism here because Robinson's argument focuses on identity theory alone." (p. 127, n. 6). Interactionism is not the comparison.
  - **Second counterargument (timing)**: one horn is that a last mental state outlives bodily annihilation. He calls this troublesome because it gives up epiphenomenalism's advantage over dualism (p. 128). That consideration has no force for a substance dualist.
  - **Chalmers excluded**: "Chalmers' version of epiphenomenalism (1996, pp. 150ff.) may avoid this move. It would involve accepting a totally different account of mental causation, nonetheless. Still, whether it can do it or not is beyond the scope of this paper." (p. 129, n. 8).
  - Calibrated conclusion: "In conclusion, it seems as though we still have good reason to believe that epiphenomenalism is rather suspicious." (p. 129).
- **Tenet alignment**: Supports Tenet 3's conclusion against *common-cause* epiphenomenalism only; it offers nothing against the acquaintance or constitution epiphenomenalist, and its main lever (parsimony) is one Tenet 5 weakens.

### Robinson, "Epiphenomenalism", SEP (rev. 2023), §2.4
- **URL**: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/epiphenomenalism/
- **Type**: Encyclopedia. The author is himself a party to the debate, on the epiphenomenalist-friendly side.
- **Key points**:
  - "The most powerful reason for rejecting epiphenomenalism is the view that it is incompatible with knowledge of our own minds".
  - Premise: "(i) knowledge of one's mental events requires that these events cause one's knowledge, but (ii) epiphenomenalism denies physical effects of mental events."
  - **The disjunction that matches the voice channel**: "So, either we cannot know our own mental events, or our knowledge of them cannot be what is causing the plainly physical event of our saying something about our mental events." The acquaintance reply takes the second horn, which leaves the *saying* as the residue. The SEP draws the strong consequence that "S’s testimonies about S’s own pains are worthless", both to us and to S (the SEP sets "S" in math markup; the rendered text is quoted).
  - Reply: "But epiphenomenalists may reject that premise without absurdity." **Parity with interactionism (Figure 2)**: a later physical event P3, caused by M only through P2, "does not have any property that contains information as to how it was caused". Denying it knowledge-conveying status means "we will be rejecting interactionism for virtually the same reason that epiphenomenalism is thought to be unacceptable."
  - On Chalmers: "In supplying non-causal relations to support the claim to knowledge of experiences, this view disconnects the knowledge question from the question of how things stand causally, and thus avoids the self-stultification argument." Chalmers is listed with "(see also Nagasawa 2010)".
  - Pauen 2006 argues that knowing the psychophysical laws needs memory of past qualia, which epiphenomenalism cannot certify. Staudacher replies with parity: "Staudacher points out that if we are allowed to raise skeptical challenges of the kind Pauen raises, it will be possible to construct analogous, unanswerable skeptical challenges for interactionism."
- **Tenet alignment**: Conflicts with Tenet 3's argumentative support; the parity arguments are the strongest pressure on the relocated premise (below).

### Jackson, "Epiphenomenal Qualia" (1982)
- **Key points**: Jackson's thesis is scoped to the physical: "Is there any really good reason for refusing to countenance the idea that qualia are causally impotent with respect to the physical world?" (p. 133). And: "Qualia cause nothing physical but are caused by something physical." (p. 134). The 1982 paper answers three objections (the obviousness of pain causing behaviour, Darwin, other minds). The self-knowledge objection is not among them.
- **Jackson leaves mental-to-mental causation open, and self-awareness is his reason.** He sets aside the classical view that "the mental is totally causally inefficacious" (p. 133). Then: "For all I will say it may be that you have to hold that the instantiation of qualia makes a difference to other mental states though not to anything physical. Indeed general considerations to do with how you could come to be aware of the instantiation of qualia suggest such a position." (p. 133; it counts 1 only after repairing the scan's split "t o" → "to", the repair the optimistic review also needed). So the originator of the common-cause reply concedes that *awareness* of qualia seems to need qualia to make a causal difference, within the mind. That is the use channel kept internal. Against such an epiphenomenalist only the voice channel remains, and this is the Jacksonian ancestor of Yetter-Chappell's position (below).

## Major Positions

### The causal theory of phenomenal knowledge (Shoemaker 1975; Dennett 1978; De Brigard 2014; Moore 2012, 2014)
- **Core claim**: non-inferential knowledge of x requires that x causally contribute to the belief.
- **Relation to tenets**: The Map needs a version of this for self-stultification, but in its original form the objection is aimed at dualism in general, and De Brigard's defence of it is framed against the identity theory. The Map can take premise (5) only for what is *built on* experience, not for presence, or it contradicts `phenomenal-acquaintance` L70 and L132.

### The counterfactual / common-cause reply (Jackson 1982; Robinson 1982, 2006, 2013)
- **Core claim**: knowledge needs only that the belief would not occur without the experience; a common cause secures that. Robinson's 2013 title, "Experiencing is not Observing", states the same structural point as the Map's constitutive-vs-referring distinction.
- **Relation to tenets**: This is the reply the hub's Coincidence Response now credits (L147). Its parity argument (SEP Figure 2) applies at the voice channel too.

### Acquaintance plus constitution (Chalmers 1996, 2003; Nagasawa 2010 per SEP)
- **Core claim**: phenomenal belief is justified by acquaintance and partly constituted by the experience, so no causal link is needed; compatible with epiphenomenalism.
- **Relation to tenets**: Aligns with Tenet 1. It concedes the presence channel to the epiphenomenalist. **The Map's own acquaintance and phenomenal-intentionality pages accept its premises.** Its stated limit (external-world knowledge, p. 48) is where the use channel begins.

### Dualism all the way down (Yetter-Chappell 2022)
- **Core claim**: the paradox comes from pairing dualism about qualia with physicalism about subjects. A non-physical judging subject relates to its experience by acquaintance.
- **Relation to tenets**: Aligns with Tenets 1 and 4 (a non-physical subject with determinate identity). If the subject has its own internal causal life, use runs inside the mind and only the voice channel remains. Whether Yetter-Chappell grants that is UNVERIFIED, but Jackson 1982 p. 133 already contemplates qualia making "a difference to other mental states though not to anything physical". That is where the hub's concession (L181) lands.

### Interactionist dualism (Elitzur 1989; the Map)
- **Core claim**: reports about consciousness are caused partly by consciousness; the zombie stipulation is rejected (`philosophical-zombies` L163).
- **Relation to tenets**: Tenet 3. **The relocated argument favours interactionism only if consciousness is efficacious in virtue of what it presents** (Tenet 3's Definition: "toward patterns that express its content").

## Key Debates

### Is the causal premise about knowing, using or voicing?
- **Sides**: Shoemaker and De Brigard state it about knowing. The SEP's disjunction separates knowing from "saying". The hub's Yetter-Chappell section retreats to asserting.
- **Current state**: on the Map's own epistemology the knowing version is unavailable. The research finding is that the argument survives at two channels, use and voice. The reviewer named one.

### Would a causal ground undercut certainty, for interactionists as well?
- **Sides**: Chalmers (1996 p. 195): causal grounds bring contingency, so they cannot deliver certainty. Bayne (2001, per Chalmers 2003 n. 14): acquaintance needs background conditions too.
- **Bearing on the Map**: the Map claims apodictic standing for the bare existence of experience (`phenomenal-acquaintance` L132; `constitutive-vs-referring-observation`). If it made causal traffic the *source* of that standing, Chalmers's argument would turn on the Map. Tenets L101 says "requires", a necessary condition, not the source. So the Map can survive only by treating causal traffic as a basing condition. Once constitution is granted for direct phenomenal beliefs, that condition does its work in the use channel.

### Parity: is interactionism better off?
- **Sides**: Robinson's P3 argument (SEP) and Staudacher's interactionist skeptical scenarios say no. De Brigard says the identity theorist is better off; he does not argue that the interactionist is.
- **Current state**: at the presence channel, parity holds; the interactionist's acquaintance is no better than the epiphenomenalist's. At use and voice, interactionism has an advantage only through efficacy in virtue of phenomenal content (next).

### The efficacy-of-evidence premise
- **Statement (Map synthesis)**: a belief, inference or assertion is held or made *from* evidence E only if E's evidential features causally contribute to producing it.
- **Strongest objection, parity with content externalism**: on externalist theories, wide content also fails to affect what local vehicles do beyond the vehicles themselves. Few epistemologists conclude that inference among externally individuated beliefs is unjustified. If the Map accepts the premise for phenomenal evidence, it must say why phenomenal constituents differ from wide content here. Possible answer (Map synthesis, to be argued): wide content is fixed by the causal history of the vehicle, so it is efficacious at one remove, whereas epiphenomenal phenomenal content is fixed by a constituent with no causal history in the vehicle at all.
- **Cost to interactionism**: Mørch's result, already on the Map at `inheritance-problem-for-the-psychophysical-law` L70, is that the evolutionary argument fails for "standard interactionism" and works for a phenomenal-powers view on which phenomenal properties produce effects "in virtue of how they feel". The relocated self-stultification argument has the same shape. Bare interactionism stands with the epiphenomenalist. The Map's position depends on Tenet 3's content clause, and its standing is the open debt at `^tenet-3-standing` and [P-Q3](/positions/quantum-interface/#p-q3).

## Three Channels: The Answer to the One-Thesis Question

| Channel | What it covers | Chalmers's property dualist | Yetter-Chappell's subject dualist | Interactionist (Map) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Presence** | acquaintance with present experience; direct phenomenal belief; "I am conscious" | secured by constitution (2003 pp. 32, 37, 40) | secured by acquaintance (abstract) | secured by acquaintance; *no advantage* (Robinson parity) |
| **Use** | inference to theory; memory; perceptual justification | runs through physical vehicles whose phenomenal constituents are idle; Chalmers requires causation for inference (pp. 31–32), allows memory only "at the level of psychology" (p. 200), leaves external-world knowledge open (p. 48) | possibly internal to the non-physical mind (UNVERIFIED for her; Jackson 1982 p. 133 contemplates qualia affecting "other mental states") | advantage only if consciousness is efficacious in virtue of content |
| **Voice** | report, assertion, publication | assertion caused by the judgment's vehicle; experience contributes content, not production; third-person concession (pp. 198–199) | assertion produced by a brain state that is no part of the subject's belief (hub L179, Map reading) | reports shaped by what they report, per Tenet 3's Definition (L91) |

**Answer.** Yes, one thesis covers both commitments, but it moves the premise from knowing to use and voice. In a Map-voice formulation for an article: *knowing by acquaintance needs no causation; holding, reasoning from and voicing what one knows does.* The self-stultification argument then concludes that the epiphenomenalist's theory is not held or voiced *from* their evidence, not that they cannot know their experience. That is a narrower conclusion than the hub's lead ("it cannot be rationally held"), which the open P3 task already conditionalises. Against Chalmers it is broader than the reviewer's "report channel", because the use channel bites too.
