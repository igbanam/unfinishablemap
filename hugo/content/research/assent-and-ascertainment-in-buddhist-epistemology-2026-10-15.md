In the Theravāda Abhidhamma, "decision" (*adhimokkha*) is a mental factor that arises together with a cognition, listed in the canon only as a jhāna factor (MN 111) and supplied to the Dhammasaṅgaṇī's first consciousness by the commentary through the "or whatever" clause; it is absent from the five bare sense-consciousnesses and from the one consciousness accompanied by doubt, so determinacy and doubt exclude each other as co-arising states, not as options an agent chooses between. ## The Abhidhamma Strand

Three facts settle what *adhimokkha* is.

**It is a co-arising factor, not an act upon a cognition.** The Abhidhamma's unit is the *cittuppāda*, a consciousness together with its associated factors (*cetasika*) that "arise on that occasion". *Adhimokkha* is one of the six "Particulars" (*pakiṇṇaka*) that accompany some consciousnesses and not others; it is defined, like every factor, by the standard four-fold schema (characteristic, function, manifestation, proximate cause) and its proximate cause is "a thing to be convinced about" or "an object fit to be decided". Nothing in the definitions names a subject who decides; the "deciding" is the factor's own function. The canonical attestation (MN 111) is a list of states Sāriputta "distinguished one by one" within each absorption. Van Gorkom's modern Theravāda gloss states the orthodox reading outright: "there is no self who can decide this"; "Adhimokkha is not self; it is sankhara dhamma, a conditioned dhamma which arises and falls away with the citta it accompanies."

**It is commentarial in the Dhammasaṅgaṇī, canonical in the Suttas.** The Dhammasaṅgaṇī's first consciousness lists more than fifty states but not *adhimokkha*; Buddhaghosa reads it in through "or whatever other incorporeal, causally induced states there are on that occasion", as one of nine "or-whatever" (*yevāpanaka*) states, with desire (*chanda*), attention (*manasikāra*) and equanimity. This matters for any claim about its antiquity: the four-fold definition is fifth-century (Buddhaghosa), and the term's canonical home is the jhāna lists of MN 111, not a theory of perception.

**It is absent exactly where there is no determinate object.** Nārada's arithmetic (89 − 11 = 78) excludes the ten bare sense-consciousnesses (two sets of five) and the single consciousness "accompanied by doubt". Seeing-consciousness has only the seven universals and so no decision; the doubt-consciousness has doubt instead of decision ("devoid of decision", Nārada; Pali *adhimokkha virahitā*). The relation to doubt is therefore exclusion within a single moment: a consciousness has one or the other. That is not the Stoic's option to withhold, since nothing withholds; it is a classification of which momentary state obtains. The relation to faith (*saddhā*) is one of support: the palikanon dictionary records that commentaries make faith inspire "confidence (okappana, pasāda) and determination (adhimokkha)", and the Expositor's definition of faith has "decision" as one description of its manifestation ("freedom from pollution or decision as its manifestation"). Faith is a "beautiful" factor confined to wholesome and related consciousnesses; *adhimokkha* is ethically variable and accompanies unwholesome consciousnesses too.

**The process model supplies a stage called "determining".** In the Abhidhammattha-saṅgaha's seventeen-moment eye-door process, sense-consciousness is followed by receiving, investigating and "determining" (*voṭṭhabbana*/*votthapana*) before the seven *javana* moments in which the ethically significant response occurs. Bodhi's guide: its function "is to determine, or define, the object that has been cognized by sense consciousness", and "There is no distinct citta known as determining consciousness" (it is the mind-door adverting consciousness doing a second job). Ganeri reads determining as "object perception, drawing on stored models of objects". This is the nearest structural analogue to Dharmakīrti's ascertainment inside the Theravāda: a non-conceptual sense-moment followed by a moment that fixes what the object is, before any response.

## The Dharmakīrti Strand

**The two stages.** Perception (*pratyakṣa*) is "devoid of conceptual construction" (Dignāga's definition, *kalpanāpoḍha*) and "fixed" by its object; it cannot by itself say "this is blue". Action, language and knowledge in the ordinary sense require a conceptual cognition that follows it. Dharmakīrti calls the perceptual case the "conceptual cognition that follows a perceptual cognition" (*pratyakṣapṛṣṭhabhāvivikalpa*) and classes it with inference as a "determinate cognition" (*niścayajñāna*). Tillemans's note 26 records that the demand for ascertainment is Dharmakīrti's innovation over Dignāga, and that Tibetan scholastics generalise it: to know *P* conceptually one "must herself ascertain *P*".

**Ascertainment is caused, and can fail.** The PVSV passage ad I.58 says that even when a partless particular has been experienced, ascertainment "does not arise with respect to all its distinctions" merely on that basis, because it depends on further causes (*kāraṇāntarāpekṣyatvāt*): experience generates ascertaining cognitions according to one's habituation to concepts, and the co-operating causes are acuity, habituation to the imprint and the situation-context (Nakasuka's list). The standard example, which Dunne reports, is the person who sees fire while distracted and "will only know that I am seeing fire when, for example, I infer its presence from seeing the smoke rising above that spot" (p. 321). Tibetan epistemology names this state "inattentive cognition" (*snang la ma nges pa*, appearing but not ascertained) and makes it a distinct type of mind (Dreyfus pp. 373, 392). Kellner 2004 (as Nakasuka reports it) gives ascertainment's job as keeping "error and doubt away".

**Whether perception needs ascertainment to count as knowledge was disputed.** Dharmottara's commentary builds the ascertaining cognition into the account of how perception is a *pramāṇa*: the perceptual cognition "is ascertained (avasthāpyate) to be grasping blue by the [subsequent] ascertaining cognition (niścayapratyaya)", which is "a conceptual cognition arisen from the force of perception". Prajñākaragupta denied this: habituated perception prompts action "without ascertaining", through its object-form alone, and where a person "acts depending on ascertainment" the *pramāṇa* is inference (Miyo 2020, p. 276). Dunne's translation (p. 375) preserves the underlying distinction: "Activity that is based on perception is twofold: initial and habitual." Dharmakīrti himself, Miyo notes, "accepts that some other factors intervene between perception and the action that it prompts".

**Action does not wait on ascertainment.** The passage Dunne translates at p. 291 lists "two causes which compel one to act: doubt about an object (artha) and the definitive determination of an object (artha)", and defends acting from doubt as what a "judicious" person (*prekṣāpūrvakārin*) does when the stakes warrant investigation. This is the clearest contrast with the Stoics, for whom assent is the precondition of rational impulse.

**Knowledge does not compel the agent.** Dharmottara's gloss on the *Nyāyabindu*'s first sentence ("All successful human action is preceded by right knowledge") denies that knowledge "has the power forcibly to incite a man (against his will)"; right knowledge "points out" an object "as an aim of a (possible) purposive action". The voluntarism that survives here is about action after cognition, not about the cognition.

## Comparison With the Stoic and Cartesian Structures

[assent-before-descartes](/topics/assent-before-descartes/) §The Stoic Double Claim records two Stoic commitments: assent is "up to us" in a way receiving an impression is not, and "he who perceives anything, assents immediately", so that the sage's freedom is a freedom to withhold from the non-cognitive. [doxastic-voluntarism](/concepts/doxastic-voluntarism/) and [assent-after-descartes-2026-10-15](/research/assent-after-descartes-2026-10-15/) track whether assent is a separate act from comprehension and whether it is voluntary.

| Is there a stage after apprehension that fixes the content? | Yes: assent (*synkatathesis*) to the impression | Yes: the will's assent to the intellect's perception | Yes: ascertainment (*niścaya*), a conceptual cognition following perception | Yes: "determining" (*voṭṭhabbana*) in the process; *adhimokkha* as a co-arising factor |
| Is that stage an act of an agent? | Yes, by definition ("in our power") | Yes (the will) | No: a cognition caused by habituation, acuity and context | No: a conditioned state; "there is no self who can decide this" |
| Can it be withheld? | From the non-cognitive, yes | Where the perception is not clear and distinct, yes | It can fail to arise (inattention), but is not withheld | It is absent in bare sense-consciousness and in doubt; not withheld |
| Relation to doubt | Suspension (*epochē*) is an attitude a cognizer takes | Doubt is the method, an act | Ascertainment keeps doubt away; action can proceed from doubt | Doubt and decision exclude each other within a moment |
| Does the evident compel? | Yes ("the mind must yield to what is evident") | Yes, for the clear and distinct | Habituated perception yields determination (Prajñākaragupta: without needing it) | Determinate cognitions carry *adhimokkha* |

So the ordering the reviewer spotted is real: comprehension comes first and determination second in both Buddhist systems. What the Buddhist systems lack is the thing the Cartesian question is about, an agent whose act the second stage is. Where the Stoics said "compelled by the evident, free elsewhere", Dharmakīrti says "caused by habituation, absent under distraction, and never anyone's doing". The Buddhist material is therefore evidence on the timing face of the assent cluster and silent on the control face.

Two secondary points. First, the Nyāya definition of perception (*Nyāyasūtra* 1.1.4) puts "well-ascertained" (*vyavasāyātmaka*) inside perception, so for the realists the second stage (*savikalpaka*) is still perception and still valid, whereas the Buddhists keep it outside (Chadha, SEP). The Indian debate is thus over where to draw the perception/judgment line, not over whether a judgment is up to us. Second, Sāṃkhya's definition of perception as "an ascertainment [of *buddhi*]" (*adhyavasāya*) puts determination on the matter side, in the intellect, which is the placement [indian-philosophy-of-mind](/topics/indian-philosophy-of-mind/) already notes for Sāṃkhya's *buddhi*.

## The Three Questions

**1. Does Dharmakīrti's perception-then-ascertainment ordering give a comprehension-before-assent structure outside the Cartesian tradition, or is *niścaya* a different kind of thing?** It gives the ordering and not the structure. *Niścaya* is a conceptual determination ("this is blue"), a second cognition "arisen from the force of perception", whose occurrence is explained by causes and whose non-occurrence is a recognised type of mind (inattentive cognition). It is closer to what the Stoics called the impression's propositional content being formed than to assent to it; the school has no further stage at which the determination is endorsed, and action can proceed from doubt without it.

**2. Is *adhimokkha* an act the agent performs or a factor that arises with the cognition, and what does each answer do to the voluntarism question?** A factor that arises with the cognition, by every source: a *cetasika* defined by its own characteristic and function, listed among states "that there then are", present or absent according to the type of consciousness and not according to anyone's choice. Had it been an act, the Abhidhamma would have supplied a non-Western doxastic voluntarism; being a factor, it supplies the opposite, a determinacy that is a property of the moment. The one place a choice-like description appears is Nārada's modern simile ("compared to a judge that decides a case"), and the Expositor's "choice of the same", but both are glosses on a state whose proximate cause is "an object fit to be decided", which is to say the object does the work.

**3. What do the contemplative reports of "grasping" at perception amount to in the Abhidhamma vocabulary?** The vocabulary offers three candidates and no report. Determining (*voṭṭhabbana*) fixes what the object is; *adhimokkha* is the factor of being settled on it; the *javana* moments are where the response (including craving, *taṇhā*, and clinging, *upādāna*, whose root sense is grasping) occurs. All three are posits of a process model that assigns seventeen moments to a sight; the model's authority is doctrinal and systematic, and Ganeri notes that even Buddhaghosa's own terms must have their import "deduced from the similes he gives". None of the sources read here presents a first-person report of a noticed grasping at the moment of perception. The assent void's L101 sentence can be closed in the negative: the Abhidhamma analyses the formation event into stages but does not report it.

> ## A Buddhist Comparison: Ascertainment Without an Act {#buddhist-ascertainment}
>
> The Buddhist epistemologists reached the same two-stage picture from the other side and left the act out. For Dharmakīrti (seventh century) perception is non-conceptual and fixed by its object; what makes it usable is a conceptual ascertainment (*niścaya*) that follows it, "the conceptual cognition that follows a perceptual cognition" (Nakasuka 2020, on *Pramāṇavārttika* I.48). Ascertainment is caused rather than performed. Its causes are acuity, habituation and context, and under distraction a seen fire goes unascertained until one infers it from the smoke (Dunne 2004, 321). Action does not wait for it either: a judicious person may act from doubt (Dunne 2004, 291). The Stoic sage withholds; Dharmakīrti's perceiver cannot, since there is no one to withhold. Dharmottara, commenting on the *Nyāyabindu*'s opening sentence, adds the limit the Stoics also drew: right knowledge does not have "the power forcibly to incite a man (against his will)" (Stcherbatsky's translation, 1930). The Theravāda Abhidhamma makes the same point by inventory. "Decision" (*adhimokkha*) is a factor that arises together with a cognition rather than an act taken upon it; it is absent from bare sense-consciousness and from the one consciousness "accompanied by 'Doubt'" (*Abhidhammattha-saṅgaha* II, Nārada's translation), so determinacy and doubt exclude each other as co-arising states, not as options. Both traditions supply the ordering the Cartesian picture relies on, comprehension first and determination second, and neither supplies the agent whose act the second stage would be.
