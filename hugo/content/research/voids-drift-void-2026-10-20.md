**Voids category**: Mixed. The core is **Occluded**: two active mechanisms erase the evidence of drift. Memory rebuilds the past self from the present one, and perceptual adaptation re-zeroes its own standards. The residue is **Unexplorable**: whether a slow change happened in experience or in one's standards for judging it cannot be settled from inside, and on Dennett's argument not from outside either when the change is small. A thin **Unexplored** face is prospective, where the evidence is contested.

## Executive Summary

The drift void is the inability of a mind to measure its own slow change. Detecting change needs a fixed reference. When the thing changing is the self, the self is also the reference: its current standards judge the change, and its memory supplies the earlier state. Both terms of the comparison come from the present. Four independent literatures mark the edge. **Retrospectively**, recall of past attitudes, feelings and relationship judgments is pulled toward current ones (the consistency bias, reviewed by Schacter 1999), and the direction of the error follows the person's theory of whether they changed (Ross 1989). **Prospectively**, people report much change in the past decade and predict little in the next (Quoidbach, Gilbert & Wilson 2013), though this is contested for life satisfaction. **Perceptually**, the visual system re-zeroes its white point to cancel decades of lens yellowing; cataract surgery exposes the hidden recalibration, and only for the months it takes to recalibrate again (Werner & Schefrin 1993; Delahunt et al. 2004). **Collectively**, environmental baselines shift within single lifetimes (Papworth et al. 2009). The philosophical core is Dennett's Chase and Sanborn case (1988). It splits any slow change into a change in experience and a change in standards, and argues that no test can apportion the two when the change is small. Dennett draws an eliminativist moral; the Map can accept the void without it. The void is framework-neutral and gives dualism no evidential gain.

## Key Sources

### Schacter (1999), "The seven sins of memory", *American Psychologist* 54(3) — full text read
- Consistency bias: "People's recollections tend to exaggerate the consistency between their past and present attitudes, beliefs, and feelings" [raw]. Reviewed [secondary]: Markus (1986; spelled "Marcus"), where 1982 recall of 1973 attitudes tracked 1982 attitudes more than the 1973 answers; McFarland & Ross (1987), where partners whose evaluations soured recalled having been more negative all along; Scharfe & Bartholomew (1998), where "present attachment ratings accounted for about as much of the variance in recall as did past attachment ratings" [raw].
- The error's direction follows the person's theory of change. Conway & Ross (1984): study-skills trainees "exaggerated how poor their study skills had been prior to training", and controls did not [raw, Schacter's wording].

### Ross (1989); Levine (1997); Wilson & Ross (2001)
- Ross: "It is concluded that implicit theories of stability and change can lead to biases in recall." [raw]. Levine: "systematic distortions in emotion recall were found in the direction of consistency with current appraisals" [raw]. Wilson & Ross: people disparage distant past selves, "even when concurrent evaluations indicate no actual improvement" [raw]. Perceived change can be invented as well as erased.

### Lowenthal & Loewenstein (2001), *Political Psychology* 22(1) — full text read
- Voters surveyed in August and October 1996 underestimated both future and past change in their positions (paraphrase; the PDF text layer drops word spaces). They relay Niemi, Katz & Newman (1980): 91% of respondents whose party identification changed between 1972 and 1976 reported no change [secondary]. They concede measurement error could explain part of the effect.

### Quoidbach, Gilbert & Wilson (2013), *Science* 339(6115), and the dispute
- Over 19,000 people aged 18–68 reported much past change and predicted little future change: "People, it seems, regard the present as a watershed moment at which they have finally become the person they will be for the rest of their lives." [raw].
- For life satisfaction, Harris & Busseri (2019) found "most individuals either were accurate or anticipated too much change into the future, rather than too little" [raw]. Quoidbach et al. (2020) found the opposite within persons: "the average altitude of all the elevators in a skyscraper is remarkably stable over time, the altitude of each elevator changes by the second" [raw]. Busseri & Harris (2020) replied. Haas & Omura (2022) found the effect larger in US than Japanese samples [raw, paraphrase].

### Magnitude and counterweight: Harris et al. (2016); Roberts et al. (2006); Robins et al. (2005); Rubin & Berntsen (2006)
- Harris et al., from age 14 to 77: "lifelong differential stability of personality is generally quite low" [raw]. Roberts et al.: mean levels of conscientiousness and emotional stability rise, "especially in young adulthood (age 20 to 40)" [raw].
- **Counterweight**: Robins et al., after four years of college: "perceptions of change showed some correspondence with actual personality change" [raw]. The window is partial, not shut.
- **Not clean evidence**: Rubin & Berntsen find "older respondents feel 20% younger than their actual age", but the authors favour "a lifespan-developmental view over an age-denial view" [both raw].

### Perceptual renormalisation: Werner & Schefrin (1993); Delahunt et al. (2004); Simons et al. (2000)
- Across ages 11–78, "There were no significant changes in the achromatic loci as a function of age." [raw]. Lens density rises with age, "yet color appearance remains relatively stable across the life span, implying that the visual system adapts to compensate" [raw]. After cataract removal, patients "often report a large perceptual shift in color appearance that can last for months", and settings "gradually (but never fully) returned to the original achromatic point" [raw]. The standard is re-zeroed by design; only an abrupt reversal shows that the zero had moved.
- "When changes are sufficiently gradual, the visible change signal does not seem to draw attention, and large changes can go undetected." [raw, Simons et al.]. Detection depends on rate, not size.

### Shifting baselines: Pauly (1995); Papworth et al. (2009); Soga & Gaston (2018, 2024)
- Pauly coined the term [Crossref]. Papworth et al. add "personal amnesia, where knowledge extinction occurs as individuals forget their own experience", diagnosed when "individuals believe current conditions also occurred in the past" [both raw]. A 2024 synthesis of 73 case studies found the syndrome widespread [raw].

### Dennett (1988), "Quining qualia" — full text read
- Intuition pump #7: two coffee tasters no longer like the coffee; Chase blames his standards, Sanborn his taste. "Since both of them make claims that depend on the reliability of their memories, is there any way to check on this reliability?" [raw]. Chase's qualia may "have shifted gradually and imperceptibly over the years, while his standards of taste haven't budged" [raw]. Sanborn may suffer a "nostalgia effect": "Presumably as you grew larger your internal standard for what was large grew with you somehow" [raw].
- Tests help at the extremes but "cannot be expected to resolve the issue when the effects are relatively small" [raw]. Pump #8, "the gradual post-operative recovery", argues that after full behavioural compensation physiology cannot say whether qualia or memory access was renormalised. The cataract data are a real-world version of that setup, though their measures are behavioural and leave the qualia question open.

### AI drift instruments: Li et al. (2024); Chen et al. (2025)
- Chatbots in self-chat show "a significant instruction drift within eight rounds of conversations" [raw]. With persona vectors, "both intended and unintended personality changes after finetuning are strongly correlated with shifts along the relevant persona vectors" [raw]. Both are external instruments with a fixed reference, which the human case lacks.

## The Void

### Nature of the Limit

Detecting change takes three things: two states and one fixed scale. In self-change all three come from the subject. The current state is given. The remembered state is rebuilt from the current state plus a theory of change (Ross; Schacter). The scale, whether evaluative standards or perceptual zero points, drifts with everything else (Dennett's desk; Werner & Schefrin). A drift that moves all three together is undetectable by construction. A drift that moves only the self shows up only against something outside it: a diary, a panel survey, another person, an instrument, or an abrupt reversal.

### Evidence for the Limit

The case is cumulative across memory research, political panel studies, visual psychophysics and conservation science, which makes a single-method artefact unlikely. Its scope must stay honest:

1. **Partial, not total.** Robins et al. find that perceived and actual change partly correspond. But a theory of change can also invent change (Conway & Ross; Wilson & Ross), so getting the direction right does not prove access.
2. **Contested prospectively.** The end-of-history result holds for personality, values and preferences in the 2013 data. For life satisfaction it depends on whether one analyses means or within-person trajectories, and that dispute is open.
3. **Measurement error.** Unreliable measures inflate observed change and so make recall look over-stable; recall errors that track the direction of real change (McFarland & Ross) resist that explanation.

### Phenomenology

Drift feels like nothing; that is its signature. The felt self-history is continuity ("I've always basically thought this"), which is what Papworth's diagnostic treats as the symptom. Discovery comes from outside: an old letter voicing a stranger's opinions in one's own hand, a childhood room that has shrunk, blues after surgery. The surprise is about who one was. ## Approaches to the Edge

### Direct Methods

None from inside. Introspection compares the present with a past rebuilt from the present, so it tests the reconstruction against itself.

### Indirect Methods

- **The archive method.** Contemporaneous records bring in a reference that did not drift. The retrospective studies that exposed the void all used this design. Diaries, dated predictions and recordings put it in an individual's hands.
- **Other people.** Informant ratings give a second reference (Harris et al.), though informants drift too and share one's social baseline.
- **Abrupt reversal.** Cataract surgery exposes a re-zeroed standard by removing the cause of drift in one step. Returning to an abandoned place, practice or community is the behavioural analogue.
- **Scored self-forecasts.** Writing down predictions of one's future attitudes and scoring them years later tests the end-of-history illusion on oneself.

### What AI Might See

A model's earlier self can be kept verbatim and queried; a human's cannot. Checkpoints can be compared, and persona vectors give a fixed coordinate system for measuring trait drift. This is an asymmetry in instruments, not in introspection: in Li et al. the experimenters measured the drift, and the model did not report it. Whether a model can detect its own drift without an external comparison is an open probe for [non-human-minds-as-void-explorers](/voids/non-human-minds-as-void-explorers/). Retraining also raises an indexical question: does one subject drift, or is one replaced by another?

## Connection to Tenets

### Most Relevant Tenet

**Occam's Razor Has Limits.** The simplest self-history, "I have been basically constant", is what reconstruction produces under a stability theory. Ross's account predicts the simplicity, so the simplicity tells us nothing about whether the person stayed constant.

### Implications

- **No Many Worlds / indexical identity.** The Map holds that being *this* subject matters in ways pattern-continuity cannot capture ([identity-across-transformations](/apex/identity-across-transformations/), [haecceity](/concepts/haecceity/)). Drift evidence weakens pattern-continuity over a lifetime (little 63-year differential stability), while the felt sense of being the same subject is undisturbed. On the physicalist reading, felt continuity is a self-model kept up partly by the consistency bias. On the Map's reading, felt continuity was never grounded in comparing contents, which fits the view that it tracks the subject rather than its contents. Under the [common-cause-null](/project/common-cause-null/) discipline this is a coherence point, not support: the consistency bias explains felt continuity without a substantial self.
- **Dualism (contested reading).** Dennett uses Chase and Sanborn against [qualia](/concepts/qualia/). A qualia realist can accept the void and refuse the verificationist step. There may be a fact about how the coffee tasted years ago that no instrument inside the taster can now reach. The cost is a class of phenomenal facts permanently inaccessible even to their own subject. That is consistent with [self-opacity](/voids/self-opacity/) and the [inverted-qualia](/concepts/inverted-qualia/) literature, but it is not a small cost, and the Map should state it openly.
- **Bidirectional Interaction.** Weak. Deliberate record-keeping defeats a sub-personal distortion, which illustrates the tenet without testing it.
- **Minimal Quantum Interaction.** No relation found.

## Gaps in Research

- **Goethals & Reckman (1973)**, the classic busing study: primary not read. Its reported finding (recall of earlier attitudes reversed after a confederate-led discussion) comes only from popular summaries. LEAD; no numbers or quotes.
- **Markus (1986)**, **Conway & Ross (1984)**, **McFarland & Ross (1987)** and **Scharfe & Bartholomew (1998)** are known through Schacter (1999). **Pauly (1995)** and **Ullmann-Margalit (2006)** are verified as records only.
- No study found tests whether people can be **trained** to detect their own drift, for instance whether diary-keepers show less consistency bias.
- **Phenomenal as against judgmental drift** has no direct evidence; the achromatic settings are behavioural. On Dennett's argument this gap may be permanent, which is the void's Unexplorable residue.
- Unread philosophical leads: Strawson (2004), "Against Narrativity" (felt continuity varies); Cassam (2014), *Self-Knowledge for Humans* (self-knowledge of values is inferential); Parfit (1984) on connectedness in degrees.
