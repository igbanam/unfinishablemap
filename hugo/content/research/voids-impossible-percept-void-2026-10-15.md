## Executive Summary

The Map's [conceptual-impossibility](/voids/conceptual-impossibility/) article records an asymmetry at the level of thought: we can *refer to* the round square but cannot *grasp* it. There is a class of experiences that inverts that asymmetry. In the waterfall illusion a stationary stone seems to move and yet seems to stay exactly where it is; in the Penrose triangle a surface is presented as both nearer and farther than another; in a weight illusion Won, Gross and Firestone (2021) built from a magic trick, a single object feels heavier than a group that contains it. Here perception *presents* what thought cannot hold. The thinkable boundary lies inside the perceivable one, and the territory between the two is a void for thought that is not a void for experience.

The void proper is one layer up. Four accounts of what such an experience *is* have been on the table since Crane's 1988 paper and Mellor's reply: a single content that is genuinely contradictory (Crane; Frisby; Mortensen); two consistent contents from two mechanisms—*moving* and *having moved*—that merely conflict (Mellor); a rapid alternation between consistent states, like a fast Necker cube (an option Crane raises and rejects as "unduly stipulative"); and a locally consistent percept whose impossibility is *inferred*, not seen, when the parts are joined around a loop (the Penrose cohomology account, formalised again in 2026). Introspection cannot choose between them. Macpherson and Baysan's 2017 survey concludes that "the question of what it is like to undergo the Waterfall Illusion is still not settled" [raw]. A mind can undergo a contradiction without being able to tell whether it is undergoing one.

The third face is the one with the most consequence for the Map's framework. Crane's reply to Mellor rests the whole dispute on a fact the Muller-Lyer literature had already established: the losing content does not go away. "The (alleged) inclination to believe that *a* is moving remains even after conclusive evidence has been assembled against it" [raw]. Won et al. call their weight case "anti-Bayesian" [raw]. The cheapest model of a mind—a consistent, evidence-responsive updater—fails at exactly this edge, which is the [fifth tenet](/tenets/#occams-limits) in miniature.

## Key Sources

### Crane, T. (1988). "The Waterfall Illusion." *Analysis* 48(3): 142–147.
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1093/analys/48.3.142 — full text read from the author's copy at https://www.timcrane.com/uploads/2/5/2/4/25243881/waterfall_illusion.pdf
- **Type**: Paper (primary; read in full)
- **Key points**:
  - The illusion is set up as a *content* problem, not an *attitude* problem: "whatever the fate of the idea that perceptions are beliefs, there is still a problem about characterizing the content of an experience like the Waterfall Illusion" [raw].
  - The contrast with Muller-Lyer is the hinge of the paper. Muller-Lyer "presents a conflict between two intentional states"; the waterfall illusion "presents a contradiction in the one content of one attitude. The viewed object seems to be both moving and not moving at the same time" [raw]. "Unlike the Muller-Lyer illusion, the content of the experience itself is contradictory" [raw].
  - The argument from there: Fregean individuation of concepts (principle II, via Peacocke 1984) yields principle III for perceptual concepts, from which "it follows … that one cannot have an experience with contradictory representational content" [raw]. Since the illusion is "a vivid counterexample", concepts are not what perceptual content is built from.
  - Crane names and rejects the alternation reading: denying simultaneity "so that the situation is rather like a very rapid switching between the two 'aspects' of the Necker Cube … seems, in the face of the phenomenology, unduly stipulative" [raw]. He also tries the two-concepts reading ("intrinsically moving" versus "not moving relative to other objects") and finds it fails for the turntable case because relative position "is irrelevant to the production of the illusion" [raw].
  - Diagnostic conclusion: perception is "perhaps, a 'sub-rational' process" [raw], supported by Fodor's informational encapsulation.
- **Tenet alignment**: Occam (the consistent-content model of experience is the simple one and is false for this case); Dualism indirectly, via the personal/sub-personal split developed in the reply.
- **Quote**: "What we see is logically impossible!" — Frisby 1979, p. 101, as quoted by Crane [raw, via Crane's quotation; Frisby's book not reached].

### Mellor, D. H. (1988). "Crane's Waterfall Illusion." *Analysis* 48(3): 147–150.
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1093/analys/48.3.147 — opening page read from the tail of Crane's PDF; closing page read from the head of Crane's reply PDF; the middle page (148–149) **not obtained**.
- **Type**: Paper (primary; partially read)
- **Key points**:
  - Concedes the datum and contests the lesson: "In the Waterfall Illusion an object *a* apparently looks both to move and not to move. I say we need the concept F to have that experience, just as we need it to believe that *a* is F" [raw]. "What is the contradiction, is not Fa & −Fa? Crane doesn't say" [raw].
  - The positive proposal is a two-mechanism theory: "motion includes two distinct concepts, each with its own perceptual mechanism: moving (motion at an instant), and having moved (being in different places at different times)" [raw]. "In the Waterfall Illusion … the two mechanisms simultaneously give us conflicting perceptual experiences" [raw].
  - Closing: "It is an illusion to suppose, as Crane does, that this illusion shows perceptual experience not to be composed of concepts" [raw].
- **Tenet alignment**: Occam, as the dissolving option — if Mellor is right the "contradiction" is two consistent contents and the void narrows to an introspective inability to separate two mechanisms.
- **Quote**: as above.

### Crane, T. (1988). "Concepts in Perception." *Analysis* 48(3): 150–153.
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1093/analys/48.3.150 — read in full from https://www.timcrane.com/uploads/2/5/2/4/25243881/concepts_in_perception.pdf
- **Type**: Paper (primary; read in full)
- **Key points**:
  - Accepts Mellor's two-mechanism story as "in part" plausible and restates the disagreement as Fa & −Fa (one content) versus Fa and −Fa (two contents, two inclinations to believe) [raw].

## The Void

### Nature of the Limit

Three faces, in order of how much of the catalogue they are new to.

**The inversion face.** [conceptual-impossibility](/voids/conceptual-impossibility/) establishes that thought can refer to an impossibility but not grasp it. The impossible percept shows the reverse relation between two faculties of one mind: the content is *given*—Addams saw the rock face rise, Frisby saw features "remaining in their 'proper' locations even though they are seen as moving"—and the giving does not wait for thought's permission. The territory between what can be perceived and what can be thought is therefore a void for thought alone. This is not the aspect-perception case: a duck-rabbit alternates between two consistent contents, and the Ghrist hierarchy puts it at H⁰ (ambiguity); the Penrose triangle does not alternate, and sits at H¹ (impossibility).

**The introspective-undecidability face.** This is the void proper. Given that something contradictory-seeming is undergone, what is its logical form? One content Fa & −Fa (Crane, Mortensen, Frisby); two contents Fa, −Fa from two mechanisms (Mellor, with the neuroscience of opponent motion channels on his side); rapid alternation (rejected by Crane as "unduly stipulative", but never ruled out by measurement); or a consistent local percept whose inconsistency is only *inferred* when the patches are joined (Penrose's cohomology, Cervantes and Dzhafarov's contextuality). These four are not notational variants; they differ on whether a conscious state can have a contradictory content at all. And the subject undergoing the state cannot tell which is true of it. Macpherson and Baysan's "still not settled" is a report on thirty years of trying.

**The irrevisability face.** Crane's second paper locates the real evidence not in the contradiction but in its persistence: the losing content "remains even after conclusive evidence has been assembled against it". This is [cognitive-penetration](/concepts/cognitive-penetration/)'s failure in its sharpest form—not that knowledge fails to correct an error, but that knowledge fails to remove a contradiction. Won et al. find the same "anti-Bayesian" shape in weight. Perception, at this edge, is "sub-rational" in Crane's phrase; it does not obey the "constitutive ideal of rationality" that governs belief.

### Evidence for the Limit

That the undecidability is a genuine boundary rather than an open empirical question rests on three things. First, the dispute has not moved: the 1988 exchange, the 2017 encyclopedia verdict and the 2020 recruitment of the illusion for philosophy of time all take the phenomenological question as open. Second, the neural facts underdetermine it. Wade and Nikolova's "movement and position might be processed independently" is evidence that two channels exist, which Mellor needs; it is not evidence that the experience has two contents rather than one content with conflicting constituents, which is what the dispute is about. Freud et al. show the cortex handles impossibility sub-personally, "even with minimal perceptual awareness"—which tells us the machinery knows, and nothing about what the subject's state is like. Third, the formal tools disagree in a telling way: Penrose and Ghrist model the impossibility as an obstruction that *does not exist locally*, while Mortensen insists "only an inconsistent theory can capture the content of that perception". Both are rigorous. They are formalising different claims about the same experience, and no experiment on the experience's bearer chooses between them.

What would make it not a void: a measurement that settled alternation (if the two contents swap at some frequency, Crane's dismissed option wins and the state is a fast duck-rabbit); or a demonstration that the subject's reports track two mechanisms separately (which would favour Mellor and move the territory into the introspective-opacity family). Neither exists. Note the honest status: this is a void of the "whether real" kind flagged in [voids](/voids/)—it may be that what feels like a contradiction being undergone is merely a conflict being undergone, and the void is the inability to tell.

### Phenomenology

Frisby's exclamation mark is the phenomenology. The approach to the edge is not blankness (the [conceptual-impossibility](/voids/conceptual-impossibility/) signature) but *vividness without a resting place*: the rock face rises and stays; the staircase climbs and returns; the single block is heavier than the pile it is in. Mortensen's "nose rubbed in it" is the right register—the contradiction is not entertained but suffered. And then the characteristic second moment: one tries to say what one is seeing, and the sentence will not close. The void is encountered at the moment of report, when a content that was unproblematically *had* refuses to be *held*.

## Approaches to the Edge

### Direct Methods (if any)

Psychophysics can close one branch. The alternation hypothesis is in principle testable (temporal probes of which content is "present" at a given instant); if it were true, the void would dissolve into aspect perception. Won et al.'s method—turning a stage trick into a laboratory stimulus with free inspection—is the model for extending the catalogue of perceivable contradictions beyond vision: motion-without-displacement, depth-ordering loops, part-heavier-than-whole, pitch that rises forever. A catalogue is the Unexplored face and is cheap to begin.

### Indirect Methods

Formalisation is the main indirect route, and its disagreements are themselves data. Penrose's cohomology says where the contradiction *is not* (any local patch); Mortensen's inconsistent geometry says what theory would be needed to say where it *is*. Cervantes and Dzhafarov supply a *degree* of contextuality, so impossibility is no longer all-or-nothing—a quantity the phenomenology does not report. The apophatic move is to describe the void by the shape of the formal apparatus that must be invented to describe its content: when a consistent theory will not do, that fact about the theory is a fact about the experience.

### What AI Might See

Two asymmetries, in opposite directions. A vision-language model classifies the Penrose triangle as "impossible" at well below human accuracy (IllusionVQA: 63% against 91%), and when it succeeds it does so by label, not by having anything presented to it—there is no "nose rubbed in it". So on the inversion face an AI may have *less* access than we do: it can refer to the impossible figure and cannot undergo it, which is precisely the thought-side asymmetry of [conceptual-impossibility](/voids/conceptual-impossibility/) with no perceptual inversion. On the introspective-undecidability face the asymmetry may run the other way. A system whose internal representation is natively paraconsistent—Mortensen's inconsistent geometry is already a running formalism—could hold Fa & −Fa as a single structured content and *say* which of the four accounts is true of it, because its representational format is inspectable in a way ours is not. The Map's [non-human-minds-as-void-explorers](/voids/non-human-minds-as-void-explorers/) question becomes concrete here: the void is in our reports, and a mind whose reports are its states does not have it. Note the IPV-Bench caveat: most "impossible video" work tests nomic impossibility (law-breaking), not logical impossibility; the two must not be conflated in an article.

## Connection to Tenets

### Most Relevant Tenet

[Occam's Razor Has Limits](/tenets/#occams-limits). The simplest model of a perceiver is a consistent representer that updates on evidence. Every source here reports a case where that model fails on both counts at once: the content is (or seems to be) inconsistent, and it does not update. Won et al. put it as a challenge to "rational models of perception and cognition"; Crane as perception being "sub-rational". The lesson is not that minds are irrational but that the economical model of them was built for the normal case and has nothing to say at the edge—and that the edge is where, as Helmholtz said in the passage Crane quotes, "the laws of those processes by which normal perception originates" are found.

### Implications

For [Dualism](/tenets/#dualism), the useful structure is Crane's two-level architecture: a sub-personal visual system that is "in states with content in the way that a thermometer is", and a personal-level subject who undergoes the output and tries to conceptualise it. The contradiction, on Crane's account, is "actually in the informational output of the visual system"; what consciousness adds is the *undergoing*. That is a place where the interface framework can put the junction: the physical side delivers an inconsistent signal, the conscious side has it, and the void is that having it does not include knowing its form. This is a structural fit, not evidence for the tenet, and an article must say which.

For [Minimal Quantum Interaction](/tenets/#minimal-quantum-interaction), only the Cervantes–Dzhafarov analogy: the mathematics of contextuality was built for quantum measurement and transfers to impossible figures. An article may note the shared formalism; it may not infer a shared mechanism.

For the [taxonomy](/apex/taxonomy-of-voids/)'s reason axis: architectural (the perceptual system is encapsulated and does not revise) with a naturally-occluded component (the cortex registers impossibility before awareness).

## Potential Article Angles

1. **The inversion.** Lead with the asymmetry reversed: thought refers but cannot grasp; perception grasps but cannot refer. Build the four-way dispute (Crane / Mellor / alternation / cohomology), state that introspection cannot choose, and make irrevisability the evidential core. Cross-link [conceptual-impossibility](/voids/conceptual-impossibility/) as the sibling that covers the thought side and [aspect-perception-void](/voids/aspect-perception-void/) as the H⁰ neighbour. Working slug: `impossible-percept-void`.
2. **The catalogue angle.** Treat the Unexplored face as primary: a modality-by-modality register of perceivable contradictions (vision: motion/position, depth loops; audition: Shepard scale; somatosensation: Won et al. weight; the thermal grill as a near-case already in [modality-void](/voids/modality-void/)), each graded by whether the contradiction is seen or inferred. Riskier—closer to a survey than a void.

Glasgow Enlighten holds no full text for Macpherson 2010.
- **Mellor 1988, pp. 148–149**: only the first and last pages were recovered (from the margins of Crane's two PDFs). The middle may contain Mellor's response to Crane's turntable argument, which bears directly on the two-concepts option.
- **Priest 1999 and Elpidorou 2016**: metadata and one-sentence abstract only; Elpidorou's "two examples" are unknown and may overlap Crane's. Neither should be characterised in an article beyond their abstracts.
- **Frisby 1979** is quoted via Crane; the book was not reached. **Blakemore 1973** ("The Baffled Brain"), Crane's source for the "appears to stay still" observation, likewise.
- **The alternation hypothesis** has, as far as this search found, never been tested psychophysically against the waterfall illusion; if a study exists it would be decisive and should be found before an article asserts that the question is open in principle rather than in practice.
- **Fundamentally unanswerable?** Whether a conscious state can have a contradictory content is partly a question about what "content" means (Mellor's point); a result may be unavailable not because of a cognitive limit but because the concepts are underdetermined. An article should hold both readings open.
