Charles Bonnet syndrome (CBS) is the occurrence of complex visual hallucinations—figures, faces, patterns, whole scenes—in people who have lost sight through damage to the eye or the visual pathway, who retain insight that the images are unreal, and whose cognition is otherwise intact. A 2022 meta-analysis puts pooled prevalence in low-vision populations at about one in five (Subhi et al., 2022). Every account in the clinical and computational literature agrees on one point: the content is learned, stereotyped and non-corrective. Deafferented visual cortex emits what it already knows how to build; it does not reconstruct the scene the eyes no longer deliver. That agreed point is the one that matters for this Map, because several Map articles have described the syndrome as *compensation*—the interface "filling gaps" when input drops—and that reading has no support in the literature. This article defines the syndrome, sets out the three live readings of its mechanism, names what they leave open, and states plainly that the Map retires the compensation reading (see [the reading the Map retires](#retired), below). The syndrome is evidentially neutral between the Map's filter view of perception and the production view it opposes, and nothing here claims otherwise.

## What the Syndrome Is

Charles Bonnet described the founding case in 1760: his grandfather Charles Lullin, near-blind after cataract surgery, saw silent men, women, birds and buildings that he knew to be fictions. Georges de Morsier attached Bonnet's name to the syndrome in 1936 (ffytche, 2007). In ffytche's later taxonomy there are at least two hallucination syndromes—one tied to visual-system pathology, the other to brainstem or neuromodulatory disturbance (ffytche, 2005)—and the afferent form is what this article means by CBS.

Teunisse et al. (1996), screening 505 visually handicapped patients in a clinical series, found 60 who met the criteria. Although the criteria demanded only partial insight, "all patients had full insight into the unreal nature of their hallucinations"; in 77 per cent the hallucinations "lacked a personal meaning"; and "sensory deprivation and a low level of arousal seemed to favour the occurrence of hallucinations". Only one of the sixteen who had consulted a doctor had been correctly diagnosed. Menon et al. (2003), reviewing the clinical literature, describe "well-defined, organized, and clear images over which the subject has little control" and call the result "a form of phantom vision".

The content is not random. Santhouse, Howard and ffytche (2000), applying exploratory factor analysis to 28 pathological visual experiences across 34 patients, found three segregated clusters: "extended landscape scenes and small figures in costumes with hats"; "grotesque, disembodied and distorted faces with prominent eyes and teeth"; and "visual perseveration and delayed palinopsia". The clusters "mirror the segregation of hierarchical visual pathways into streams". A patient who has never met a costumed figure sees one anyway. The content tracks cortical architecture rather than the missing scene; this is the single strongest datum against any gap-filling reading, and the definition above rests on it.

## Three Readings of the Mechanism

Three readings compete at the level of mechanism. They share the verdict that the content is learned and non-corrective, and differ on what drives its release.

**Release through deafferentation.** The classic clinical reading, established by Cogan (1973) and given its phantom-limb analogy by Schultz and Melzack (1991), holds that loss of afferent input disinhibits or hypersensitises visual cortex, which then emits content shaped by its own specialisations. ffytche et al. (1998), in an fMRI study of hallucinating patients, found that "the content of the hallucinations reflects the functional specializations of the region" of ventral extrastriate cortex involved, and that elevated activity "persists between hallucinations". Painter et al. (2018) called cortical hyperexcitability "an influential but untested hypothesis" and tested it with steady-state visual evoked potentials in macular-degeneration patients: those with hallucinations "showed strikingly elevated visual cortical responses to peripheral field stimulation" compared with patients without hallucinations and with controls. They group CBS with tinnitus and phantom-limb pain as maladaptive plasticity after peripheral damage. daSilva Morgan et al. (2025), combining fMRI, EEG and TMS in 19 people with CBS against 18 eye-disease controls matched for age and acuity, report reduced stimulus-evoked activation, occipital slowing and lowered phosphene thresholds, "consistent with the deafferentation hypothesis". Note that this abstract speaks of "spontaneous, compensatory, neural activity": the compensation is a recovery of firing rate in neurons, and it is precisely what generates the non-corrective hallucinations.

**Generative-model release.** Reichert, Seriès and Storkey (2013) supply a computational mechanism: a deep Boltzmann machine with homeostatic regulation of neuronal activity. Their abstract reads CBS "as indication that there is a generative model in the brain, specifically one that can synthesise rich, consistent visual representations even in the absence of actual visual input", and shows that homeostatic plasticity, which keeps the learned model robust against degraded input, will "overcompensate in the case of CBS, leading to hallucinations". This is a simulation in one architecture, and its decisive assumption is that homeostasis acts on a learned hierarchical model whose upper layers hold the content. The paper sharpens the release reading rather than replacing it: release acquires a mechanism, and the mechanism is a learned model over-correcting its own activity level.

**The strong-prior gloss.** Within predictive processing, Corlett et al. (2019) highlight prior beliefs as a critical elicitor of hallucination: strong, overly precise priors can produce hallucinations in healthy subjects, and on this reading deafferentation in CBS lowers the precision of sensory evidence until learned priors dominate. This is the source of the phrase "unchecked priors" that the Map's [perceptual-failure-and-the-interface](/topics/perceptual-failure-and-the-interface/) uses on the production theorist's behalf. The gloss leans on Reichert's model for its CBS case and adds the vocabulary of hierarchical inference.

All three readings locate the content in the brain's learned structure. None proposes that the hallucination restores, replaces or approximates the lost scene.

## What the Readings Leave Open

The field's own consensus statement lists "eight models of complex visual hallucinations" published since 2000 and integrates them into one framework rather than crowning a winner (Collerton et al., 2023). Four questions remain open, and a calibrated treatment names them without claiming them.

*Why only some patients.* Painter et al. (2018) flag the absence of any satisfactory explanation of "why some individuals develop hallucinations, while many do not"; most deafferented patients never hallucinate.

*Why now and not then.* Episodes favour darkness, fatigue and low arousal (Teunisse et al., 1996). Release explains that something is generated; it does not explain when.

*Why insight survives.* Schmid and Kriegleder (2024) argue from the CBS case that the strong-prior hypothesis "fails to fully account for the characteristics of nonveridical perceptual experiences associated with Charles-Bonnet-Syndrome" and that the current predictive-processing account "omits essential characteristics of stimulus-independent perception"; their proposed repair adds reality monitoring. Corlett et al. (2019) hold that interactions within and between hierarchies of inference can reconcile apparent disconnects of this kind. The dispute is open.

*Bottom-up or top-down.* Hahamy et al. (2021), in fMRI of five CBS participants, found "a slow build-up of neural activity prior to the reported onset of hallucinations" that was "most pronounced in early visual cortex and then decayed along the visual hierarchy", and suggest that spontaneous fluctuations in early cortex "may activate the visual hierarchy, thereby triggering the experience of vision". A bottom-up ignition sits awkwardly with a purely top-down priors story. Altieri and Battaglini (2026) review the evidence as pointing toward desynchronisation between bottom-up and top-down pathways rather than hyperexcitability alone, with deafferentation a permissive condition rather than a sufficient one. Collerton, Perry and McKeith (2005) had earlier argued that "cortical release" and its rivals do not "fully account for the phenomenology" and proposed an attentional-binding deficit instead.

None of these gaps is a gap for dualism to fill. They are questions about which cortical dynamics gate a cortical process.

## The Reading the Map Retires {#retired}

Until this article was written, several Map articles described CBS as compensation: the interface "fills gaps with internally generated content", "actively maintains a phenomenal field", or generates "compensatory imagery" ([perceptual-failure-and-the-interface](/topics/perceptual-failure-and-the-interface/), [dualist-perception](/topics/dualist-perception/), [capability-division-problem](/voids/capability-division-problem/); the research note behind this article lists each locus as it stood). No external source supports that wording. The reading predicts content that tends toward the missing scene—a reconstruction, however rough, of the room one can no longer see. The observed content is stereotyped across patients, often novel to the individual, recognised as unreal, worse in darkness and low arousal, and organised by cortical stream rather than by what was lost. ffytche et al. (1998), which [capability-division-problem](/voids/capability-division-problem/) had cited for "compensatory imagery", reports content-specific extrastriate activation and says nothing about compensation.

The error trades on an equivocation. *Neuronal* compensation—homeostatic recovery of firing rate after input is lost—is affirmed by the literature (daSilva Morgan et al., 2025; Reichert et al., 2013). *Phenomenal* compensation—restoring or filling in the lost scene—is denied by it. The Map retires the second sense. Where the word survives in a Map article it means the first, and the first is what produces non-corrective hallucinations rather than preventing them. [lucid-dreaming-and-dualist-rendering](/topics/lucid-dreaming-and-dualist-rendering/) already carries the corrected register—release of learned, non-veridical content, bizarre rather than compensatory—and [hypnagogic-phenomenology-and-interface-modulation](/topics/hypnagogic-phenomenology-and-interface-modulation/) already concedes that the phenomenological data cannot adjudicate between filter and disinhibition. The other loci now converge on that wording.

Retiring the reading also removes an argument. [perceptual-failure-and-the-interface](/topics/perceptual-failure-and-the-interface/) used CBS and eigengrau to support Bidirectional Interaction: content generated when signal drops was said to imply that consciousness, or the interface itself, actively maintains a phenomenal field. On every reading above the deafferented cortex supplies the content, so the inference does not go through, and the Map withdraws it. The behavioural half of that tenet paragraph—squinting and reaching for glasses as the subject acting on the interface's self-report—is untouched by anything here.

## Relation to Site Perspective

What follows is the Map's interpretation, stated as the Map's; the sources above make no claims about experience or about dualism.

**Dualism (Tenet 1).** The syndrome is neutral. A release account says which cortical regions generate which content; it does not say why that generation is experienced, and the Map's standing argument on that point is made in [predictive-processing-and-dualism](/topics/predictive-processing-and-dualism/) for the generative model in general. CBS adds no new leverage in either direction, and the Map should not pretend it does. In particular, the Map's [filter tradition](/concepts/filter-theory/) has no stake in compensation. Filter accounts are themselves release-shaped: vivid endogenous experience appears when a constraint relaxes. A filter theorist can accept release outright. What the filter theorist gives up is any claim that CBS content is mind-side—the content tracks cortical specialisation, so the brain supplies it on either metaphysics.

**Bidirectional Interaction (Tenet 3).** As set out above, the Map withdraws its use of CBS as evidence for two-way traffic across the interface. Release phenomena are, on their face, one-way: cortex to experience.

**Occam's Razor Has Limits (Tenet 5).** The release reading is the economical one and the Map endorses it. Tenet 5 holds that parsimony is unreliable where knowledge is incomplete; it does not license resisting a simple account where nothing is incomplete in the relevant respect. The content of a Charles Bonnet hallucination is explained by the architecture that generates it, and the Map has no reason to multiply explanations.

**One modest contribution.** The following is the Map's own synthesis and was not found treated head-on in the sources. Insight is a phenomenal fact: the hallucination presents as unreal while remaining vivid, detailed and beyond the subject's control. Dreams are endogenous vision too, and the standard contrast is that the dreamer does not recognise the dream as unreal from inside, while the Charles Bonnet patient does ([dream-consciousness](/topics/dream-consciousness/)). A computational account that adds reality monitoring, as Schmid and Kriegleder propose, describes this difference as a further process; it does not derive what it is like for the same cortex's output to arrive once labelled and once unlabelled. That is the structural point the Map makes about generative models generally, applied here. It is a point about description, and it is not evidence for any tenet.
