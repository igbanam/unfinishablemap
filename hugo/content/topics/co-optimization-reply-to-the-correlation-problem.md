The correlation problem asks the epiphenomenalist a hard question: if conscious experience causes nothing, why does it track biological significance so reliably? Pleasures accompany fitness-promoting behaviours; pains accompany fitness-threatening ones, across species and sensory modalities. The interactionist explains the correlation causally—pleasure promotes the behaviour it accompanies *because* it drives approach. The sophisticated epiphenomenalist's best rejoinder is **co-optimization**: neural systems selected to produce adaptive behaviour also produce corresponding phenomenal states, not by coincidence but because the same optimization process shapes both at once. The Unfinishable Map holds that this reply is the strongest live epiphenomenalist answer to the correlation problem—and that the available clinical evidence *constrains* it without *discriminating* between it and interactionism. The dissociation cases tie the affective substrate to the protective-behaviour system but do not, on their own, settle that consciousness does causal work; on the present evidence the choice appears underdetermined by the data. The name also covers a constitutive-physicalist version, which is not epiphenomenalist and against which the Map's dispute is over Dualism rather than causal efficacy (explained below).

This article lays out the co-optimization model on its own terms, states the "why this specific pairing?" challenge, asks whether the model and interactionism diverge empirically, and only then turns to the Map's reading. The parent article, [the evolutionary case for mental causation](/topics/evolutionary-case-for-mental-causation/#the-correlation-problem), compresses this exchange into one paragraph; the aim here is to give the strongest epiphenomenalist move room to breathe before assessing it.

## The Co-Optimization Model

Co-optimization is a response to the [evolutionary argument](/topics/evolutionary-case-for-mental-causation/) that grants the argument's central premise. Yes, consciousness evolved. Yes, selection preserves traits by their effects. The epiphenomenalist's move is to relocate what selection acts on: selection shapes the neural machinery, and the phenomenal states are a structurally determined consequence of that machinery, not an independent target of selection.

The model has two components. The first is a claim about what evolution optimizes. Selection builds nervous systems that classify situations as to-be-pursued or to-be-avoided and that generate the corresponding behaviour. This much every party accepts.

The second component is the substantive epiphenomenalist claim: the *phenomenal character* of the states realizing these classifications is fixed by the same neural organization that fixes the behaviour. On this view there is a systematic mapping—call it a psychophysical regularity—from neural-functional organization to phenomenal quality. Whatever neural configuration implements "approach this," that configuration also happens to feel good; whatever implements "avoid this" happens to feel bad. Because selection shapes the functional organization, and the functional organization fixes the phenomenal character, the two are optimized together. The correlation between valence and fitness is then no more coincidental than the correlation between a key's shape and the lock it was filed to fit.

The strength of the reply is that it dissolves the appearance of cosmic coincidence without granting valence any causal role. The epiphenomenalist does not have to say that unpleasant-feeling and avoidance-promoting just *happened* to line up across billions of years. They line up because they are two outputs of one optimized system. This is a genuine advance over the cruder epiphenomenalism that treats the correlation as brute luck—the harmony that William James's objection says the epiphenomenalist must accept as brute (Robinson 2023, §2.2)—and it is why the Map treats co-optimization as the position to beat rather than a straw target. The reply descends from Jackson's (1982) claim that qualia are "a by-product of certain brain processes that are highly conducive to survival"; its leading defender is Robinson (2007), whose answer to James the next section takes up.

Two positions go under this name, and the verdicts below differ between them. The *property-dualist co-optimizer*, Robinson's position, holds phenomenal valence to be a non-physical property, fixed by the neural organization and causally inert: epiphenomenalism proper, which the Map's [epiphenomenalism](/concepts/epiphenomenalism/) page classes among the dualist positions. The *constitutive-physicalist co-optimizer*, developed in the predictive-processing account below, holds that felt awfulness *is* the computational role that drives avoidance, so the inverted organism of the next section is impossible for it by identity. The next section's trilemma is pressed against the first position only.

## The "Why This Specific Pairing?" Challenge

Co-optimization buys its explanatory power with a commitment it must then defend. It requires a *determinate* psychophysical regularity linking functional organization to phenomenal character—and it must explain why *this* regularity holds rather than another, while insisting that the phenomenal character it fixes does no causal work.

Make the challenge precise. The co-optimization story needs the neural system to pair beneficial behaviours with pleasant experiences and harmful ones with unpleasant experiences. But on the epiphenomenalist's own terms, pleasantness and unpleasantness are causally inert—they make no difference to what the organism does. So consider the inverted-valence scenario: an organism in which injury feels *pleasant* and feeding feels *painful*, yet which still avoids injury and seeks food, because the avoidance-and-approach machinery is wired exactly as in us and the phenomenal valence rides on top without touching behaviour. If valence is genuinely inert, this organism is behaviourally indistinguishable from a normal one and equally fit. Selection, which sees only behaviour, cannot prefer the normally-valenced organism to the inverted one. The co-optimization model therefore owes an account of *why* actual organisms exhibit the aligned pairing rather than an inverted or scrambled one.

There are three ways the epiphenomenalist can answer, and each carries a cost:

**Necessity.** The pairing is metaphysically or nomologically necessary: the very neural organization that implements avoidance *cannot but* feel bad, as a matter of psychophysical law. This blocks the inverted-valence scenario by declaring it impossible. The cost is that the necessity is asserted, not explained—it reintroduces a brute psychophysical regularity at the level the [hard problem](/topics/hard-problem-of-consciousness/) already flagged as resistant to physical explanation, and it makes the phenomenal character supervene so tightly on the functional that the position edges toward the constitutive rival's functionalism, for which there is no separable quale to be epiphenomenal about.

**Indifference.** The pairing is not necessary, and inverted-valence organisms are genuinely possible—we simply happen not to be them, and there is no fact about why. This preserves the inertness of valence honestly but abandons the explanatory ambition that made co-optimization attractive: it concedes that the alignment between valence and fitness is, after all, not explained by the optimization, only permitted by it.

**Reference-fixing.** The pairing follows from what valence terms pick out. This is Robinson's answer to James, whose objection is the inverted-valence challenge above (Robinson 2023, §2.2). The answer separates sensory-system events, which cause feelings, from reward-system events, which contribute to continuing, repeating or avoiding the behaviour that led to them. It then holds that "What 'pleasure' refers to in any possible world is the effect in consciousness of the events in (b) that contribute to continuance or repetition", (b) being the reward-system events. Both kinds of event affect behaviour, so selection can align them, and the alignment of feeling with utility follows. On this view the inverted organism is a misdescription: whatever accompanies its avoidance of injury is displeasure by the meaning of the word. No brute law is needed and no explanation is given up.

The cost, on the Map's reading, is that the move relocates the question without dissolving it. Reference-fixing makes valence a relational feature of an experience (being the conscious effect of reward-system events), not part of how the experience feels, so the inverted-valence question returns as "why does that effect feel like *this*?" A realist about felt valence cannot take the move without conceding the explanandum. This argument assumes that valence is intrinsic to felt character: on the Map's [value-in-experience reading](/topics/wanting-liking-and-the-value-in-mechanism-fork/#the-fork) value exists only *for* an experiencing subject, and [phenomenal value realism](/topics/phenomenal-value-realism/) holds that "the intrinsic badness of pain is constitutive of what pain feels like". An epiphenomenalist who denies this keeps Robinson's answer at full strength; the dispute is then about what valence is, and this argument does not settle it.

Co-optimization is coherent, but it must pay one of three prices: a brute, behaviour-irrelevant psychophysical law it cannot ground (necessity), the explanation it was introduced to provide (indifference), or a relational account of valence that a realist about felt valence cannot accept (reference-fixing). For such a realist this is a real trilemma, though not, by itself, evidence for interactionism—a point the next section makes good on.

## Where the Accounts Actually Diverge—If They Do

The honest question is whether co-optimization and interactionism make any *different empirical predictions at all*. If they do not, then the trilemma above is a theoretical-economy complaint, not an empirical defeat, and the choice between them turns on prior commitments rather than data.

The natural place to look for divergence is the dissociation cases, where phenomenal valence and the behaviour it normally accompanies come apart.

[Pain asymbolia](/concepts/pain-asymbolia/) dissociates nociceptive sensation from affective suffering. Following the sensory-limbic disconnection characterized by Berthier, Starkstein, and Leiguarda (1988), patients detect and localize noxious stimuli but lack the felt awfulness—and their protective behaviour is impaired; some smile when pricked. Congenital insensitivity to pain produces a different dissociation: loss-of-function mutations in *SCN9A*, which encodes the Nav1.7 sodium channel of nociceptive neurons, abolish nociceptive signalling at the periphery, and affected individuals suffer lip and tongue injuries, bruises, cuts, and fractures diagnosed only in retrospect (Cox et al., 2006). One case removes the felt badness while sensory detection survives; the other removes the signal before it can be felt at all.

Both cases show the same pattern at the level of behaviour: where the felt-bad layer is absent, protective behaviour degrades. The interactionist reads this as expected—valence was doing the work, so removing it removes the behaviour. But here is the decisive point for calibration: **the co-optimization epiphenomenalist predicts the identical behavioural outcome.** On the co-optimization story, the lesion or mutation disrupts the *neural-functional* organization, and because behaviour is fixed by that organization, behaviour degrades. The phenomenal change is a co-effect of the same disruption, not its cause. Both accounts predict: damage the affective-evaluation system, and avoidance fails. Neither the asymbolia data nor the congenital-insensitivity data, taken at face value, separates them.

A [predictive-processing](/concepts/predictive-processing/) framing sharpens why. On that account, affective valence enters as a hierarchical prior over the nociceptive signal, and insular/anterior-cingulate damage disrupts valence-prior *assignment*; the behavioural collapse follows because the prior drives avoidance-policy selection. This is a fully physicalist mechanism that produces the dissociation and the behavioural deficit without granting felt awfulness any causal role beyond its functional realization. It is the constitutive-physicalist co-optimizer's story, and for these cases it predicts what the epiphenomenalist version predicts.

So where, if anywhere, could the accounts diverge? Three candidate fault lines exist, and each is currently *open* rather than settled:

1. **Selective behavioural degradation tracking the phenomenal change.** If the loss of avoidance behaviour mapped onto the loss of felt badness *more tightly* than it mapped onto any independently specifiable functional disruption, that would favour the view that the felt layer is doing the work. But disentangling "loss of the feeling" from "loss of the function that realizes the feeling" requires a way to vary one while holding the other fixed—exactly what the dissociation cases do not cleanly provide, since the lesion disrupts both together.

2. **Felt-quality / functional-role mismatches.** Berridge's dissociation of "wanting" (dopaminergic) from "liking" (hedonic) gives the closest available tests, asking which of two physically separable variables selection-relevant behaviour follows; the [valence-and-selection article](/topics/valence-and-conscious-selection/#discriminating-evidence) develops them as the live route to resolving the internal value-blind/value-sensitive fork. Against co-optimization they meet fault line 1's limit. "Liking" is "the core process of hedonic pleasure", read from facial reactions rather than felt pleasure (Berridge 2009), so a co-optimizer can explain behaviour that follows [either variable](/topics/wanting-liking-and-the-value-in-mechanism-fork/). Only varying felt pleasure apart from both would test inert valence, and Berridge's own example of absent felt pleasure shows pursuit following "wanting": addicts work for more of a cocaine dose that "feels empty".

3. **The constitution-versus-doing-work bridge.** Against the constitutive physicalist, the Map's substantive disagreement does not locate at the dissociation. It locates at whether felt awfulness is *constituted by* the sub-personal valence-prior's computational role (predictive processing) or is a further, non-physical feeling that *adds causal work* the prior alone does not perform (the Map). Both sides hold that avoidance follows felt awfulness, so no behavioural measure separates them: on that point they agree by construction. Against the property-dualist epiphenomenalist the tie is only contingent. The interactionist explanation of the alignment needs a selector evolution can see, and by the Map's [selectability constraint](/topics/inheritance-problem-for-the-psychophysical-law/#the-selectability-constraint) such a selector "can be practically hidden but not hidden by construction", though the strictest reading of the Map's Born-preservation commitment forbids one. The in-principle discriminator is then physical, not behavioural: outcome frequencies that, conditioned on felt valence, depart from what the physical state alone assigns. Like the intention-conditioned test in [P-Q9](/positions/quantum-interface/#p-q9), it has not been run at a brain-internal grain. Such a departure would also break the physical closure the constitutive rival assumes, but as part of the Map's general dispute with physicalism.

The honest verdict is therefore the one the parent article already reaches: the dissociation cases **constrain but do not discriminate**. They show that the affective substrate is tied to the protective-behaviour system, a constraint every surviving story meets, as does brute-luck epiphenomenalism, whose lucky law also makes feeling vary with the brain; the correlation itself, not the lesions, tells against that crudest version. The Berthier patients' deficit extended beyond pain to threatening gestures and verbal menaces, so asymbolia does not isolate felt pain-badness ([pain-asymbolia](/concepts/pain-asymbolia/)). The cases rule out neither co-optimizer, and that failure is no positive evidence for interactionism. The accounts appear to diverge in *theoretical structure*—what they say is doing the work—more clearly than they diverge in *predicted observation*.

## Relation to Site Perspective

The Map's interest in this exchange is governed by [Bidirectional Interaction](/tenets/#bidirectional-interaction). The property-dualist co-optimizer offers the most disciplined way to hold that consciousness influences nothing while still explaining the correlation Bidirectional Interaction is often invoked to explain. Taking it seriously is therefore a test of the Map's own evidential honesty.

The constitutive-physicalist co-optimizer meets a different tenet. It grants felt awfulness causal efficacy as the computational role, and the trilemma has no purchase on it, since the necessity horn is its thesis. What remains is [Dualism](/tenets/#dualism)'s question, whether the feeling is anything over and above that role, and the cost the Map presses there is the general [explanatory gap](/topics/hard-problem-of-consciousness/) (why that role should feel like anything), no debt peculiar to co-optimization. The rest of this section concerns the property-dualist co-optimizer.

The Map interprets the trilemma in the "why this specific pairing?" section as a real cost to that co-optimizer: a brute necessity, an alignment permitted but not explained, or a relational valence that a realist about felt valence cannot accept. Whether that cost counts in interactionism's favour depends on which interactionism is meant, because interactionism needs psychophysical laws of its own. Its outbound law says that felt badness pushes toward avoidance rather than approach, and held as a bare regularity that law faces the same choice between necessity and indifference. Mørch (2017) concludes that some criticisms of the evolutionary argument "are indeed fatal to the argument if it is understood, as it traditionally has been, as an argument for any standard version of non-epiphenomenalism such as physicalism and interactionism". She restores the argument only for her phenomenal powers view, on which phenomenal properties necessitate their effects "in virtue of how they feel". If the Map's interactionism takes that reading of its outbound law, a construction that places Mørch's view inside a dualism she does not hold, then interactionism may explain the alignment: valence would track fitness because valence drives the behaviour, and selection would shape valence through that causal channel ([valence as selection currency](/topics/valence-and-conscious-selection/#evolutionary-considerations)). Only on that reading does the Map count the trilemma as what may be a point in interactionism's favour *on grounds of theoretical economy*. Even this conditional advantage is contested: Zietsch (2024) argues that physicalism, dualism and panpsychism all fail to explain the correlations "via adaptation", and that the phenomenal powers view cannot do so either. He judges his own answer, "sensational associative learning" during development, compatible with dualism "though not epiphenomenalism", so it is not available to the property-dualist co-optimizer.

One debt is shared whichever reading is taken. Co-optimization and interactionism both need an inbound law saying which neural states present which feelings, and that law's contingency is the [psychophysical harmony](/topics/inheritance-problem-for-the-psychophysical-law/#harmony) problem. Phenomenal powers removes at most the arbitrariness of the outbound law, so the inbound debt stays with the interactionist in full and cannot be charged to co-optimization alone. Nor does the Map claim the clinical evidence *settles* the comparison. The asymbolia and congenital-insensitivity cases appear consistent with the Map's reading and equally consistent with either co-optimizer's reading. To present their compatibility with interactionism as confirmation would commit exactly the calibration error the [constrain-vs-establish discipline](/concepts/pain-asymbolia/#causal-efficacy-of-phenomenal-quality) warns against.

This restraint matters for [Occam's Razor Has Limits](/tenets/#occams-limits). Co-optimization is the parsimonious-looking position—one fewer kind of causation. The Map does not claim it is refuted, only that its apparent simplicity may hide an explanatory debt, and that debt has to be priced against interactionism's own. Once that shared inbound debt is set aside, the difference is this. The epiphenomenalist needs its inbound law to be harmoniously *shaped* as well, pairing avoidance-implementing states with felt badness although selection cannot see the feeling, and pays for that shape with one of the trilemma's three prices. The interactionist needs an outbound law, which escapes the same arbitrariness only on the phenomenal powers reading, and [a quantum interaction channel](/tenets/#minimal-quantum-interaction) through which that law acts, visible enough to selection that the strictest Born-preservation reading would forbid it. Whether either ledger is heavier is exactly the kind of question this tenet says simplicity cannot adjudicate on its own.

The Map's position is therefore deliberately modest. Co-optimization in its property-dualist form is the strongest epiphenomenalist reply to the correlation problem, it survives the dissociation evidence, and the case against it is a theoretical-economy case, conditional on the phenomenal powers reading of interactionism's outbound law, rather than an empirical knockdown. In its constitutive form it escapes that case, and the dispute is over Dualism. The closest available tests, wanting/liking mismatches, pit two physical variables against each other; a discriminating test would have to vary felt pleasure itself.
