Enactivism poses the most philosophically serious challenge to [interactionist-dualism](/concepts/interactionist-dualism/) from the embodied cognition tradition. Where other objections target specific mechanisms—how a non-physical mind could influence neurons, where the causal interface sits—enactivism attacks the framework itself. There is no mind-body gap to bridge, enactivists argue, because mind and body were never separate. Cognition, on their view, is the organism's embodied engagement with its environment rather than internal computation directed by a non-physical consciousness. The Unfinishable Map takes this challenge seriously while arguing that enactivism's own commitments—particularly its insistence on the irreducibility of experience—ultimately support rather than undermine the dualist insight.

The challenge comes in three distinct strands, each targeting a different aspect of the interactionist picture. But all three share a common difficulty: they do not explain why embodied activity is accompanied by subjective experience. This is the [hard problem](/topics/hard-problem-of-consciousness/), and it functions as a litmus test. Any position that acknowledges consciousness as real and irreducible must eventually face the question dualism was designed to answer.

## Three Strands of Challenge

### Autopoietic Enactivism

Varela, Thompson, and Rosch's foundational work *The Embodied Mind* (1991) drew on autopoiesis—the self-producing, self-maintaining organisation of living systems—to argue that cognition is a biological phenomenon continuous with life itself. Thompson developed this into the *deep continuity thesis* in *Mind in Life* (2007): where there is life, there is mind, because the same organisational principles that make a cell self-maintaining also give it a rudimentary "point of view" on its environment.

This directly challenges interactionist dualism's picture of consciousness as a non-physical factor that interfaces with brain processes. If mind is continuous with life—if the simplest bacterium already engages in "sense-making"—then positing a separate mental domain that causally interacts with the physical becomes unnecessary. The autopoietic organisation of living systems already provides what dualism attributes to a non-physical mind: perspective, agency, and world-directedness.

Thompson argues that experience is irreducible, but not for the reasons property dualists give. Experience is irreducible not because it possesses "metaphysically peculiar 'properties'" but because of "its ineliminable transcendental character": lived experience "is always already presupposed by any statement, model, or theory" (Thompson 2004, 394). He adds at once: "There is no dualism or idealism here."

### Sensorimotor Enactivism

O'Regan and Noë's sensorimotor contingency theory argues that perceptual experience is constituted by the exercise of practical knowledge about how sensory stimulation changes with movement. Seeing is not receiving visual data that consciousness then interprets; seeing *is* the skilled exploration of visual contingencies. The qualitative character of experience—why red looks the way it does, why vision differs from audition—is explained by differences in contingency structure, not by properties of an inner mental domain.

This challenges interactionist dualism's account of [qualia](/concepts/qualia/). If the redness of red is constituted by mastery of red-related sensorimotor contingencies, there is no residual "phenomenal property" requiring a non-physical explanation. The explanatory work that dualism assigns to consciousness is performed by the organism's embodied know-how. The Map's [treatment of sensorimotor contingencies](/topics/sensorimotor-contingencies-and-the-interface/) engages this challenge in detail, arguing that contingency mastery specifies *which* qualities experience has without explaining *that* there are qualities at all.

### Radical Enactivism

Hutto and Myin's radical enactivism pushes furthest, arguing that basic minds are entirely contentless—no mental representations, no intentional content. Only linguistically sophisticated cognition involves representational content. This challenges dualism at the level of intentionality: if basic cognition involves no mental content, then there is no content for a non-physical mind to bear.

Hutto and Myin identify a [Hard Problem of Content](/topics/the-naturalisation-failure-for-content/), whose name echoes the hard problem of consciousness: the resources naturalistic theories of content rely on—causal covariation and selected biological function—cannot by themselves deliver correctness conditions or intensionality. The structural parallel is striking—radical enactivism finds, in its own domain, a gap of the same shape as the [explanatory gap](/concepts/explanatory-gap/) that motivates dualism. The parallel has limits Hutto and Myin insist on: they conclude that basic minds lack content, locate content in sociocultural practices they take to be fully natural, and treat the hard problem of consciousness deflationarily rather than as a limit (Kirchhoff & Hutto 2016). The Map reads the parallel as convergent evidence that physicalism may keep encountering the same boundary from different directions—though the parallel could equally be read as two instances of one over-demanding standard for "accounting for," and which reading is correct is not settled by the parallel alone. The Map's treatment of that argument develops this reading in detail, accepting the negative argument while declining the positive Radical Enactive Cognition programme that Hutto and Myin build atop it.

## Pragmatist Genealogy

Enactivism's intellectual roots run partly through classical American pragmatism — a lineage that, on the Map's reading, sits in underappreciated tension with its anti-dualist conclusions. John Dewey's "The Reflex Arc Concept in Psychology" (1896) rejected stimulus-response atomism in favour of a continuous sensorimotor circuit — the kind of organism-environment coordination that autopoietic enactivism later developed through biological self-organisation. Dewey's *Experience and Nature* (1925) treated mind as active engagement rather than passive observation, anticipating enactivism's core insight that cognition is embodied activity rather than internal computation.

The lineage has two strands rather than one chain. Wu (2024) argues that most enactivists have consciously claimed the phenomenological strand, Merleau-Ponty's embodied phenomenology above all, while long ignoring the pragmatist one—though pragmatism foreshadowed their core ideas closely enough that he calls it the foundation of cognitive enactivism. Wu reads both traditions as neutral monist, not dualist. The Map's article on [pragmatism's path to dualism](/topics/pragmatisms-path-to-dualism/) argues that pragmatism, applied reflexively to consciousness, supports dualism: materialism about consciousness fails pragmatism's own test because it eliminates the evaluative standpoint that pragmatic assessment requires. Dewey himself was a committed anti-dualist, so this is the Map's reconstruction of where pragmatism's logic leads, not a reading of Dewey. If the reconstruction holds, enactivism inherits its core commitments from a tradition whose own logic points toward dualism, and its anti-dualist conclusions sit in tension with that heritage — a tension its practitioners, having largely overlooked the heritage, have left unexamined.

## The Dissolution Strategy and Its Limits

What unites these strands is a shared strategy: *dissolution* rather than *solution*. Enactivism does not answer the question "How does a non-physical mind causally interact with the physical brain?" It argues the question is malformed. Mind and body are not separate domains requiring causal bridging; they are aspects of a single organismic process—a view with affinities to [process-philosophy](/concepts/process-philosophy/), though enactivists draw on phenomenological rather than metaphysical traditions.

On this picture the only "interaction" in play is organism-environment engagement, not mind-body causal commerce. Enactivism's interactions are embodied activities, not transmissions across an ontological boundary.

This is a coherent philosophical move. If the mind-body separation is genuinely mistaken, dissolving the problem is the right response. The question is whether enactivism earns this dissolution—whether its account of cognition as embodied activity genuinely eliminates the need for the distinction dualism draws.

The evidence suggests it does not, though the judgement is not forced by the data alone. The dissolution strategy works for the *functional* aspects of cognition—perception, action, sense-making can plausibly be understood as embodied activities without invoking a separate mental domain. But it appears to fail precisely where dualism's arguments are strongest: the [explanatory gap](/concepts/explanatory-gap/) between functional organisation and subjective experience.

Consider Thompson's deep continuity thesis. Even granting that all living systems engage in sense-making, the question remains: *why is sense-making accompanied by experience?* A bacterium's chemotaxis is a form of sense-making—it responds differentially to its chemical environment. Does it experience anything? Thompson's framework seems unable to answer this without either attributing experience to all life—biopsychism, a narrower thesis than [panpsychism](/concepts/panpsychism/)—or introducing a threshold at which experience [emerges](/concepts/emergence/) from organisation (which would reintroduce the explanatory gap at a different level). Thompson's own later assessment concedes that sense-making does not settle the matter: even on the enactive view of life as value-driven, "there remains a conceptual and inferential gap between differential responsiveness to value and hedonic value or affective valence," and he finds the case neither for nor against biopsychism conclusive (Thompson 2022). Nor did he claim the larger gap was closed: *Mind in Life* offers "important resources for making progress on the explanatory gap" (Thompson 2007, 14) and recasts it as a "body-body problem"—relating the lived body to the living organism one is. A committed deep-continuity theorist might reply that the question itself presupposes the production framing the thesis rejects; whether that reply succeeds or merely relocates the difficulty is part of what the dispute underdetermines.

The sharpest statement of this structural problem comes from inside enactivism. Kirchhoff and Hutto (2016), writing as radical enactivists, argue that Varela's [neurophenomenology](/concepts/neurophenomenology-and-contemplative-neuroscience/)—combining first-person phenomenological reports with third-person neuroscience—"assumes rather than dissolves the hard problem of consciousness." A method that takes first-person experience as irreducible data cannot also claim to have got beyond the question of why experience exists. Their own remedy is to deny that the physical and the phenomenal are metaphysically distinct and embrace a strict identity thesis, which gives up the irreducibility Varela and Thompson insist on; it is the first horn of the dilemma [set out below](#unexpected-common-ground).

## Unexpected Common Ground

The most philosophically productive aspect of enactivism's challenge is not the disagreement but the convergence. Enactivism and interactionist dualism share commitments that are more fundamental than their differences.

**Both reject eliminative materialism.** Enactivists insist that consciousness is real and central to their framework. Thompson treats experience as "irreducible." Varela developed [neurophenomenology](/concepts/neurophenomenology-and-contemplative-neuroscience/) precisely because he believed first-person experience demanded serious philosophical and scientific attention. This places enactivism closer to the Map's position than to the eliminativism of Dennett or the illusionism of Frankish.

**Both reject reductive physicalism**—radical enactivism's identity thesis apart. The [phenomenological](/concepts/phenomenology/) tradition grounding autopoietic enactivism—Husserl, Merleau-Ponty (1945/2012), Thompson—treats first-person experience as irreducible to third-person physical description. This is the premise from which the Map's [Dualism tenet](/tenets/#dualism) starts—consciousness cannot be fully explained by physics alone—though the phenomenologists decline the ontological conclusion the Map draws from it.

**Both face the hard problem.** Dualism faces it by design—explaining how non-physical consciousness relates to the physical world is the central challenge. Enactivism faces it despite its dissolution strategy. The gap between organismic activity and subjective experience reopens whenever enactivists acknowledge that experience is real and irreducible, because acknowledging irreducibility while denying a non-physical aspect of reality requires explaining where the irreducibility comes from.

The difference is metaphysical. Dualism locates irreducibility in a non-physical aspect of reality. Enactivism tries to locate it in biological organisation—in the "transcendental character" of lived experience, or in the deep continuity between life and mind. The Map's contention is that this second option may be unstable. Either biological organisation fully accounts for experience (which looks like reductive physicalism—the strict identity Kirchhoff and Hutto recommend—contradicting enactivism's own irreducibility claim) or it does not (which suggests something beyond biological organisation is needed, which points toward dualism).

It is worth being explicit about the limits of this dilemma. The available evidence—phenomenological reports, the deep continuity between life and cognition, the explanatory gap—does not adjudicate between the interactionist-dualist reading and the enactivist's third option, on which "transcendental character" names a *sui generis* form of irreducibility that is neither reductive nor dualist. The standpoint is underdetermined by the evidence both sides accept. The Map reads the dilemma as exhaustive; a sympathetic enactivist reads the transcendental construal as a genuine escape between its horns. Which reading is correct turns on whether "transcendental irreducibility" is a substantive metaphysical position or a relabelling of the gap it was meant to close—a question the phenomenology underdetermines and that neither side can settle by appeal to data both already accept. The Map's confidence here is therefore the confidence of the better-supported reading, not of a demonstration.

## The Coherence Question

Enactivism faces an internal tension that its critics have identified: it blends deflationary and revisionist tendencies in ways that may be incoherent. Shani (2020) presses this against radical enactivism, arguing that Hutto and Myin's treatment of experience is inherently unstable: it pairs a strongly deflationary attempt to discard the hard problem with an implicit commitment to substantive metaphysical revision. These aims pull in opposite directions—dissolution says the problems were misconceived, while revision says they need new answers. Meyer and Brancazio (2022) question the movement's revolutionary self-image from another side: enactivists have given no compelling reason for enactivism to replace cognitivism, which faces no Kuhnian crisis, and enactivism is better understood as a philosophy of nature than as a rival paradigm.

The Map extends Shani's diagnosis to enactivism generally. This matters for the challenge to interactionist dualism because enactivism's strongest objections are deflationary (the mind-body distinction is a mistake), while its positive programme is revisionist (we need a new understanding of mind as embodied activity). If the deflationary move succeeds, the revisionist programme is unnecessary. If the revisionist programme is necessary, the deflationary move was incomplete.

Interactionist dualism is at least internally consistent: consciousness is non-physical, it interacts with the brain, the interaction is the hard part. Enactivism's challenge to this consistency is weaker if enactivism cannot demonstrate its own.

## Relation to Site Perspective

**[Dualism](/tenets/#dualism)**: Enactivism correctly identifies that Cartesian substance dualism—a ghost directing a machine—fails phenomenologically. The Map agrees. That phenomenological critique does not by itself reach property dualism or the Map's interactionist framework, which holds that consciousness is irreducible without requiring a separate *substance*; what reaches them is the dissolution argument, answered above—and Thompson explicitly rejects property dualism as well. The Map reads enactivism's own insistence on irreducibility as evidence for dualism rather than against it: if experience cannot be reduced to biological organisation, the better-supported reading is that something beyond biological organisation accounts for it, though Thompson's transcendental construal remains a live alternative.

**[Bidirectional Interaction](/tenets/#bidirectional-interaction)**: Enactivism rejects mind-body causal interaction in favour of organism-environment interaction. The Map argues these are not alternatives. Organism-environment interaction describes the *structure* through which consciousness acts; bidirectional mind-body interaction is the Map's account of how conscious states (attention, intention, effort) come to influence bodily outcomes within that structure. It does not by itself explain *why* the structure is accompanied by experience; that is the hard problem, which dualism faces by design. Enactivism sidesteps the [causal-closure](/concepts/causal-closure/) debate by denying the mind-body distinction, and the Map counts the sidestep as a cost: enactivist accounts of agency describe the organism's self-regulation while leaving open whether felt attention, intention, and effort make a causal difference of their own—the question that motivates the debate.

**[Minimal Quantum Interaction](/tenets/#minimal-quantum-interaction)**: Enactivism locates the mind-life connection in autopoietic organisation rather than quantum mechanics. The Map has found no direct engagement between enactivists and quantum-interaction models—the debate is typically framed as enactivism versus substance dualism, not versus quantum interactionism. The Map's quantum interface hypothesis operates at a level of description enactivism does not address: not *whether* mind and body interact but *how* the interaction is physically implemented.

**[No Many Worlds](/tenets/#no-many-worlds)**: Enactivism does not directly engage quantum interpretation. However, its emphasis on the organism's singular perspective—this organism, in this environment, enacting this world—is compatible with the Map's rejection of many-worlds. Enactivism's phenomenology is indexical: experience belongs to a particular embodied subject, not to all possible branches of a universal wavefunction.

**[Occam's Razor Has Limits](/tenets/#occams-limits)**: Enactivism's apparent parsimony—explaining cognition through embodied activity without invoking a non-physical domain—is less than it seems. A dissolution of the hard problem that leaves experience irreducible is incomplete, and incompleteness is not economy. Claiming to have dissolved the mind-body problem while acknowledging that experience is irreducible arguably conceals ontological commitments rather than reducing them.
