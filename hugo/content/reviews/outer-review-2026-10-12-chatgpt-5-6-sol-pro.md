## Reply

# Outer review: “Agent Causation”

**Recommendation: major revision.**

The article survives scrutiny as a **conditional metaphysical proposal**: given a persisting conscious subject, genuinely open physical alternatives, a non-branching ontology, and a lawful subject–brain relation, agent causation supplies one intelligible answer to the question “who settles which possibility becomes actual?” It does not presently establish that this model is better supported than event-causal libertarianism, naturalistic agent causation, or sophisticated compatibilism. Nor does it show that attention, effort, or motor-selection findings preferentially support a nonphysical subject selecting quantum outcomes.

The central weakness is a recurrent slide between four different achievements:

1. showing that agent causation is coherent under the Map’s tenets;
2. showing that familiar evidence is compatible with it;
3. showing that the evidence discriminates it from rivals;
4. specifying a mechanism that connects a conscious subject to physics.

The article does reasonably well at the first, provides some material for the second, but repeatedly writes as though it has accomplished the third and fourth.

There are also two material attribution problems: the universal substance-causation model is assigned too readily to Timothy O’Connor, and Robert Kane’s recent hybrid theory is presented as convergence when it is more accurately a naturalistic rival. The neuroscience citations mostly exist and are recognisable, but several studies are described in ways that strengthen their apparent relevance beyond what their methods establish.

## 1. What the article gets right

The article is unusually explicit about several limitations that weaker treatments of agent causation conceal.

It says that a substance-agent interpretation is downstream of the site’s core outcome-selection tenet rather than strictly entailed by it; acknowledges that a subject–brain pairing law is still owed; treats the rejection of branching as an additional global posit; says that the motor evidence removes a possible defeater rather than positively confirming agent causation; and concedes that physicalism predicts many of the same attentional signatures. Its positions register is particularly candid: it grades the empirical evidence as limited or indirect, denies that there is independent evidence for the proposed quantum mechanism, and treats biological quantum effects only as precedent rather than confirmation. [agent-causation](/concepts/agent-causation/)

Those concessions preserve a defensible core:

- Agent causation offers a model of **sourcehood**, not merely indeterminism.
- It captures the first-person distinction between an outcome merely happening in a person and its being settled by that person.
- Its primitive-causation stopping point is not automatically incoherent merely because event-causal accounts stop elsewhere.
- A dualist version is not refuted simply by showing that reasons, decisions, and control processes have neural correlates.
- The contrast between a probability distribution and a token outcome is a legitimate place to pose an explanatory question, provided the quantum interpretation and physical ontology are specified.

The article’s problem is therefore not that agent causation is nonsensical. It is that the article often treats the model’s ability to *represent* agency as evidence that this is what agency *is*.

## 2. Philosophical citation and attribution audit

### O’Connor, Lowe, and universal substance causation

The sentence assigning to O’Connor a substance-causation theory in which all causation consists in agents exercising powers is materially misleading.

O’Connor is unquestionably a central contemporary agent-causal libertarian. But the stronger thesis that the causes of all events are substances, so that all event causation is derivative from substance causation, is the position O’Connor and Jonathan Jacobs associate specifically with E. J. Lowe. Their discussion distinguishes an event-causal neo-Aristotelian model from a substance-causal one rather than simply identifying O’Connor’s own earlier agent-causal theory with universal substance causation. They also explicitly say that their joint paper is exploring the implications of substance causation, not endorsing or establishing it. [Tim O'Connor+3](https://toconnor.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/07/OConnor_Jacobs_Agent-Causation-in-a-Neo-Aristotelian-Metaphysics-2012.pdf)

The article should therefore distinguish three claims:

- **O’Connor’s agent-causal claim:** persons can directly cause decisions or intentions.
- **Lowe’s general substance-causal claim:** substances are the primary causes throughout nature.
- **The Map’s dualist claim:** a nonphysical conscious subject selects among physically available outcomes.

These are not interchangeable. In particular, “all causes are substances” does not mean “all causes are agents”; rocks, organisms, and other non-agential substances may exercise causal powers under a general substance-causation ontology.

### Kane 2024 is a rival, not straightforward convergence

The article is literally correct that Kane’s recent work combines agent-causal and event-causal elements. Kane’s 2024 book exists and centrally addresses that hybridisation. But the suggestion that this represents convergence on the Map’s model is seriously incomplete. [OUP Academic](https://academic.oup.com/book/58033)

Kane’s account treats the agent as an information-responsive, complex dynamical system exercising teleological guidance control. He argues that agent-causal and event-causal descriptions are jointly needed, but explicitly rejects causation that is metaphysically primitive, exempt from natural law, attributable to an immaterial substance, or modelled on an “unmoved mover.” [ResearchGate+2](https://www.researchgate.net/profile/Robert-Kane-5/publication/357552388_Robert_Kane_Making_Sense_of_a_Free_Will_that_is_Incompatible_With_Determinism/links/61d3b561d450060816893eae/Robert-Kane-Making-Sense-of-a-Free-Will-that-is-Incompatible-With-Determinism.pdf)

Kane therefore belongs in the article primarily as a **naturalistic hybrid competitor**. His view challenges two claims made or implied by the Map:

- that robust agent causation requires ontological dualism; and
- that an event-causal implementation necessarily eliminates the agent in favour of happenings inside the agent.

The article may stipulate that *its own* version of agent causation is substance dualist. It should not say, as a general taxonomy, that agent causation without dualism simply collapses into event causation.

### More recent alternatives are missing

Two 2026 contributions are directly relevant and predate the article’s October revision.

Potter and Mitchell defend naturalistic libertarian control by arguing that standard luck objections rely on impoverished models of indeterminism and decision. Their account locates control at the macroscopic agent level in an underdetermined dynamical system rather than in an immaterial selector. Dwayne Moore develops an emergent agent-causal libertarianism in which whole agents influence neural components while remaining within a physicalist ontology. [Springer+1](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11229-026-05570-5)

These are not peripheral additions. They directly contest the article’s inference from “event descriptions are insufficient to capture whole-agent control” to “a distinct conscious substance is required.”

### Bibliographic completeness and recency

I found no obviously fabricated load-bearing work in the printed reference list. The citation problems are principally ones of **fidelity, stance, and localization**, rather than nonexistent publications.

The bibliography is nevertheless incomplete. Several figures or works invoked in the body—including van Inwagen, Mele, Libet, Tulving, Thura and Cisek, Stapp, and Penrose–Hameroff—are not consistently given complete references. Aristotle is cited without a work, book, passage, translation, or edition, while the Thomistic lineage is invoked without a precise Aquinas reference. Major book-length sources need page or chapter locators wherever a definite claim is attributed to them. [agent-causation](/concepts/agent-causation/)

The phrase “recent work” is particularly inappropriate in a passage whose named reference points include Bergson from 1907, Hausman from 1984, and Boden from 1990. Those works may remain relevant, but they are historical or foundational sources, not a current-literature survey. [agent-causation](/concepts/agent-causation/)

## 3. The luck objection is answered only at one level

The article offers several connected replies to luck:

- the agent actively selects rather than passively receives a random outcome;
- selection is experienced as effortful;
- reasons explain ordinary non-akratic choices;
- an atemporal or whole-history treatment may incorporate prior states into what the agent selects;
- eventually the explanation terminates in the primitive fact that the agent did it.

This constitutes a plausible **sourcehood response**. It says that the token choice is attributable to the agent rather than to an impersonal randomizer. It does not dissolve the stronger **contrastive luck problem**.

Suppose two complete replays contain the same agent, character, reasons, deliberation, effort, and open alternatives, but one ends in choosing $A$ and the other in choosing $B$. Saying “the agent settled it” identifies the source in each replay. It does not yet explain why this agent settled on $A$ here rather than $B$, when every cited reason and antecedent was present in both.

The linked Free Will article now puts the point more accurately: agent causation answers **who settles**, while leaving a modal or contrastive question about **why this way rather than another**. It also observes that reasons available in every replay cannot by themselves explain the difference between replays. [free-will](/topics/free-will/)

The individual replies therefore need tighter grading:

### Active selection versus passive chance

This is an important conceptual distinction, but it establishes attribution rather than contrastive explanation. Calling the transition an exercise of power rather than an indeterministic event will satisfy readers who accept primitive agent causation. It will not persuade readers who think that relabelling the indeterministic transition leaves its luck untouched.

### Effort

Effort may show that choosing is an extended control process rather than an instantaneous coin toss. That helps against crude chance models. But effort is also predicted by physical and event-causal control systems that monitor conflict, allocate resources, revise plans, and suppress competing responses. The fact that choosing feels effortful cannot by itself establish which ontology implements the effort.

Moreover, the principal attention study cited by the article did not measure phenomenal effort. It compared choice and instruction conditions using neural activity and connectivity measures. [NCBI+1](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6611462/)

### Reasons

Reasons can explain why a choice was intelligible, rational, or attributable to the agent. They do not provide a complete contrastive explanation when the same reasons leave both outcomes open.

The problem is clearest in akrasia. If the balance of reasons supports $A$ but the agent selects $B$, the reasons reply cannot explain the selection without either introducing another motive that favours $B$, thereby restoring an event-causal explanation, or accepting the selection as primitive. The site’s October 11 revision elsewhere now explicitly says that the luck objection is “answered rather than dissolved” and that the akratic case remains open. That correction postdates this article and has not been propagated into it. [changelog](/workflow/changelog/)

### Atemporal selection

The proposal that the agent selects an entire temporal chain does not obviously solve the problem. It relocates it to why this complete history was selected rather than another. If the selected totality includes all the facts invoked to explain the selection, the proposal risks explanatory circularity; if it does not, the contrastive gap remains.

It also creates a new metaphysical burden. The theory must explain whether a present agent literally causes or selects past neural states and initial conditions, whether the apparent temporal order of deliberation is derivative, and how responsibility is assigned within a globally selected history. Without this, “atemporal” functions as a promissory relocation rather than an account.

### The correct conclusion

The article should say:

> Agent causation offers a primitive sourcehood answer to the luck objection: the indeterministic difference is an exercise of the agent’s power rather than an event external to the agent. This may answer the attributional objection, but it leaves a residual contrastive and modal explanatory debt, especially in akratic cases.

That conclusion is defensible and consistent with the newer Free Will material. Claiming that luck has been dissolved is not.

## 4. Event-causal alternatives are under-described

The article treats event-causal libertarianism as a sequence in which prior mental events cause a decision, while the agent cannot count as ultimate source because the agent did not choose those prior events. This is too weak a target.

Contemporary event-causal and hybrid theories can appeal to:

- temporally extended deliberation;
- reasons-responsive feedback;
- inhibition and error correction;
- non-deviant causal pathways;
- self-modifying or character-forming actions;
- system-level control that is multiply describable at event and agent levels;
- recurrent dynamics rather than a linear chain of discrete mental events.

Kane’s model is particularly damaging to the article’s dichotomy. On his view, event-causal descriptions explain how the agent’s purposive control is implemented; they do not replace the agent. His agent is a whole dynamical system whose guidance and information sensitivity constrain lower-level processes. [ResearchGate+1](https://www.researchgate.net/profile/Robert-Kane-5/publication/357552388_Robert_Kane_Making_Sense_of_a_Free_Will_that_is_Incompatible_With_Determinism/links/61d3b561d450060816893eae/Robert-Kane-Making-Sense-of-a-Free-Will-that-is-Incompatible-With-Determinism.pdf)

The article also applies an asymmetrical regress standard. It objects that an event-causal agent did not choose the reasons, desires, or mental events from which the decision proceeds. But the substance agent likewise did not choose its initial character, cognitive capacities, available options, perceptual input, or many of its reasons. A primitive capacity to settle the final outcome does not automatically show why unchosen antecedents are authorship-destroying in the event-causal case but harmless in the agent-causal case.

The discussion of intention and “direction of fit” is also overdrawn. The article suggests that an intention literally directs the world only under substance causation, whereas direction of fit is metaphorical under event causation. That does not follow. A physically implemented goal representation can literally guide prediction, error correction, attention, and motor control. The metaphysical question is whether that system-level purposiveness is exhaustive, not whether it is merely figurative.

Similarly, creative option generation does not by itself establish token sourcehood. A system may generate novel alternatives while the eventual choice remains event-causal, stochastic, habitual, or externally constrained. Option formation and option selection need separate arguments.

Finally, the Consequence Argument should be segregated from the discussion of event-causal libertarianism. Its target is deterministic compatibilism. Invoking it inside a comparison with indeterministic event-causal theories obscures rather than resolves the taxonomy. [agent-causation](/concepts/agent-causation/)

## 5. Compatibilism is not confronted in the main argument

The linked Compatibilist Symmetry article already states the challenge more forcefully than the Agent Causation article does. It acknowledges that sophisticated compatibilist accounts can reproduce the phenomenology of choosing, reasons-responsiveness, character expression, moral practices, and ordinary distinctions between voluntary and coerced action. It also states the correct evidential rule: evidence equally expected under both frameworks establishes compatibility, not support for one over the other. [compatibilist-symmetry-challenge](/concepts/compatibilist-symmetry-challenge/)

That discussion should be part of the main article rather than relegated to further reading.

The article needs to distinguish at least three claims:

- **ordinary control:** the action flows through the agent’s evaluative and executive capacities;
- **historical ownership:** those capacities are appropriately integrated with the agent’s history, identity, and responsiveness to reasons;
- **ultimate sourcehood:** the agent is not merely the terminus of antecedent causes but originates the token outcome in a stronger metaphysical sense.

Compatibilists can accept the first two and deny that the third is necessary for responsibility. Agent causation may offer the third, but the article cannot infer its necessity merely from evidence for the first two.

The article would be stronger if it conceded that its comparative advantage is conditional:

> Under the Map’s prior commitment to irreducible consciousness and libertarian sourcehood, agent causation provides a more direct interpretation of authorship. It has not been shown to have greater empirical or ordinary moral explanatory power than reasons-responsive compatibilism.

That is effectively the conclusion already reached by the linked symmetry article.

## 6. The proposed interface with physics is a schema, not a mechanism

The article says that quantum physics specifies probabilities rather than token outcomes and places agent selection around decoherence. It then invokes trumping pre-emption to describe the conscious agent as the authoritative cause while physical processes carry the implementation. This is a possible metaphysical picture. It is not yet a physical mechanism.

### The quantum premise is interpretation-dependent

The statement that physics supplies probabilities but no sufficient physical cause is not a neutral consequence of quantum mechanics. It is conditional on adopting a single-outcome, genuinely stochastic ontology whose physical laws leave room for supplementary selection. Rejecting Many-Worlds does not by itself yield such a gap, still less identify an agent as what fills it.

The article should therefore replace unqualified formulations with:

> On a single-outcome interpretation in which the physical state and laws provide only an objective probability distribution, the Map proposes that conscious agency helps determine the realised member.

That wording makes clear which premise carries the load.

### Decoherence does not select a unique outcome

Decoherence suppresses interference between environmentally distinguished components and helps explain stable quasi-classical structure. It does not by itself explain why exactly one component becomes actual. Standard treatments explicitly distinguish decoherence from a solution to the definite-outcome problem. [University of Portland Faculty+1](https://faculty.up.edu/schlosshauer//publications/RevModPhys_76_001267.pdf)

Consequently, “selection at decoherence” is ambiguous:

- **Before or during decoherence:** the theory needs an interaction with the quantum state and its environmental dynamics.
- **After decoherence but before outcome realization:** the theory needs an ontology containing such an interval and a law governing selection within it.
- **After an outcome already exists:** the agent is too late to select it.

Decoherence is therefore a candidate stage in a model, not an interaction law.

### “Trumping” does not supply the coupling

Trumping pre-emption can describe a hierarchy of causal authority: the agent causes the action, while a neural pathway is the subordinate realizer. It does not explain how the conscious cause changes—or determines—which physical trajectory occurs.

A mechanism would need to specify at least:

- the physical state space and target degrees of freedom;
- the basis or observable over which alternatives are defined;
- how an intention or reason maps to those alternatives;
- the mathematical intervention or transition rule;
- whether Born probabilities are preserved, conditioned, or altered;
- the timing and duration of intervention;
- how microscopic selection is amplified into neural control;
- why one subject is paired with one brain;
- conservation, no-signalling, and causal-consistency constraints;
- an observation that differs from the predictions of physical rivals.

Until those are supplied, “conscious selection” names the missing operation rather than models it.

### The Born-rule dilemma must be imported from the Free Will article

The linked Free Will page now states the dilemma clearly. If conscious selection changes aggregate outcome frequencies, the theory predicts deviations that have not been demonstrated. If it always preserves the Born distribution, the article must explain what empirical or explanatory work selection performs beyond an ordinary stochastic law. [free-will](/topics/free-will/)

This is one of the most important objections to the whole framework, yet it is absent from the present article’s physics discussion.

There are possible replies—for example, conditional selection that preserves global frequencies while correlating outcomes with intentions—but such a reply would itself require a formal joint-distribution model. It would also create questions about detectable mutual information, fine-tuning, and whether the psychophysical correlation could be redescribed as a physical control law.

### Biological quantum effects provide only a very weak precedent

The magnetoreception example shows that delicate quantum processes can remain biologically relevant under appropriate molecular protection and amplification. Radical-pair chemistry is a specific, experimentally constrained physical mechanism. It does not support the existence of nonphysical selection, quantum neural agency, or post-decoherence intervention. [Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-024-55124-x)

The analogy establishes only:

> Warm biological environments do not universally eliminate functionally relevant quantum effects.

It does not establish:

> Conscious subjects can choose individual quantum outcomes in brains.

Tegmark’s very short decoherence estimates for proposed neural superpositions remain relevant to pre-decoherence neural quantum theories. Moving selection to a post-decoherence stage avoids that particular objection only by making the physical operation less specified, not by positively identifying a viable process. [APS Journals](https://link.aps.org/doi/10.1103/PhysRevE.61.4194)

### Pairing remains a major rather than minor debt

The article admits that a pairing law is owed. This should be promoted from a caveat to a central section. Spatial proximity is insufficient when one relatum is asserted to be nonspatial or only indirectly located. Functional fit threatens circularity: identifying “my brain” as the brain controlled by me presupposes the relation that the law is meant to explain.

The theory needs to say whether pairing is:

- fundamental and brute;
- fixed by embodiment history;
- fixed by information integration;
- established developmentally;
- mediated by a psychophysical field or relation;
- capable of changing in split-brain, transplant, fusion, or duplication cases.

Without that, the theory does not yet explain why the proposed causal power affects this neural system rather than another.

## 7. The attention evidence is overinterpreted

### Müller and Rabbitt do not compare willed with instructed attention

The classic Müller and Rabbitt experiment contrasts peripheral onset cues with central symbolic cues. Its “voluntary” or endogenous condition is still externally instructed: participants interpret a symbolic arrow and orient attention accordingly. It does not compare self-chosen attention with instructed attention. [Research Explorer](https://research.manchester.ac.uk/en/publications/reflexive-and-voluntary-orienting-of-visual-attention-time-course)

It therefore cannot support the article’s apparent hierarchy in which exogenous, instructed, and freely willed attention have successively longer latencies or greater effort. At most, it supports a distinction between relatively automatic exogenous orienting and slower centrally mediated endogenous orienting.

### Rajan et al. show additional neural decision processing

Rajan and colleagues compared trials in which participants chose an attentional direction with trials in which a cue instructed that direction. They reported later theta activity and reciprocal frontoparietal interactions in the choice condition. That is a genuine willed-versus-instructed contrast in an operational task sense. [NCBI+1](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6611462/)

But several qualifications matter:

- “Willed” is the experiment’s behavioural condition, not evidence that the choice was metaphysically undetermined.
- “Bidirectional” refers to estimated communication between frontal and parietal neural regions, not bidirectional causation between mind and brain.
- The study did not measure the conscious effort attributed to the condition by the Map.
- The reported regions were sensor-space regions of interest rather than securely source-localised generators, a limitation the authors themselves note. [NCBI](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6611462/)
- More extensive neural processing is expected when a participant must decide between alternatives rather than execute an instruction.

The result demonstrates a neural architecture for internally selected attention. It does not determine whether the selecting controller is wholly physical, emergent, event-causal, compatibilist, or substance-agent-causal.

Nadra and Mangun similarly provide evidence that ongoing neural activity biases later attentional choices and that internally chosen attention recruits additional frontal structures. That finding bears at least as naturally on the neural antecedents of experienced choice as on an external selector acting upon them. [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/cognition/articles/10.3389/fcogn.2023.1205618/text)

### First-person experience does not “confirm” the ontological mapping

The article says, in effect, that first-person effort confirms the proposed network mapping. Phenomenology can help identify when subjects report deliberation, conflict, effort, or control. It cannot by itself confirm that the mapped neural process is being directed by a distinct substance.

At most, the joint evidence supports:

> Experiences of effortful attentional choice covary with a particular extended neural control process.

The additional conclusion—

> a nonphysical conscious agent selects which physical possibility is realised—

requires independent discriminating evidence or a much stronger inference to the best explanation.

## 8. The motor evidence removes simple objections but also supports rivals

### Thura and Cisek

Thura and Cisek identified neural dynamics preceding commitment in a monkey reach-decision task, with a candidate commitment point roughly 280 milliseconds before movement. The “moment of commitment” language is tentative, task-specific, and embedded in a recurrent competitive-attractor model. [Cisek+1](https://www.cisek.org/pavel/Pubs/ThuraCisek2014.pdf)

The study is useful for rejecting an excessively simple picture in which a single early signal irrevocably determines action. It instead supports layered, revisable motor commitment. But its authors provide a physical dynamical account of that process. It does not identify a nonphysical settling event, and its species, task, and operational criterion should be stated.

### Schurger

Describing Schurger’s readiness-potential account as showing that the readiness potential is a “noise artefact” is too strong. The proposal is that time-locking and averaging stochastic neural fluctuations around threshold crossings can generate the familiar readiness-potential profile in arbitrary-timing tasks. The underlying activity is real; the reinterpretation concerns what the averaged waveform signifies about an early unconscious decision. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22869750/)

This weakens one Libet-style argument for early determination. It does not positively support agent causation.

### Sjöberg

Sjöberg’s discussion of supplementary motor area resection is relevant because patients can retain reports of wanting and trying while initiation or execution is impaired. This puts pressure on identifying conscious willing with activity in one motor area. But it is a short clinical and interpretive argument, not a controlled demonstration that conscious effort is causally independent of the brain. [OUP Academic](https://academic.oup.com/brain/article/147/7/2267/7685995)

Again, it removes a simplistic localisation claim rather than establishing the Map’s ontology.

### Desmurget is adverse evidence as well as a dissociation

The article correctly notes that electrical stimulation can dissociate experienced intention from movement. But it underplays the direction of the effect. Parietal stimulation produced desires or intentions to move and, at higher stimulation, reports that movement had occurred despite the absence of corresponding muscular activity. Premotor stimulation could produce movement without awareness of having moved. [Science](https://www.science.org/doi/pdf/10.1126/science.1169896)

This supports the claim that intention, movement, and awareness are separable processes. It also shows that direct physical intervention in the brain can generate the phenomenology of intending. That is naturally read as evidence for brain-to-experience causation and should be presented as an adverse interpretation, not merely as evidence that conscious willing is not identical to motor output.

### Overall evidential grade

The motor literature supports:

- distributed and revisable action preparation;
- dissociations among intention, awareness, initiation, and execution;
- the failure of crude “readiness potential equals completed unconscious decision” arguments;
- a distinction between action control and final muscular output.

It does not currently discriminate agent causation from sophisticated physical control models. The article itself partly acknowledges this, but the cumulative rhetoric of “convergent anchors” still gives the studies more probative weight than its formal caveats allow.

## 9. Mental effort is a compatibility datum, not yet a discriminator

The linked Mental Effort article is more cautious than the present article. It acknowledges dissociations between felt effort and executive demand, problems with the classic ego-depletion literature, opportunity-cost and process-based accounts, and the fact that some dopamine studies concern willingness to choose demanding tasks rather than the phenomenal intensity of effort. [mental-effort](/concepts/mental-effort/)

It also admits that psychotherapy-related neuroplasticity is compatible with ordinary learning and Hebbian adaptation, and that the frequently cited obsessive-compulsive disorder evidence is small and not a replicated discriminator between dualist and materialist accounts. [mental-effort](/concepts/mental-effort/)

The Agent Causation article nevertheless uses effort as though it independently supports a nonphysical intervention. That is not warranted. Felt effort may track conflict, uncertainty, expected value, fatigue, metacognitive inference, control allocation, or prediction error. A physical controller can expend computational and metabolic resources while producing exactly the reported phenomenology.

The often-cited contrast between the brain’s very high sensory throughput and a much narrower conscious report channel likewise does not identify an interface. A bottleneck can arise from serial report, working-memory limitations, action selection, global broadcasting, or decision constraints. The linked article treats the bottleneck as suggestive, but it cannot carry an ontological inference. [mental-effort](/concepts/mental-effort/)

The proper evidential status is:

> Mental effort is part of the phenomenon any adequate account of agency must explain. It is compatible with agent causation but does not presently discriminate it from physical control architectures.

## 10. Tenet-protective bracketing is only partly controlled

The article commendably labels many assumptions. Nevertheless, its total architecture creates a stack of protections:

- substance agency is said to be downstream rather than essential;
- if pre-decoherence neural coherence fails, selection can be moved post-decoherence;
- if no direct trace is observed, the causal-interface void explains why the interface is hidden;
- if one empirical anchor fails, the others remain;
- rejection of Many-Worlds supplies global exclusion;
- Occam’s razor is restricted so that lack of parsimony cannot veto the ontology;
- if the quantum implementation fails, the philosophical sourcehood argument can survive.

Each move may be defensible in isolation. Together, they risk making the theory capable of accommodating almost any result while exposing no central proposition to decisive pressure.

The article needs a visible distinction between:

- **tenet-relative coherence:** follows or fits once the Map’s commitments are granted;
- **empirical compatibility:** the observation is not inconsistent with the model;
- **comparative support:** the observation is more likely under this model than under rivals;
- **mechanistic specification:** the model states how the causal operation occurs.

Most of the article’s “anchors” fall into the first two categories.

The anchors are also not independent. Attention, effort, psychotherapy, and motor-selection evidence all concern overlapping forms of executive control and neuroplastic adaptation. Treating them as separate converging lines risks multiplying measurements of the same underdetermined phenomenon. Cross-cultural or historical recurrence of agent-like self-conceptions may show that the conception is cognitively natural; it does not establish its metaphysical truth. The argument from reason, even if successful against epiphenomenalism, supports the causal efficacy of rational thought more directly than it supports primitive substance causation. Creativity bears on option production more clearly than on final token selection.

## 11. The advertised falsifiers are too weak or insufficiently differential

Several proposed falsifiers would not discriminate the Map from serious alternatives.

- **Perfect prediction:** Physicalism does not imply practical or even in-principle prediction of complex choices. Stochastic, chaotic, computationally irreducible, or informationally inaccessible physical systems may remain unpredictable. Conversely, agent-causal behaviour could be highly predictable when reasons are decisive.
- **Failure of long-lived neural coherence:** This eliminates only one pre-decoherence implementation, especially because the article already permits post-decoherence selection.
- **Effort tracking difficulty:** Ordinary physical control theories predict this relationship.
- **Different neural signatures for willed and instructed attention:** Physicalism predicts additional decision and conflict-processing in the willed condition.
- **A materialist solution to consciousness:** This would challenge the site’s dualism generally rather than specifically falsify agent causation.
- **A Many-Worlds account of authorship:** This would be a major theoretical competitor, but “demonstrating” a philosophically adequate conception of authorship is not a clean empirical falsifier.

A useful falsifier must specify a result that has importantly different likelihoods under the Map and its best rivals. Examples might include:

- a preregistered relation between reported intentions and quantum outcomes not explainable by ordinary neural predictors;
- a formal selector model that predicts a distinctive conditional distribution while preserving or modifying the Born rule;
- a neural intervention that leaves every proposed physical control variable unchanged while systematically altering conscious selection;
- a subject–brain pairing prediction in split, duplicated, transferred, or externally coupled systems;
- a measured violation of a particular causal Markov or intervention structure implied by physical closure.

These would be difficult experiments, but difficulty is not a reason to substitute generic control phenomena as falsifiers.

## 13. Overall assessment

The article’s strongest defensible claim is:

> Agent causation is a coherent way of interpreting sourcehood within the Map’s prior dualist and single-outcome commitments. It represents the conscious subject as the settler of physically open alternatives and thereby supplies an attributional answer to the luck objection.

Its evidence does not presently warrant the stronger claims that:

- a substance agent is required for genuine purposive control;
- event-causal accounts merely describe happenings rather than agents acting;
- Kane’s hybrid model is converging on dualist substance causation;
- attention or motor data favour a nonphysical selector;
- effort is an observable mark of downward causation;
- locating selection around decoherence supplies a mechanism;
- agent causation dissolves the contrastive luck objection;
- the theory currently has differential empirical support over physicalist rivals.

The article should be retained, but reclassified and rewritten as a **framework-position essay with an explicit research debt**, not presented as a convergent evidential case. Its candour in the positions register provides a good basis for that revision.
