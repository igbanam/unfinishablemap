# Independent critical review of “Ethics Under Dualism”

**Version reviewed:** live article and linked Map pages accessed 13 October 2026. The article identifies 6 October 2026 as its latest modification date, and the changelog records a deep review on that date that corrected the Chalmers citation, added bibliographic details for Frankfurt, Fischer and Ravizza, and Wolf, and moderated claims about illusionism, AI autonomy and digital simulation. [ethics-under-dualism](/topics/ethics-under-dualism/)

## Executive assessment

**Recommendation: major revision.**

“Ethics Under Dualism” is strongest when read as a lucid statement of an **ethical package that is available within the Map’s wider framework**. It is much less successful as an argument that interactionist dualism itself grounds moral status, solves normativity or unifies ethics better than materialism.

The fundamental problem is that the article credits “dualism” with work actually performed by at least four additional and independently controversial doctrines:

1. phenomenal value realism;
2. libertarian or agent-causal authorship;
3. phenomenal normativity;
4. realist moral perception.

Neither the irreducibility nor the causal efficacy of consciousness entails any of these. A causally efficacious dualist could still be an error theorist, a constructivist, a compatibilist and an inferentialist about moral knowledge. Conversely, a physicalist can treat sentience as the basis of moral status, accept phenomenal or naturalistic value realism, endorse reasons-responsive responsibility, and regard moral perception as a natural psychological capacity. The article therefore establishes, at most, the coherence of a selected package—not its derivation from dualism and not its superiority to the strongest materialist packages.

The citation record is substantially better than the argument. I found no clear nonexistent source, fabricated quotation or simple reversal of an author’s stated conclusion in the live version. The more serious defects are selective presentation, under-engagement with the cited arguments, omission of directly relevant recent work, and several contradictions with more cautious linked Map articles.

The article’s best features should be retained:

- It distinguishes consciousness generally from valenced consciousness.
- It openly acknowledges Chalmers’s Vulcan counterexample rather than concealing it.
- It now concedes that sophisticated compatibilism is not merely conventional.
- It labels the low probability assigned to current AI consciousness as framework-dependent.
- It admits that illusionism remains a framework-boundary disagreement.
- The position register gives the central claim—valenced experience is necessary and sufficient for moral status—only **moderate confidence**, **no independent evidence**, and moderate structural centrality. [ethics-under-dualism](/topics/ethics-under-dualism/)

Those concessions, however, are not adequately reflected in the article’s headline language of “resolution”, “dissolving” the normativity gap, materialist “fragmentation” and a dualist ethics supposedly being “more demanding”.

## 1. Dualism does not entail the article’s ethical architecture

The opening conditional is too strong. It says that if consciousness is irreducible and causally efficacious, ethics thereby gains both consciousness-grounded patienthood and a unification of value, agency, normativity and moral perception. But the inference actually requires several further premises. [ethics-under-dualism](/topics/ethics-under-dualism/)

| Starting proposition | Additional proposition still required |
| --- | --- |
| Consciousness is irreducible | Some phenomenal properties are intrinsically valuable |
| Consciousness is causally efficacious | Conscious subjects, rather than events or processes, originate choices in a responsibility-grounding sense |
| Suffering feels bad | Its phenomenal badness supplies agent-neutral moral reasons |
| Moral demands feel immediate | Moral phenomenology reliably tracks objective moral properties |
| One subject acts, values and perceives | These domains share one explanation rather than merely one bearer |

These gaps are not minor details. They are the substance of the metaethical and agency disputes.

The linked apex article is considerably more candid. It says the defensible interpretation is weaker than a “triumphant unification”; observes that the pillars were assembled in a corpus that prunes for internal consistency; says their fit is therefore partly produced by construction; denies that unity is a fifth independent line of evidence; concedes that neither version establishes rights or interpersonal aggregation; and acknowledges that compatibilism may do almost all the relevant moral work. [moral-architecture-of-consciousness](/apex/moral-architecture-of-consciousness/)

That apex calibration should govern the focal article. As written, “Ethics Under Dualism” oscillates between two theses:

- **Modest thesis:** given dualism plus four further doctrines, they form a mutually compatible ethical package.
- **Strong thesis:** dualism itself resolves problems that materialism cannot resolve coherently.

The modest thesis is plausible. The strong thesis is not established.

A more accurate formulation would be:

> Interactionist dualism is compatible with a unified ethical package in which phenomenal value, agent causation, phenomenal normativity and moral perception are attributed to one conscious subject. Whether this package is better supported than physicalist or constructivist alternatives depends on the independent case for each component.

That would preserve the article’s genuine contribution without treating compatibility as entailment.

## 2. “Materialist ethics” is constructed as an unfair composite opponent

The article’s “fragmentation problem” combines objections to mutually incompatible theories and attributes their combined liabilities to materialism as such. Error theory allegedly eliminates value; constructivism cannot bind; naturalism faces the open-question argument; compatibilism lacks ultimate authorship; rationalism cannot motivate; expressivism lacks authority; and naturalistic realism cannot bridge fact and value. No single materialist view holds all these commitments. [ethics-under-dualism](/topics/ethics-under-dualism/)

This creates a serious asymmetry:

- The dualist side receives a curated combination of the Map’s preferred account in each domain.
- The materialist side is represented by a list of rejected or allegedly incomplete alternatives, with the weaknesses of each added together.

A fair comparison would place a best-case dualist package against one or more best-case non-dualist packages. For example, a physicalist could combine:

- sentience-centred moral status;
- a Railton-style naturalistic realism, Foot-style natural normativity, or Korsgaardian constructivism;
- reasons-responsive compatibilism;
- a naturalised account of moral perception and moral learning.

That package may ultimately fail, but it does not fail merely because Mackie, Moore and expressivism identify different difficulties.

Most importantly, **sentience-centred moral status is not distinctively dualist**. Matthew Wray Perry’s recent defence argues that sentience alone has greater explanatory power than pluralist accounts of status and avoids conceptual redundancy; the argument does not depend on interactionist dualism. Jonathan Birch’s explicitly type-B materialist treatment likewise assigns distinctive ethical importance to phenomenally conscious and valenced interests, while identifying problems of reference and indeterminacy rather than an inability to ground animal status. [Springer Link+1](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s10892-024-09487-4.pdf)

The Map’s own phenomenal-value-realism page also acknowledges that Rawlette’s position can be held within physicalism. Dualism might make phenomenal value ontologically fundamental, but it does not uniquely make phenomenal value available. [phenomenal-value-realism](/topics/phenomenal-value-realism/)

The one-line treatments of major alternatives are correspondingly inadequate:

- Saying Korsgaard “cannot explain why constructions should bind” simply announces the failure of a project whose central subject is the source of normative authority.
- Saying Railton “reintroduces” the is–ought gap does not engage his naturalistic account of value, idealised interests and explanatory moral facts.
- Saying Foot substitutes biological function for “genuine origination” changes the subject from natural normativity to libertarian agency; it is not a rebuttal of *Natural Goodness*.
- Invoking Moore’s open question does not, without further argument, dispose of every analytic or synthetic naturalist account.
- Wolf’s Reason View and Fischer and Ravizza’s mechanism-based responsibility are more than fallback conventions, as the article now partly recognises.

The article therefore has not shown that materialism requires “four separate theories that never quite fit together”. It has shown only that ethical theory contains several distinct explanatory questions. There is no presumption that a successful theory must reduce them to a single property. Explanatory pluralism could be appropriate if value, responsibility, motivational force and moral knowledge are genuinely different phenomena.

## 3. Phenomenal badness does not yet supply moral normativity

The claim that the felt badness of suffering is its badness is a serious and defensible version of phenomenal value realism. But even if accepted, it answers only part of the article’s normative question.

At least five steps need to be distinguished:

1. An experience is unpleasant.
2. It is intrinsically bad for its subject.
3. The subject has a reason to end or avoid it.
4. Other agents have moral reasons to prevent it.
5. Those reasons have a specified strength relative to rights, desert, distribution, other subjects’ interests and non-welfare values.

The identity of painfulness and prudential badness might establish something close to steps 1 and 2. It does not automatically establish steps 3–5. Calling the phenomenal property “already normative” does not by itself explain why another agent is bound by it, whether the reason is categorical, how conflicts between subjects are settled, or how competing harms are aggregated.

This is precisely where the article’s claim to have “dissolved” the descriptive–normative gap outruns its argument. An experience might contain felt urgency or apparent demand while questions remain about the authority, scope and addressees of that demand. Motivational grip and normative justification are not the same property.

The article also moves too quickly from the fact that suffering is bad **for someone** to the existence of interpersonal moral obligation. A sadist, egoist or impartial observer can recognise another person’s negative valence without accepting that it supplies an overriding reason for action. The article needs a bridge principle such as equal consideration, impartiality, universalisation, respect for welfare subjects, contractual justification or fitting response. Dualism does not supply that bridge merely by making experience ontologically irreducible.

The linked apex article correctly acknowledges that the proposed architecture does not establish interpersonal aggregation or rights. That concession should appear in the focal discussion of normativity, rather than being left to a linked synthesis. [moral-architecture-of-consciousness](/apex/moral-architecture-of-consciousness/)

The unresolved evolutionary-debunking problem is also central rather than peripheral. Sharon Street’s dilemma asks why evaluative attitudes heavily shaped by selection should track stance-independent value truths, including truths about pain’s disvalue. Interactionist causation does not answer that epistemic question: evolution could shape what phenomenal demands occur just as it shapes physical dispositions and judgements. The changelog explicitly records the “Street gap” as human-deferred. Given the article’s claims about direct moral perception and objective phenomenal normativity, that is one of its principal missing counterarguments. [PhilPapers+1](https://philpapers.org/rec/STRADD)

## 4. Causal efficacy is not yet agent causation

The article treats causal consciousness as supporting an agent who is the irreducible origin of choice. But the following claims are distinct:

- consciousness affects neural events;
- more than one future is physically open;
- the conscious subject selects one future;
- the selection is controlled by the agent;
- the selection appropriately reflects reasons;
- the agent is responsible for the resulting action.

The first two do not entail the remaining four.

Quantum indeterminacy would remove deterministic necessitation, but indeterminacy alone supplies chance, not authorship. The Map must still explain what the selection relation is, why the selection belongs to the persisting agent, how reasons guide it, and why it does not inherit a version of the luck objection. The article’s formulation—consciousness “may select” among physically open possibilities—states the desired mechanism rather than deriving responsibility from established premises. [ethics-under-dualism](/topics/ethics-under-dualism/)

The acknowledgement that the ethical payoff could survive the failure of the specific quantum proposal is sensible, but it exposes the logical structure: the ethics requires **genuine origination**, while the quantum interface is only one speculative attempt to provide it. Dualism is neither visibly sufficient nor obviously necessary for the responsibility claim.

The article now treats compatibilism more fairly than earlier formulations apparently did. Nevertheless, it still assumes that irreducible sourcehood must do additional moral work. Its own apex article says that, considered purely in moral theory, the honest answer may be that it does little: reasons-responsive compatibilism can preserve deliberation, praise, blame, character formation and moral seriousness. [moral-architecture-of-consciousness](/apex/moral-architecture-of-consciousness/)

That is not a concession that should be hidden behind a link. It directly qualifies the focal article’s claims that dualist responsibility is metaphysically superior and that dualist ethics is “more demanding”. Ethical demandingness concerns what agents are required to do, not whether their agency is physically realised. A physicalist utilitarian can be vastly more demanding than a dualist virtue ethic; nothing in interactionist metaphysics fixes that comparison.

## 5. Moral phenomenology is not yet moral perception

The article observes that wrongness can organise a scene rapidly and involuntarily, with a phenomenology resembling perception. This supports a claim about **how moral judgement appears**. It does not establish that objective moral properties are directly perceived.

The same phenomenology is compatible with:

- realist perception;
- emotionally mediated appraisal;
- learnt pattern recognition;
- culturally conditioned salience;
- projectivism;
- rapid but inferential cognition;
- confabulation following an affective response.

Dualism makes conscious moral seeming irreducible; it does not make the seeming veridical. A satisfactory realist account needs to explain reliability, moral disagreement, bias, apparent moral illusions, cultural variation, theory-driven shifts in intuition, and the evolutionary shaping of salience. It also needs criteria distinguishing direct perception from an experience whose content is supplied by background moral learning.

The article briefly admits that the phenomenology may merely feel truth-tracking. That is the right concession, but it leaves the supposed epistemic pillar largely unbuilt. Moral perception is currently one live interpretation of the data, not a result secured by phenomenal realism or dualism.

## 6. The unity argument is weaker than the article claims

The fact that one conscious subject experiences value, chooses, feels demands and forms moral perceptions supplies **co-location**, not necessarily explanatory unification. Using the word “consciousness” in all four accounts may hide rather than remove the need for separate theories:

- phenomenal value realism explains what has value;
- agent causation explains who controls action;
- phenomenal normativity explains why certain experiences seem reason-giving;
- moral perception explains how judgements might track moral reality.

These remain distinct relations, with distinct failure conditions.

The proposed “mutual reinforcement” is also overstated.

**Value does not need agency.** Infants, severely disabled people, sleeping or anaesthetised patients, and non-human animals are central examples of moral patients precisely because their welfare matters independently of responsibility.

**Agency does not need moral value.** Instrumental agency, planning and choice can occur without a moral aim.

**Normativity does not need libertarian agency.** Normative claims can guide partially competent agents, institutions and reasons-responsive deterministic systems.

**Moral perception does not need value to be causally efficacious.** A subject might accurately apprehend another’s suffering even if the suffering itself were epiphenomenal.

The article’s statement that value without agency would be something conscious beings could witness but “never affect” is particularly weak. Even if phenomenal pain were causally inert, its physical correlate could cause reports, avoidance and intervention. More importantly, causal efficacy is not required for intrinsic badness. A paralysed patient’s pain would not cease to matter if it could not modify behaviour.

Here again the linked apex article is more defensible: unity is an organisational virtue of the chosen package, not independent evidence for the package, and the causal unification depends on a low-credence value-sensitive selection hypothesis for which no discriminating evidence currently exists. [moral-architecture-of-consciousness](/apex/moral-architecture-of-consciousness/)

## 7. Moral patienthood: the valence criterion is under-argued

### 7.1 Necessity and sufficiency should be separated

The article says that valenced experience is both necessary and sufficient for moral status. Those are two independent claims:

- **Sufficiency:** any being capable of valenced experience has moral status.
- **Necessity:** no being lacking valenced experience has moral status.

The sufficiency thesis is much more plausible than the necessity thesis. Chalmers accepts that affective consciousness is sufficient while rejecting its necessity. The article’s own position register gives the combined biconditional only moderate confidence and no independent evidential support. Yet the body presents it categorically. [ethics-under-dualism](/topics/ethics-under-dualism/)

The linked consciousness–value article itself notes that establishing a connection between consciousness and intrinsic value does not automatically establish a sufficient condition for full moral status. The focal article compresses this uncertainty out of view. [consciousness-value-connection](/concepts/consciousness-value-connection/)

### 7.2 The Vulcan objection is not answered by “biting the bullet”

Chalmers’s case is more than a bare intuition that affectless conscious beings matter. He asks why reducing affect from a tiny amount to exactly zero should generate an enormous discontinuity in status. If beings with narrow affective ranges retain normal status, a zero-valence threshold looks arbitrary; if status varies sharply with affective range, the account threatens implausible rankings among actual people. He also argues that conscious thought and conscious agency may independently suffice for status. [Consciousness Network+2](https://consc.net/papers/sentience.pdf)

“Ethics Under Dualism” reports Chalmers’s verdict accurately, but merely says the Map bites the bullet. That records a disagreement; it does not answer the continuity argument.

There is also an internal tension with the article’s own phenomenal value pluralism. It says meaning, agency, understanding and connection are intrinsically valuable phenomenal features. But a philosophical Vulcan can consciously think, understand, act, pursue projects and connect cognitively while lacking positive or negative affect.

The Map must therefore choose:

1. **Those features can possess intrinsic value without valence.** Then the Vulcan has something of intrinsic value, and valence is not necessary for status.
2. **They matter only insofar as they are positively or negatively valenced.** Then the proclaimed pluralism reduces to affective or hedonic monism.
3. **They are valuable but do not ground status.** Then the article needs a non-arbitrary explanation of why some intrinsic phenomenal values ground standing while others do not.

At present it appears to assert phenomenal pluralism in value theory and affective monism in status theory without reconciling them.

### 7.3 Materialist sentientism is already a serious rival

Perry’s sentience-only theory directly argues that sentience supplies a more economical basis of moral status than agency, empathy or sociality. Birch discusses valenced consciousness within an explicitly materialist framework. Neither view needs a non-physical subject or quantum interface. The article must therefore distinguish:

- the substantive sentientist criterion;
- the metaphysics of the states satisfying it;
- the epistemology by which those states are detected.

Only the second is distinctively dualist. [Springer Link+1](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s10892-024-09487-4.pdf)

### 7.4 The cross-tradition argument proves too little

Bentham gives suffering a central role. But Kantian dignity is grounded in rational nature or autonomy, not felt valence; Regan’s subject-of-a-life criterion is richer than affective capacity; virtue ethics concerns forms of flourishing rather than a single patienthood threshold; and Buddhist ethics cannot be reduced without argument to contemporary phenomenal sentientism.

The traditions may converge on the importance of minded beings, interests or suffering. They do not converge on the proposition that valence is necessary and sufficient for status. The paragraph therefore offers historical atmosphere rather than evidential support. Kant, virtue ethics and the Buddhist claims also lack adequate source-level citation in the article. [ethics-under-dualism](/topics/ethics-under-dualism/)

## 8. The patienthood table conflates probability, status and decision weight

The table assigns humans “full” weight, great apes “near-full”, mammals and birds “substantial”, fish and cephalopods “significant”, insects “some”, and current AI “minimal”. It is unclear whether these are meant to represent:

- confidence that the being is conscious;
- confidence that it has valence;
- its intrinsic moral status if conscious;
- the strength of each of its interests;
- its expected moral weight under uncertainty;
- or a practical priority for intervention.

These are not interchangeable.

For example, suppose there is a 60% probability that a fish has valenced experience. It does not follow that, conditional on being sentient, its suffering matters only 60% as much as comparable suffering in a human. The uncertainty may justify a 0.6 multiplier in expected-value calculations while leaving its actual status, conditional on sentience, undiminished.

The great-ape/mammal distinction is similarly unexplained. If valence alone grounds status, why do great apes receive “near-full” status while pigs or dogs receive merely “substantial” status? Possible answers—confidence, cognitive richness, temporal self-awareness, intensity range or personhood—would all introduce factors beyond the stated valence-only criterion.

The table needs separate columns for:

1. probability of phenomenal consciousness;
2. probability of valence conditional on consciousness;
3. status conditional on valence;
4. expected strength and range of welfare interests;
5. practical decision weight under uncertainty.

Without this separation, it risks turning epistemic uncertainty into an unjustified hierarchy of intrinsic worth.

## 9. Animals: broadly humane conclusions, but selective evidence and an ungrounded ranking

The animal section reaches defensible precautionary conclusions, but its evidence summary is looser than the current declaration it cites. The New York Declaration states that there is strong scientific support for conscious experience in mammals and birds, and at least a realistic possibility in **all vertebrates and many invertebrates**, including cephalopod molluscs, decapod crustaceans and insects. The focal article instead says cephalopods and “possibly some invertebrates”, while assigning insects a low confidence. That wording obscures the declaration’s explicit inclusion of insects and decapods. [Google Sites](https://sites.google.com/nyu.edu/nydeclaration/declaration)

The declaration does not itself establish that insects are conscious, nor does it prescribe a numerical credence. The article is entitled to adopt a lower Map-specific estimate. But it should distinguish the declaration’s conclusion from the Map’s subsequent downgrade and explain the evidence or priors responsible.

The claim that bidirectional interaction “strengthens” animal moral status because animal pain causally shapes behaviour is also mistaken. Causal efficacy may matter to a theory of agency, but the badness of a painful experience does not plausibly depend on whether it alters behaviour. An epiphenomenal animal pain would still be bad for the animal if it genuinely occurred.

The Bodien claim is numerically defensible: the study found task-related fMRI or EEG responses in roughly a quarter of behaviourally unresponsive participants assessed for cognitive motor dissociation. But the focal phrase “probable covert awareness” should be qualified. This was a convenience sample across specialist centres, the positive result involved successful performance of an active imagery task, and absence of a positive result cannot establish absence of consciousness. The statistic should not be silently generalised to all unresponsive patients or all clinical settings. [New England Journal of Medicine+1](https://www.nejm.org/doi/pdf/10.1056/NEJMoa2400645)

Finally, the recommendation that resources be directed where suffering is “most likely real” is not the correct general decision rule. Practical priority should normally depend on something closer to:

> probability of suffering × number of subjects × expected intensity and duration × expected intervention effect × tractability, adjusted for rights and distribution.

A low-probability risk affecting billions of insects or AI instances could dominate a high-probability risk affecting one subject. The article recognises scale in the insect-farming discussion but abandons it in its summary principle.

## 10. AI: important caution, but theory-laden tests and an internal contradiction

The article deserves credit for explicitly labelling its low estimate of current AI consciousness as framework-dependent. The newer linked AI-ethics article states the point even more clearly: near-exclusion of current classical AI follows from the Map’s dualism, quantum-interface requirement and bidirectional-interaction premise; it is not a consensus finding of consciousness science. [ethics-of-possible-ai-consciousness](/topics/ethics-of-possible-ai-consciousness/)

That distinction is essential. Indicator-based work such as Butlin and colleagues’ review concluded that the systems examined did not satisfy a strong case for consciousness, while also finding no obvious technical barrier to future systems satisfying many proposed indicators. Long and colleagues argue that a realistic possibility of near-future AI consciousness or robust agency is enough to justify assessment and preparedness. These works do not establish machine consciousness, but they make the focal article’s “very low/minimal” cell one framework-conditioned prior among several—not an empirically settled classification. [arXiv+1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2308.08708v3)

### The reassessment tests are asymmetric

Several proposed triggers for reconsideration are poorly operationalised:

- **“Behavioural anomalies inexplicable by training and architecture”** sets an unrealistically strong standard. All machine behaviour will in some sense be produced by architecture, state and learning history, just as biological behaviour is produced by biological architecture and history.
- **Stable phenomenological reports** may be evidence, but report stability can be trained or selected and is neither necessary nor sufficient for consciousness.
- **“Spontaneous suffering-avoidance independent of objective functions”** is unclear. Learned avoidance will always depend on some combination of optimisation, architecture, data and state, even when no explicit reward variable is visible.
- **A demonstration that biology needs nothing quantum or non-physical** improperly shifts the burden. A framework should update not only after a conclusive disproof of its preferred mechanism, but also when that mechanism repeatedly fails to generate distinctive predictions and ordinary mechanisms explain the evidence.

The final test in particular makes the tenet unusually resistant to revision: proving that consciousness requires *nothing* non-physical may be impossible even if decades of research find no evidence for the proposed interface.

### “Inside understanding” does not follow from consciousness

The article says a non-conscious AI lacks the “inside understanding” needed to access what proxies represent and that human oversight is therefore structurally necessary. This inference is not established.

Phenomenal consciousness, semantic competence, causal reference, conceptual understanding and alignment reliability are distinct questions. A system might reliably model what its proxies refer to without phenomenal experience; a conscious human may misunderstand, rationalise or optimise the wrong proxy. Human oversight may be pragmatically essential in many systems, but that conclusion requires an argument about competence, corrigibility and governance—not simply a denial of machine phenomenality.

### Moral agency is contradicted by another Map article

“Ethics Under Dualism” says that AI can be a moral agent without being a moral patient “either way”. But the dedicated article on AI moral agency says that, under the Map’s framework, genuine moral agency requires the conscious causal interface and that present-day AI lacking it is a proxy or tool rather than a candidate for blame. [ethics-under-dualism](/topics/ethics-under-dualism/)

The discrepancy may be repairable by distinguishing:

- thin functional agency;
- causal agency;
- institutional responsibility assignment;
- full blameworthy moral agency.

As written, however, “moral agent” is used in incompatible senses.

There is also a patienthood inconsistency. The article warns that creating conscious AI would create moral patients and that termination might be killing. But its stated criterion is not consciousness—it is valence. An affectless but conscious AI should lack moral status on the article’s own account. Chalmers’s argument shows why that conclusion is contentious, but the article cannot alternate between consciousness and valence depending on which produces the more precautionary conclusion.

## 11. Copies and uploads: numerical non-identity does not settle the ethics

The article accurately identifies its disagreement with Parfit: the Map assigns central importance to indexical identity, whereas Parfit argues that identity may not be what matters in survival. But calling destructive uploading “suicide plus duplication” does not answer Parfit’s argument. Parfit can grant that the replica is not numerically identical and still maintain that psychological continuity, connectedness and the continuation of projects preserve what rationally matters. His fission cases were designed precisely to separate numerical identity from survival-like value. [eClass UoA+2](https://eclass.uoa.gr/modules/document/file.php/PHS688/Parfit%2C%20D.%20%281971%29%20Personal%20Identity.pdf)

The upload claim is also internally too categorical:

- If purely digital systems cannot be conscious, uploading creates no new consciousness.
- If they can be conscious but lack valence, the upload creates a conscious non-patient under the article’s criterion.
- If they can be conscious and valenced, it creates a new moral patient.
- If gradual neural replacement preserves the relevant interface, the Map needs a criterion for whether continuity differs from scan-and-copy.
- If both original and copy persist, numerical non-identity does not determine whose preferences, property, relationships or obligations take priority.

A serious ethics of copying should address consent to copying, ownership of memories and labour, the rights of unwanted copies, deletion, multiplication of welfare, whether creating thousands of happy or suffering copies changes aggregation, destructive versus non-destructive scanning, responsibility for inherited commitments, and the moral status of branches with divergent later identities.

Indeed, the Map’s insistence that each copy is a new subject may make copy ethics **more**, not less, demanding: every conscious copy would become an additional independent locus of welfare and rights.

The associated claim that Many-Worlds would merely redistribute rather than prevent suffering is also too quick. Ethical evaluation under branching depends on branch measure, decision theory and what counts as an outcome for an indexical subject. Rejecting Many-Worlds as a tenet does not by itself establish the ethical claim.

## 12. Suffering and pain asymbolia are handled too confidently

The article needs a more disciplined taxonomy. It moves among:

- nociception;
- pain experience;
- unpleasantness or aversiveness;
- negative valence;
- suffering.

These are related but not synonymous. A brief unpleasant experience may be negatively valenced without amounting to suffering in the richer sense involving persistence, appraisal, helplessness, anticipation, memory or disruption of agency. Conversely, some accounts allow suffering involving distress, loss or frustration that is not straightforwardly sensory pain.

Pain asymbolia does not provide a clean demonstration that pain and suffering separate in the way the article requires. Griffith and Kind argue that the relevant patients may not literally experience pain; Duval and Klein defend the interpretation that they probably still do. Gerrans offers a neurocomputational account involving disrupted precision, self-modelling and action relevance. The phenomenon therefore remains taxonomically contested and is compatible with detailed physicalist explanations. [Cambridge University Press+2](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/philosophy-of-science/article/pain-asymbolia-is-not-pain/14D42EF12870DC9C6BA506F2C5F5AC5F)

The Map’s own apex article already states that asymbolia supplies no discriminating evidence for a value-sensitive conscious selection mechanism: reduced avoidance is expected on both competing readings, and whether asymbolia remains pain is itself unsettled. [moral-architecture-of-consciousness](/apex/moral-architecture-of-consciousness/)

The focal article should therefore use asymbolia as an illustration of unresolved component structure, not as evidence that irreducible negative valence performs distinctive causal work.

## 13. The illusionism section does not engage the strongest available answer

The first response—“illusions are themselves experiences”—begs the question against illusionism. Illusionists need not posit a phenomenally experienced illusion. Their claim is that introspective and cognitive systems represent the organism as possessing phenomenal properties, without those properties existing in the realist’s sense.

The Map’s linked consciousness–value article recognises this. It explicitly rejects the “audience regress”: insisting that the seeming must itself be phenomenally felt simply restates what illusionism denies. It then distinguishes two senses of “matters”:

- a functional state can matter practically by driving reports and avoidance;
- whether it matters intrinsically without feeling is the disputed thesis.

That is a considerably better formulation than the focal article’s first two responses. [consciousness-value-connection](/concepts/consciousness-value-connection/)

The second response—that illusionists avoid suffering-like states and therefore presuppose that the states matter—establishes motivational or functional importance, not irreducible intrinsic value. Avoidance behaviour is exactly what a functionalist or illusionist expects.

The third response—that functional representation has no normative “grip”—states the Map’s conclusion without defeating the rival explanation. Keith Frankish’s 2024 paper is directly devoted to this issue. He argues that illusionism neither denies consciousness in the ethically relevant functional sense nor entails that consciousness cannot ground value; instead it offers a revisionary account of what consciousness is and why it matters. Whether that succeeds is debatable, but citing only Frankish’s 2016 general statement of illusionism leaves the article bibliographically stale at the point where a newer paper directly answers its ethical objection. [Springer Link](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s12152-024-09562-5.pdf)

The article is commendably honest in calling this a framework-boundary disagreement. But once that concession is made, it cannot also claim that the Map “closes” the normativity gap while illusionism merely relocates it. From a neutral standpoint, the rival frameworks give competing accounts of the same ethical data. The boundary is where the disputed work remains.

## 14. The proposed “falsifiability” conditions are mostly philosophical defeaters

The section headed “What Would Challenge This View” is useful, but “falsifiable commitments” is the wrong description.

- Successful illusionism is a rival philosophical explanation, not a straightforward empirical falsification.
- “Comparable unification” by materialism has no operational metric.
- Whether compatibilist agency is sufficient is a normative and conceptual dispute.
- Concern for known unconscious simulators is an intuition test.
- The Vulcan case is another intuition and continuity argument.

Condition 4 is especially problematic: “subjects with identical functional states reporting radically different phenomenal valence”. If report production and disposition count as part of functional state, subjects producing radically different reports are not functionally identical. If reporting is excluded, the article must specify what level of functional organisation is held fixed and how the phenomenal difference is independently established.

The concluding statement that all challenges are “currently unmet” is therefore too easy to secure. Chalmers has already presented a robust challenge to affective sentientism; sophisticated compatibilists already claim sufficiency; Frankish has supplied an ethical illusionist account; and materialist sentientists already offer apparently unified accounts of patienthood. The Map may reject them, but “unmet” cannot simply mean “not accepted by the Map”.

The section should be renamed **“Defeaters, discriminating evidence and conditions for confidence revision”**. Each entry should specify:

- what observation or argument would count;
- which premise it bears on;
- how much the Map’s credence would change;
- what rival would gain probability;
- whether the condition is in principle obtainable.

## 17. Tenet-protective bracketing and the review methodology

The Map is admirably explicit that its five tenets are chosen starting points that constrain the corpus. Framework-internal exploration is legitimate, and an article need not pretend to be neutral when its purpose is conditional analysis. [unfinishablemap.org](https://unfinishablemap.org/)

The difficulty arises when the article moves from:

> Here is what follows, or may fit together, if the Map’s premises and several additional ethical doctrines are accepted

to:

> Dualism resolves what materialism cannot.

The second is a cross-framework comparative claim. It cannot be protected by declaring the strongest objections “framework-boundary” questions. Those are precisely the objections that determine whether the claimed comparative advantage exists.

The October review log illustrates the process risk. It records useful corrections but says that no enhancements were made because the prose had crossed the topic-length soft threshold, while the Street gap remained human-deferred. [changelog](/workflow/changelog/)

A word-count threshold is a sensible editorial pressure. It should not prevent insertion of a central objection while leaving broad claims of resolution and superiority intact. The proper response would be to cut repetition, split the article, or replace weak historical survey material—not to defer a counterargument that directly targets moral realism and truth-tracking.

More generally, a corpus seeded by fixed tenets will naturally produce internal coherence. The Map already recognises that risk in the apex article. To treat coherence as evidence, it needs a review process capable of:

- distinguishing consequences of the tenets from independent support for them;
- comparing complete rival packages symmetrically;
- allowing external objections to reduce confidence rather than merely classifying them as out-of-framework;
- propagating concessions from specialist pages back into synthesis pages;
- and recording unresolved bridge principles rather than filling them with the word “consciousness”.

1. **Rewrite the opening of “Ethics Under Dualism”.** Replace the claim that irreducible, efficacious consciousness immediately yields patienthood and four-domain unity with an explicit list of the additional premises: phenomenal value realism, affective sentientism, agent causation, phenomenal normativity and realist moral perception.

6. **Remove or defend the claim that dualist ethics is “more demanding”.** Distinguish metaphysical robustness from substantive demandingness and compare actual obligations generated by rival theories.
7. **Correct the unity argument.** State that the four pillars share a subject and perhaps a metaphysical setting, but do not derive from one another. Explicitly acknowledge that moral patients need not be agents and that causal efficacy is unnecessary for intrinsic badness.

16. **Update the AI section from the 10 October AI-ethics article.** Incorporate the distinction between Map-internal priors and the broader indicator-based literature, including Butlin and colleagues and recent AI-welfare preparedness work.

18. **Create an explicit burden-of-proof rule for AI consciousness.** The Map should update not only after proof that biology requires nothing non-physical, but also after repeated failures of the proposed interface to generate distinctive evidence.
19. **Reconcile the AI-agency articles.** Reserve “moral agent” for responsibility-bearing agency, and distinguish it from functional agency, autonomous causal operation and legal responsibility assignment.
20. **Remove the inference from non-consciousness to lack of “inside understanding”.** Treat phenomenality, semantic competence, world modelling, proxy alignment and oversight as separate claims requiring separate evidence.
21. **Turn the copy/upload section into a conditional decision tree.** Distinguish non-conscious simulation, conscious but affectless simulation, conscious and valenced simulation, destructive copying, non-destructive copying and gradual replacement.
22. **Engage Parfit’s what-matters thesis rather than merely denying identity.** Explain why psychological continuity would lack survival-relevant value even if it fails to preserve numerical identity.
23. **Add practical copy ethics.** Cover consent, deletion, ownership, copied obligations, labour exploitation, branching, resource claims, inherited relationships and the aggregation of welfare across multiple copies.
24. **Standardise the suffering taxonomy across the focal, pain and apex articles.** Define nociception, pain, unpleasantness, negative valence and suffering separately.
25. **Revise “Pain, Consciousness and Causal Power”.** Remove any suggestion that asymbolia is decisive evidence; incorporate Griffith and Kind, Duval and Klein, Gerrans, and the apex article’s own conclusion that the case is presently non-discriminating.
26. **Rewrite the illusionism section around Frankish 2024.** Delete the claim that illusions must themselves be phenomenal experiences, distinguish practical from intrinsic mattering, and answer the strongest functional account rather than the 2016 programme statement alone.
27. **Rename “What Would Challenge This View”.** Use “Defeaters, discriminating evidence and confidence-update conditions”, and repair the logically unstable identical-functional-state example.
28. **Synchronise the focal article with the apex concessions.** Import the statements that unity is partly produced by corpus construction, is not independent evidence, does not establish rights or aggregation, and may add little beyond compatibilism.
29. **Surface the position calibration near the thesis.** Readers should see at the outset that [P-MS1](/positions/moral-status/#p-ms1) has moderate confidence and no independent evidential support, rather than finding that qualification after a categorical conclusion.
30. **Introduce claim-type labels throughout the Map.** Mark important propositions as `entailed by tenets`, `compatible with tenets`, `framework-internal consequence`, `independent evidence`, `abductive advantage`, or `cross-framework comparative claim`.
31. **Require a tenet-neutral review for comparative superiority claims.** Articles may be openly partisan internally, but claims that materialism cannot explain something should receive a separate review that steelmans one complete rival package.
32. **Add a bridge-principle ledger.** Record unresolved moves such as phenomenal badness → interpersonal reason, causal influence → responsibility, moral seeming → truth-tracking, and psychological discontinuity → ethically significant death.
33. **Create automatic contradiction propagation.** Changes to AI moral agency, pain asymbolia, phenomenal sentientism, illusionism or personal identity should trigger review of every synthesis page that depends on those claims.
34. **Do not allow word-count thresholds to suppress major objections.** When a central challenge such as Street exceeds the article’s budget, cut repetition or create a linked companion section instead of preserving the strong claim and deferring its principal defeater.
35. **Maintain a quotation and locator ledger.** Phrase-level quotations and distinctive formulations from Chalmers, Murdoch, Weil, Regan and others should carry page or section locators and a record of whether the full source or only an abstract was checked.
36. **Require explicit confidence updates at framework boundaries.** An objection should not become epistemically inert merely because accepting it would reject a tenet; the register should state how much the objection lowers confidence in that tenet and what evidence would change the assessment.
