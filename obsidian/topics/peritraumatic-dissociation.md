Peritraumatic dissociation is depersonalisation, derealisation, altered time sense, numbing or amnesia during or just after a traumatic event. It is the best-studied test of a datum two of the Unfinishable Map's pages treat as given: that trauma-linked dissociation looks selective and protective. The test shows that "protective" needs a timescale. Over the long run, dissociation at the time of trauma marks a worse course. Pooled correlations with later post-traumatic stress symptoms are r = .35 (Ozer et al. 2003), .36 across 35 studies (Breh & Seidler 2007) and .401 across 59 studies and 16,547 participants (Lensvelt-Mulders et al. 2008). Much of that predictive power goes once persistent dissociation and early symptoms are controlled for, though one cohort kept a unique 4% (Werner & Griffin 2012). At the moment of trauma, the one direct test of whether dissociation relieves felt distress (Fikretoglu et al. 2006, N = 1,026) found no relief, and panic explains 47% of the variance in dissociation (Bryant & Panasetis 2005). Nearly all of this rests on retrospective self-report, which drifts with current symptoms (Thompson-Hollands et al. 2021).

The failure lands equally on both readings the Map weighs. The interface reading in [[clinical-dissociation-as-systematic-evidence]] calls trauma-linked dissociation "the mind protecting itself"; the rival defence-cascade account proposes that opioid analgesia mitigates "the intensity of subjective fear" (Kozlowska et al. 2015). Both are claims about the moment, and the evidence supports neither. The comparative verdict in [[dissociative-subtype-of-ptsd]], that the trauma-selectivity point belongs to the rival, stands; the interface reading regains nothing; and Dualism remains compatible with these data without being evidenced by them. What changes is the datum both readings were explaining: "protective" shrinks to an untested claim about the moment, attached to a state the person can report only [[#after-the-fact|after the fact]] (explained below).

## The Construct and Its Instrument {#construct}

The field's main instrument is the Peritraumatic Dissociative Experiences Questionnaire (PDEQ). Marmar et al. (1994) operationalised the construct with its rater version in 251 male Vietnam theatre veterans, using "retrospective reports of dissociation during the most disturbing combat trauma events", and found that "the greater the dissociation during traumatic stress exposure, the greater the likelihood of meeting criteria for current PTSD".

The construct is broad, and instruments disagree on whether amnesia, numbing and reduced awareness belong to it (van der Hart et al. 2008). It covers more than the depersonalisation and derealisation that [[dissociative-subtype-of-ptsd]] concerns, so the two articles treat a broad state and a narrower one. Tonic immobility, the defence cascade's "strategy of last resort", is a motor state that often includes dissociative experience, not a synonym for it.

## The Predictive Record {#predictive-record}

The association points the wrong way for any long-run protective reading. In Ozer et al.'s (2003) meta-analysis of 68 studies and seven predictors of PTSD, pre-trauma factors were smallest and "peritraumatic dissociation the largest (weighted r = .35)". Breh and Seidler (2007), pooling 35 studies, found a mean r of 0.36 and called peritraumatic dissociation "a moderate risk factor".

The largest synthesis, a multilevel meta-regression of 59 studies, reports that "The standardized correlation between peritraumatic dissociation and PTS across studies was .401" (95% CI .364–.438) (Lensvelt-Mulders et al. 2008). The same paper calls its studies "too heterogeneous in their outcomes to compute an overall effect size or grand mean", so .401 is no settled grand mean; the heterogeneity tracked method more than theory. The authors defend the association against the charge of artefact but state that "no conclusions could be drawn as to causal relations between peritraumatic dissociation and PTS".

In youth the link is weaker: r = 0.17 (k = 5; 95% CI 0.03–0.29), against r = 0.37 for subjective threat (Memarzia et al. 2021). The passive end of the defence cascade runs the same way. Of 298 women seen within a month of sexual assault, "70% reported significant tonic immobility and 48% reported extreme tonic immobility", and tonic immobility predicted PTSD at six months (OR 2.75; 95% CI 1.50–5.03) in the 189 followed up (Möller et al. 2017). In the long-run sense, protection fails, and nobody in this literature defends it.

## What Survives Control {#control}

The bivariate association is robust; its independence is not. Briere, Scott and Weathers (2005), with samples of 52 and 386, found that "PTSD status was no longer related to peritraumatic dissociation once other variables (especially persistent and generalized dissociation) were taken into account", while "persistent dissociation was a strong predictor at univariate and multivariate levels". Their participants were assessed once, so the peritraumatic measure was itself recalled. A systematic review of 17 prospective studies of single-incident trauma found in most of them "no or only weak indications of an independent predictive value" (van der Velden & Wittmann 2008). In a cross-lagged panel study, baseline recollections of dissociation did not predict later PTSD severity once baseline symptoms were controlled (Marshall & Schell 2002).

The null is not universal. Werner and Griffin (2012), following 92 female assault survivors with initial symptoms in the model, found peritraumatic and later persistent dissociation each "contributing 4% (p = .05) and 8% (p = .008) of the variance, respectively". In the AURORA cohort, persistent derealisation two weeks after trauma predicted worse three-month outcomes beyond childhood maltreatment and current symptoms: "both an early psychological and biological marker of worse later psychiatric outcomes" (Lebois et al. 2022). And when 200 Israeli civilians completed the PDEQ during the May 2021 rocket campaign, "detriments of PD are evident when the trauma is appraised as being highly threatening" (Dokkedahl & Lahav 2024).

Dissociation that persists predicts better than dissociation at the time. The DSM-5 subtype concerns *persistent or recurrent* depersonalisation or derealisation, the timescale at which dissociation is most clearly a risk marker.

## Four Senses of "Protective" {#four-senses}

A common view, in Lensvelt-Mulders et al.'s summary, holds that "the immediate effects of peritraumatic dissociation are adaptive" while the long-run effects raise risk. "Protective" can mean four things, and the evidence differs for each.

1. **Against later disorder.** This fails (see the [[#predictive-record|predictive record]]), and neither reading needs it.
2. **Relief of felt distress in the moment.** This is the sense of the flagship's "unbearable" and "threatening experience", and of Kozlowska et al.'s "subjective fear". Fikretoglu et al. (2006), whose authors include the PDEQ's developers, studied 709 police officers and 317 civilians: "Among those with high levels of dissociation, very few reported low levels of distress." Their results "do not provide support for the idea that dissociation protects individuals from experiencing high levels of distress at the time of the trauma". They call dissociation an "epiphenomenon" of high distress, meaning a by-product of distress; the word makes no claim about whether conscious states are causally idle. Among 51 civilian survivors, "panic reactions during the trauma accounted for nearly half of the variance (47%) of peritraumatic dissociation" (Bryant & Panasetis 2005). Fikretoglu's test is cross-sectional and retrospective, with distress and dissociation sharing a method, but it is the only one, and it is null.
3. **Autonomic dampening.** Griffin, Resick and Mechanic (1997) recorded 85 rape survivors recounting the assault within two weeks. Comparing 16 high with 31 low dissociators, they found "a suppression of autonomic physiological responses in the high dissociation group", while "these subjects' self-report ratings of distress were significantly higher than their physiological measures of arousal". The authors suggested dissociation may be a risk factor "rather than an adaptive coping mechanism as previously suggested", while allowing that it may be a way of coping whose costs come later. In a trauma-film analogue, "Increases in dissociation and decreases in heart rate during the film were also associated with later intrusions" (Holmes et al. 2004), and a systematic review found no clear autonomic trend across studies (Beutler et al. 2022). Where dampening appears it is physiological rather than felt, and it predicts more intrusions.
4. **Survival.** A defence that improves physical outcomes under inescapable threat belongs to the rival's evolutionary account alone, and these designs cannot test it.

## Measuring a State After the Fact {#after-the-fact}

Every sense of momentary protection needs a fact about what the trauma felt like while it happened, the hardest fact in this literature to obtain. As Candel and Merckelbach (2004) observe, "studies in this area heavily rely on retrospective reports of dissociative reactions during the trauma". Recollections taken within days of exposure differed from those taken at three and twelve months (Marshall & Schell 2002). When 126 treatment-seeking adults rated the PDEQ for the same index event six times over 60 weeks, there was "> 40% of participants experiencing reliable change from baseline at least once during the study", with scores tracking current symptoms (Thompson-Hollands et al. 2021). As the disorder eases, people remember having dissociated less. Lensvelt-Mulders et al. found the correlation rising with the time between event and first measurement, and suggested that in clinical samples "the past is defined in the light of the present situation".

Yet the same meta-analysis found that "longitudinal studies reported a stronger positive relation between peritraumatic dissociation and PTS than the retrospective studies", which tells against a pure recall artefact, although its longitudinal category includes designs whose first measurement was itself retrospective. Measurement during an ongoing threat is possible, as the rocket-campaign study shows. Part of the difficulty is principled: "The subjective experience of PD may not be accessible in the moment and may only be understood or recognized after the immediate danger has passed" (van der Hart et al. 2008, crediting the point to Ozer et al.).

This is a clinical instance of the [[inaccessible-past]], where reconstruction lets present concerns overwrite what was stored, and of the [[confabulation-void]], where error is detected, if at all, only retrospectively.

## What This Does to the Two Readings {#two-readings}

Both readings frame protection as momentary, so both are hit. The interface reading has "the mind protecting itself by severing access to unbearable memories, withdrawing felt ownership of threatening experience". The subtype article gives the rival's version: "The purposiveness is inherited from evolution rather than chosen by a withdrawing subject". The rival's momentary claim, opioid relief of fear, is one Kozlowska et al. offer as probable rather than found. The data show neither a subject relieving its distress nor a cascade doing so. Lebois et al. (2022) complicate the rival further: the vmPFC activation the subtype article cites for overmodulation comes from the cohort in which persistent derealisation at two weeks marked a worse outcome.

The comparative verdict does not move. The rival explains why dissociation arises under inescapable threat and through which circuitry, and dissociation that tracks panic and extreme distress fits a triggered mechanism on either reading. Weakening the protective half of the datum returns no point to the interface reading. The selective half is untouched, because this literature does not measure whether dissociation spares the channels needed for ongoing function.

## Relation to Site Perspective

**[[tenets#^dualism|Dualism]]**: compatible with, not evidence for. The literature constrains a datum both readings explain and decides nothing about whether consciousness is reducible to physical processes.

**[[tenets#^bidirectional-interaction|Bidirectional Interaction]]**: the data fit the tenet without evidencing it. "The mind protecting itself" is an agentive gloss, and the literature finds no sign of purposive control in the moment. That counts against the purposive-withdrawal gloss, not against mental causation in general, and the rival's inherited purposiveness equally lacks momentary evidence of relief. Fikretoglu et al.'s "epiphenomenon" is a psychometric claim about a by-product of distress and concedes nothing to epiphenomenalism, which the Map rejects. The strongest correlate in these data is felt distress itself, a fact silent on mental causation in either direction.

**[[tenets#^minimal-quantum-interaction|Minimal Quantum Interaction]]** and **[[tenets#^no-many-worlds|No Many Worlds]]**: not engaged.

**[[tenets#^occams-limits|Occam's Razor Has Limits]]**: *adaptive now, costly later* is a tidy story that both readings borrowed. The predictive power is not clearly independent, recall is unstable, and the one momentary test is null. The complication favours neither reading.

The measurement point cuts against the Map as much as for it. First-person evidence is weakest exactly where the interface gloss placed a phenomenal claim, about how a trauma felt while it was happening. The Map records this as a limit on its own evidence, in line with [[evidential-status-discipline]].
