## Summary

The three centuries after the Fourth Meditation rehearse every position the modern debate holds, under psychologies as different from each other as from Descartes's. **Hobbes** (1641) answered Descartes at once with the modern psychological point: "Whatever is proved by strong Arguments, or Credibly told, we Believe whether we will or no" [raw, Molyneux 1680], and drew the distinction the whole later debate relies on, between affirming and denying, which are "Acts of the Will", and "Internal Assent", which is not. Descartes's **reply** gives away less than it seems: to say we assent to the clearly perceived "whether we will or no" is "the same, as if it were said, that willing or nilling, we desire Good clearly known" [raw], the compatibilist freedom-as-inclination Boespflug (2023) later used to take Descartes at his word; and the *Principles* (1644) restate that "we possess a free will, capable of giving or withholding its assent" as one of "the first and most common notions which are born with us" [raw, Veitch]. **Pascal** (1670) supplied the indirect route everyone since has conceded: to the man who says "I am so made that I cannot believe", the answer is to act as the believers do, "taking the holy water, having masses said, etc. Even this will naturally make you believe, and deaden your acuteness" [raw, §233]. **Spinoza** (1677) gave the conceptual denial, "Will and understanding are one and the same", and made suspension of judgment "strictly speaking, a perception, and not free will" [carried, Elwes]; Gilbert's own 1990 paper cites Shirley's *Ethics* at pp. 96–101 (the IIP48–49 region) for the claim that "all ideas are accepted (i.e., represented in the mind as true) prior to a rational analysis of their veracity" [raw], so the "Spinozan" label rests on the scholium's psychology, as the 10-07 note guessed, and stylises it into a sequence Spinoza does not state. **Locke** (1689) stated the involuntarist thesis in its modern form, "assent is no more in our power than knowledge" [raw, IV.xx.16], while granting control over *inquiry* ("we can hinder both KNOWLEDGE and ASSENT, BY STOPPING OUR INQUIRY"), a "power to suspend" in "less clear cases" [raw, IV.xx.15], and a conceptual-sounding impossibility of assenting against the apparent probability, "as impossible as it is to believe the same thing probable and improbable at the same time" [raw]; the *Letter Concerning Toleration* drew the political consequence that the understanding "cannot be compelled to the belief of anything by outward force" [fetch-summarised]. **Hume** (1739; 1748) moved the limit from the will to the nature of belief as a feeling: belief "depends not on the will, but must arise from certain determinate causes and principles, of which we are not masters" [raw, Appendix], "it is not in our power to believe that such an animal has ever really existed" [raw, *Enquiry* V.ii], and in a footnote he denied that judgment is a distinct act of the understanding at all [raw, I.iii.7 n.]. Reid and Kant [SEP-mediated] stand on the same side. **Newman** (1870) reopened the question from a third direction: certitude "is not a passive impression made upon the mind from without, by argumentative compulsion, but … an active recognition of propositions as true, such as it is the duty of each individual himself to exercise at the bidding of reason, and, when reason forbids, to withhold" [raw, ch. IX], governed by an "Illative Sense" that is "the mind that reasons, and that controls its own reasonings, not any technical apparatus" [raw]. **James** (1896) conceded Hume's point in full, "we are absolutely impotent to believe" what the intellect has settled [raw], criticised Pascal's wager because a faith so adopted "would lack the inner soul of faith's reality" [raw], and confined the will's authority to genuine options the intellect cannot decide, adding the self-verifying case in which "faith in a fact can help create the fact" [raw]. **Williams** (1973) is the point at which the conceptual argument takes its standard modern form [SEP-mediated], with the fourteenth-century credit already installed on [[assent-before-descartes]].

For the Map the yield is the one the first history gave, now tested under a second set of psychologies: a Hobbesian materialist, a Spinozist monist, a Humean associationist, a Reidian realist, a Kantian, a Catholic apologist and a pragmatist all recognised the same limit on believing at will, and the one who contested it, James, contested it only where the intellect has nothing to say. None of this is evidence for the tenets. The specific things that move for the two live pages, the Gilbert verdict and the Ginet discrepancy, are in the Implications section.

## The Stations

Ordered by date. Each entry says what the station adds to the voluntarism question, with the question's shape at that point; the two sentences of "Map use" are for the article writer.

### 1. Hobbes, Third Objections (1641), and Descartes's reply

Hobbes's thirteenth objection makes two moves. The first is the appeal to experience that Alston (1988) would later formalise: proved or credibly reported things "we Believe whether we will or no". The second is a distinction: to "Affirm or Deny Propositions, to Defend or Oppose" them are acts of will, but "Internal Assent" does not follow them. This is the earliest modern separation of the *speech act* of assertion from the *state* of belief, the separation that lets later writers grant Descartes everything he says about judging as a performance while denying that believing is one. Hobbes also attacks the "Great Light in the Understanding" as a metaphor that the obstinate can claim as readily as the knower, which is an early statement of the point the Map's [[noetic-feelings-void]] makes about the feeling of certainty: it does not discriminate.

Descartes's reply is short and does not deny the experience. He says "whether we will or no" is the wrong qualification for clear perceptions, "the same, as if it were said, that willing or nilling, we desire Good clearly known": to be inclined by clear perception just *is* to will, so the "nilling" has no application. This is the compatibilist freedom-as-inclination of the Fourth Meditation (§8: the more inclined, "the more freely do I choose"), and it is exactly the reading Boespflug (2023) uses to take Descartes at his word; the SEP's list of "reluctant" scholars (Cottingham 2008; L. Newman 2015; Vitz 2010; Schüssler 2013; Davies 2001) is the other side. Three years later the *Principles* (I.34, I.39) repeat that the will "is required in order to our assenting" and that a will "capable of giving or withholding its assent" is among the notions "born with us", so the exchange moved Descartes not at all.

**What it adds**: the question splits here into (a) whether we can believe against or beyond evidence (Hobbes: no) and (b) whether assent to the evident is nonetheless free (Descartes: yes, because freedom is inclination). The two questions are not in conflict, which is why the SEP can file Descartes as a voluntarist and a compatibilist at once. **Map use**: the [[assent-void]] L97 sentence that Descartes's picture "has no special claim on introspection" has its first witness in Hobbes; and Descartes's reply shows that the "freedom" he claims for assent is the Stoic sage's freedom (cf. [[assent-before-descartes]] §Stoic Double Claim), which is to say Hieronymi's evaluative control, not a handle. The 1680 Molyneux wording is public domain and quotable; the CSM wording ("whether we want to or not") should be attributed to the SEP.

### 2. Pascal, *Pensées* §233 (1670)

The wager fragment ends with the exchange that matters here. The interlocutor grants the arithmetic and says "I am so made that I cannot believe." Pascal's reply concedes the point ("learn your inability to believe") and prescribes the remedy that bypasses it: not more proofs but "the abatement of your passions", achieved by "acting as if they believed, taking the holy water, having masses said, etc. Even this will naturally make you believe, and deaden your acuteness." Pascal thereby states indirect control in its purest form: an action the will can perform, whose foreseen effect is a belief the will cannot produce. Alston's "indirect influence" and Hieronymi's "managerial control" both cite this passage (the [[doxastic-voluntarism]] page already lists "Pascal's wagerer taking holy water" under Alston's fourth kind); the [[assent-void]] already reads it as probing the void "by working around it". What this run adds is James's 1896 verdict on it (station 9): a faith "adopted wilfully after such a mechanical calculation would lack the inner soul of faith's reality", and the option "is not a living option" without a pre-existing tendency. James's point is Williams's opacity condition seventy-seven years early: a belief known to have been installed for a reward is not experienced as a belief. **Map use**: one paragraph pairing Pascal's remedy with James's objection to it gives the article its indirect-control station without repeating the void page.

### 3. Spinoza, *Ethics* IIP48–49 (1677), and Gilbert's label

The 10-07 note recorded the texts; what is new here is the check on Gilbert. Spinoza's IIP49 and its corollary ("Will and understanding are one and the same") are the conceptual denial: there is no faculty of assent distinct from having the idea, so the question "can the will add assent?" is ill-formed. The scholium's boy and horse, "he will necessarily regard the horse as present: he will not be able to doubt of its existence, although he be not certain thereof", is the psychological claim, and the dream case follows. Gilbert's label can now be adjudicated against Gilbert's own citation. Gilbert, Krull & Malone (1990) cite "Spinoza (1677/1982, pp. 96—101)", Shirley's Hackett translation, for the claim that "all ideas are accepted (i.e., represented in the mind as true) prior to a rational analysis of their veracity, and that some ideas are subsequently unaccepted". Pages 96–101 of the Shirley *Ethics* fall in Part II's closing propositions, which is where IIP48–49 sit, so the citation points at the right passage (the page mapping is inferred from the edition's layout, not checked against a copy). Verdict: the label is **accurate to the scholium's psychology**, an idea is affirmed unless another idea excludes its object, and **a stylisation in two respects**. First, Spinoza does not describe a two-step sequence (accept, then "unaccept"); on his account the exclusion is itself another idea, not an operation on a stored tag. Second, the "Spinozan model" owes nothing to the corollary's identity thesis, which is a monist premise about will and intellect the empirical model does not need; a Cartesian dualist could hold the scholium's psychology. Gilbert 1990 also concedes what the [[assent-void]] L97 says: "In the case of abstract beliefs, the Cartesian procedure seems to describe the phenomenology of believing much better than does the Spinozan." Della Rocca (2003) is the standard philosophical treatment of IIP49's "critique of pure will" [metadata only]. **Map use**: the void's L69 can keep Gilbert's labels and should cite IIP49 *scholium*; the void's quoted Gilbert 1991 phrase still needs a raw check (Gaps).

### 4. Calvin and the Reformation (1559), a prelude the first article skipped

The 10-07 note listed "the Reformation debate on faith and will (Luther, Calvin)" as not searched. Calvin's *Institutes* III.ii is now read at raw. He defines faith as a knowledge "revealed to our minds, and sealed on our hearts, by the Holy Spirit" (III.ii.7, [fetch-summarised]), says that "assent itself … is more a matter of the heart than the head, of the affection than the intellect" (III.ii.8, [raw]), and that "The word is not received in faith when it merely flutters in the brain, but when it has taken deep root in the heart" (III.ii.36, [raw]). This is a third model, neither Augustine's voluntary consent nor Descartes's judgment: assent as something *given* to the believer and lodged in affection, so that the will's part is receptive. Luther was not reached (Gaps). **Does it earn a station?** Not as a station in an article slugged "after Descartes". It earns one sentence in the opening, naming it as the model the Cartesian exchange ignored, and a Gaps-honest note that the Reformation debate still lacks a Map treatment. The writer may omit it without loss to the voluntarism thread.

### 5. Locke, *Essay* IV (1689) and the *Letter Concerning Toleration*

Locke is the station the modern taxonomy descends from, and neither live page mentions him. Four things are in the text. (i) The thesis, in IV.xx.16: "assent is no more in our power than knowledge", with the perceptual analogy that runs through the chapter (IV.xiii.2: "It depends not on his will to see that black which appears yellow"). (ii) Control over *inquiry*, not assent: a man "may choose whether he will curiously survey" an object (IV.xiii.2); "we can hinder both KNOWLEDGE and ASSENT, BY STOPPING OUR INQUIRY" (IV.xx.16); the wishful believer "hath a power to suspend and restrain its inquiries" (IV.xx.12). This is Alston's long-range control and Hieronymi's managerial control, stated as the *only* locus of doxastic responsibility: "If it were not so, ignorance, error, or infidelity, could not in any case be a fault." (iii) The narrow permissive space the SEP flags: "In other less clear cases, I think it is in man's power to suspend his assent" (IV.xx.15), which is the territory Aquinas reserved for the will and the modern permissivists (Ginet; Crowe) claim. Note that Locke's power here is to *suspend* and "stop from further search", not to assent to either side at pleasure. (iv) A conceptual-sounding impossibility: assenting to "that side on which the less probability appears" is "as impossible as it is to believe the same thing probable and improbable at the same time". That is an impossibility of content, not of capacity, and it stands between Holcot's contradiction argument and Williams's. The *Letter* draws the political consequence: the understanding "cannot be compelled to the belief of anything by outward force", and "only light and evidence" change opinion; the *First Tract* (1660) already had assent as what God "hath reserved the disposure to himself" [SEP-mediated]. Newman's ch. VI attack on Locke's "degrees of assent" [carried] is the nineteenth-century reply. Wolterstorff (1996) is the standard study of Locke's ethics of belief [metadata only]. **Map use**: Locke supplies the doxastic-voluntarism page's missing early-modern anchor for "indirect influence is common ground", and his (iv) extends the Holcot-before-Williams credit line by one more pre-modern statement of the conceptual route.

### 6. Hume, *Treatise* I.iii.7 and Appendix (1739–40); *Enquiry* V.ii (1748)

Hume relocates the limit. For Hobbes and Locke the will cannot command assent; for Hume there is no act of assent to command. Belief is "a lively idea related to or associated with a present impression", differing from fiction only "in the manner of its being conceived"; "An idea assented to FEELS different from a fictitious idea". The Appendix makes the involuntarism explicit: belief "depends not on the will, but must arise from certain determinate causes and principles, of which we are not masters." The *Enquiry* gives the test case the SEP quotes, the man-headed horse one can conceive but not believe to have "really existed", and the reason: if the mind could "voluntarily annex this particular idea to any fiction", it would "be able to believe whatever it pleases; contrary to what we find by daily experience." The feeling "must be excited by nature, like all other sentiments". The footnote to I.iii.7 goes further than any later involuntarist: the schools' "three acts of the understanding", conception, judgment and reasoning, "all resolve themselves into the first", so judgment is not a distinct act at all; the one difference is "when we join belief to the conception", and "This act of the mind has never yet been explain'd by any philosopher". Reid (1764) reaches the same involuntarism from the opposite theory of perception: "My belief is carried along by perception, as irresistibly as my body by the earth" [SEP-mediated], and Kant's *Logic* says "The will does not have any influence immediately on holding-to-be-true; this would be quite absurd" [SEP-mediated], while, as the [[assent-void]] L65 already notes, allowing choice to direct attention to the evidence. **What it adds**: under associationism the limit survives and sharpens, because the thing one would command is a feeling. **Map use**: Hume is the named dissenter the void's Seam needs. The Seam adopts Shah & Velleman's "judgment … is an act"; Hume denies there is such an act, and his footnote is the cleanest statement of the "no act" position the void's control face flirts with. The article should say the Map sides with Shah & Velleman and Newman against Hume on whether judging is an act, and that the void's three faces do not depend on who is right.

### 7. Newman, *Grammar of Assent* (1870)

Newman answers Locke and Hume together. Against Locke, assent is unconditional and "distinct" from inference, "substantive" enough to be "withheld" or "withdrawn" with "the reasons remaining" [carried, ch. VI]. Against Hume, certitude "is not a passive impression made upon the mind from without, by argumentative compulsion, but … an active recognition of propositions as true" (ch. IX). Yet the activity is bound to reason: it is a recognition "such as it is the duty of each individual himself to exercise at the bidding of reason, and, when reason forbids, to withhold", and "reason never bids us be certain except on an absolute proof". The faculty that passes from converging probabilities to unconditional assent in concrete matters is the Illative Sense, "the mind that reasons, and that controls its own reasonings, not any technical apparatus of words and propositions", modelled on Aristotle's *phronesis*. Newman declines to explain the mechanism: "for me it is sufficient that certitude is felt." Ferreira (1980) is the monograph on the will in Newman's thought [metadata only]. **What it adds**: a position that is voluntarist about the *act* (assent is something the mind does, and can withhold while reasons stand) and involuntarist about the *grounds* (it may be given only "at the bidding of reason"). This is the ancestor of the act/state split the [[assent-void]] takes from Shah & Velleman, and of the all-or-nothing premise its timing face assumes. Newman's withholding-with-reasons-standing is also a seventeenth-century-style voluntarism about *suspension*, which the [[suspension-void]] should know about (its verification face says one cannot verify one has suspended; Newman says one can withhold at will, which is a stronger claim than the void allows). **Map use**: the article's hinge station; Newman is the only figure between Descartes and James who holds assent to be an act and still denies believing at will.

### 8. Clifford (1877) and James (1896)

Clifford's "It is wrong always, everywhere, and for any one, to believe anything upon insufficient evidence" (as James quotes it) presupposes that belief can be regulated, and James answers on the same presupposition. James's concessions are larger than his reputation: we "are absolutely impotent to believe" against the intellect; the "whole fabric of the truths that we do believe in" is of that kind, "matters of fact … as Hume said, and relations between ideas"; Pascal's remedy produces a faith that "would lack the inner soul of faith's reality". His thesis covers only options that are living, forced and momentous and "cannot by its nature be decided on intellectual grounds", and there "our passional nature … must, decide", because suspending is "itself a passional decision". The one place James goes beyond permissivism is the self-verifying case, "faith in a fact can help create the fact", where belief precedes and produces its own evidence; the Map's [[pragmatisms-path-to-dualism]] and [[william-james-consciousness]] pages carry "The Will to Believe" for other purposes and neither treats this clause. **What it adds**: the modern voluntarist high-water mark is a permissivism about underdetermined options, which is Aquinas's and Locke's territory, plus a class of cases in which the truth-aim and the practical reason coincide because the belief makes itself true. **Map use**: James's self-verifying cases are a gap on [[doxastic-voluntarism]]: Williams's argument needs believing for reasons "unconnected to truth", and in a self-fulfilling case the practical reason is a truth-related reason. The article should flag this as the one historical case that bears on the conceptual argument's premise rather than on the psychological datum.

### 9. Endpoint: Williams (1973)

"Deciding to Believe" gives the conceptual argument its standard modern form [SEP-mediated reconstruction above], and the [[doxastic-voluntarism]] page carries the Winters–Bennett–Hieronymi repair chain. The history above shows the two routes arriving separately: the psychological route from Hobbes through Locke, Hume, Reid and Kant to Alston; the conceptual route from Holcot (per Boespflug) through Spinoza's identity thesis and Locke's "probable and improbable at the same time" to Williams. The credit line already on [[assent-before-descartes]] §Who Stated the Arguments First stands; this history adds Locke's clause as a further pre-modern statement and does not change the originator.

## Implications for the Map

**Tenet-neutral framing (mandatory for the lede)**. The record carries no evidence for the tenets. Its one use is the test the first history ran, now under a second set of psychologies: Hobbes's materialism, Spinoza's monism, Hume's associationism, Reid's realism, Kant's critical philosophy, Newman's Catholic apologetics and James's pragmatism all recognised a limit on believing at will, and the one dissenter confined dissent to options "that cannot by its nature be decided on intellectual grounds". A limit that survives this many theories of mind is, on the Map's reading, a fact about belief's answerability to truth rather than about any metaphysics of mind, which is the reply the [[assent-void]] and [[doxastic-voluntarism]] already give to the objection from Bidirectional Interaction. This removes a defeater and supplies no evidence for interaction. Nothing here bears on Dualism, Minimal Quantum Interaction, No Many Worlds or Occam's Razor Has Limits.

**What moves for [[doxastic-voluntarism]] §The Map's Reading and §Rival Positions**:
1. *Introspection cuts both ways* gains its first witness: Hobbes's "we Believe whether we will or no" (1641) is the introspective appeal against Descartes, and Descartes's reply is that the appeal mis-describes what willing is. A one-clause addition.
2. *Indirect influence is common ground* has a date: Pascal 1670 and Locke 1689 ("we can hinder both KNOWLEDGE and ASSENT, BY STOPPING OUR INQUIRY"), and Locke already ties doxastic *fault* exclusively to inquiry, the move Alston makes against epistemic deontology three centuries later.
3. *Rival Positions* lacks the self-verifying case: James's "faith in a fact can help create the fact". It is the one historical case that touches the conceptual argument's premise (reasons "unconnected to truth"), since in a self-fulfilling case the practical reason is truth-connected. Suggested as a sentence under permissivist voluntarism with a pointer to the new article.
4. Steup's parity claim (belief and action are reasons-responsive in the same way) has a precedent in Descartes's reply to Hobbes: assent to the clearly perceived is "willing" in the same way desiring a clearly known good is. Optional.

## Tenet Assessment

- **Dualism**: no evidence either way. Hobbes's materialism and Spinoza's monism reach involuntarism; so do Descartes's dualism (in the compatibilist form of his reply) and Newman's theism. The Map does not borrow authority from any of them.
- **Minimal Quantum Interaction**: nothing bears.
- **Bidirectional Interaction**: the objection "if consciousness causes things, why can it not make itself believe?" is answered as before: the limit is belief's answerability to truth (Locke's "probable and improbable at the same time", Williams), recognised under every theory of mind in this history. Removes a defeater; adds no evidence.
- **No Many Worlds**: nothing bears.
- **Occam's Razor Has Limits**: Gilbert 1990's concession that the Cartesian procedure fits the phenomenology of abstract belief better than the Spinozan is the void's L111 point (the simpler picture may be the wrong one) in the psychologist's own words; a one-clause use at most.
