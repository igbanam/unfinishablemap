## Critiques by Philosopher

### The Eliminative Materialist
Churchland would say the "gestalt" of wrongness is a fast, learned pattern-completion in a network trained on social reward and punishment. Its seeming "a feature of the world" is just what well-trained prototype activation feels like from inside. The page anticipates this only at L73's parenthesis ("the phenomenological and neural descriptions may pick out the same process at different levels"). That concession is good, but L131 then says "no description of amygdala activation captures what it is like". This swaps a straw target (the amygdala alone) for the distributed account she would actually give. The page's dualism claim lives on the explanatory gap, which it states fairly; it gains nothing from the moral case specifically.

### The Hard-Nosed Physicalist
Dennett would accept the whole phenomenology as heterophenomenological data and deny that any of it is evidence about metaphysics. He would also press L111 hard. People do sacrifice their lives because of how situations seem to them, and the *seeming* is a functional state that does all the motivating. Calling that "miraculous" assumes the conclusion. The Map's own illusionism page grants the point for the parallel causal argument (Issue 2). He would add that L119 calls epiphenomenalist correlation "systematically coincidental". A Chalmers-style epiphenomenalist posits psychophysical *laws*, so the correlation is lawful, not coincidental. The hedge "A thoroughgoing epiphenomenalist can maintain this position" softens this but does not correct the description.

### The Quantum Skeptic
The page makes no quantum-mechanism claims. It has 0 hits for closure, Born, biasing, photosynthesis, Lowe and chance (positive control: "Weil", 2 hits in the same file). Tegmark's complaint is therefore indirect. L55 says moral phenomenology is "the felt character of a real causal process", where the process is consciousness selecting "among open possibilities". Moral deliberation runs over seconds to days, at timescales and spatial scales where decoherence leaves nothing quantum to select. The conditional "If this is correct" protects L55. L133 has no such protection.

### The Many-Worlds Defender
Deutsch's critique is the strongest one against this page (Issue 1). L135 says that under many-worlds "the question 'what should *I* do?' loses its grip." The Deutsch–Wallace decision theory exists precisely to show that the question keeps its grip. The rational branching agent still deliberates, still weighs, and still chooses a policy. L135 also says "every option is actualised". That overstates the physics. A deliberation that is quasi-classical yields the same choice across almost all of the branch measure, and only outcomes with non-negligible amplitude are realised at all. That phrasing is corpus-wide, so it is not on the Priority List (see Secondary Findings). The decisive point is internal. The Map's own `background-commitments` page concedes that inside a branch "deliberation is reasons-responsive, and exactly one history persists". It concedes that rejecting MWI on agency grounds rests on a global-exclusion posit "the framework asserts rather than argues for".

### The Empiricist
A Popperian would ask what observation could distinguish the page's reading of moral phenomenology from the identity theorist's. The page's honest answer, at L121, is "none from this case alone": "Moral deliberation sharpens the question but does not settle it alone." The Relation section then forgets that answer. L133 offers "felt effort … tracks real cognitive reallocation" as better explained by conscious participation, but identity theory predicts exactly that tracking. P-A4 in the agency register adds a further point: no agent can certify its own causal efficacy by introspection, so the felt-effort datum cannot carry the evidential weight L133 gives it.

### The Buddhist Philosopher
Nagarjuna would say that L125 borrows Buddhist practice and then inverts its lesson. *Mettā bhāvanā* is cultivation (*bhāvanā*, "bringing into being"). The classical Visuddhimagga sequence builds goodwill in graded stages, from self to dear person to neutral person to enemy, until the barriers between them break down. Calling it "uncovering something already present" fits some later Buddha-nature and modern framings, not "Buddhist *mettā* practice" as such. And where the page says sustained attention "reveals more moral structure", the Madhyamaka contemplative would say it reveals the emptiness of the self–other distinction that the page's "single locus" (L135) and "felt ownership" (L103) presuppose. The parenthetical at L125 does acknowledge the divergence of frameworks, which is to its credit.

## Counterarguments to Address

### "Moral phenomenology is evidence for interactionist dualism"
- **Current content says**: The arc from strike to ownership is "the felt character of a real causal process" (L55, conditional), and felt effort is "better explained by genuine conscious participation" (L133).
- **A critic would argue**: Every datum on the page is predicted by identity theory and is securable on a compatibilist scaffold (P-A5). The page's own L121 says so.
- **Suggested response**: Let the page be what its best paragraph says it is. The phenomenology sharpens the hard problem and burdens bare-correlation epiphenomenalism. Its fit with interaction is coherence, not evidence. Issue 3's fix does this.

### "Moral remainder shows moral experience registering limits that reasoning cannot reach"
- **Current content says** (L89): "the guilt that persists after making the right choice in a tragic dilemma", followed by "even where reasoning reaches its limits".
- **A critic would argue**: The linked `normative-void` (L68–L74) defines tragic dilemmas as "situations where no choice is right" ("It's the *absence* of a right answer"). The page's triage example has a right choice, saving five, so applying that definition makes it ordinary moral remainder after a resolvable conflict, in the sense of Williams's "Ethical Consistency" (1965) and Marcus (1980). In that example, reasoning did *not* reach its limits.
- **Suggested response**: Say "after making the right choice in a moral conflict" and keep the normative-void sentence for genuinely tragic cases. Low priority; not on the list.

### "Obligation as perceived, not inferred, departs from Kant"
- **Current content says** (L85): Kant locates obligation in reason; "The Map suggests a different reading: the felt quality of obligation is irreducible phenomenal character, not a rational inference."
- **A critic would argue**: Kant does not treat obligation as felt *as an inference*. His account includes a distinctive feeling of respect (*Achtung*) produced by the moral law (Groundwork 4:401n). The real difference is about what grounds the feeling, not whether there is one.
- **Suggested response**: "Kant grounds the felt demand, respect for the law, in reason; the Map reads the felt quality as itself irreducible phenomenal character." Low priority.
