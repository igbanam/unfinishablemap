# Referee Report: "Neurological Dissociations as Interface Architecture"

## Introduction and General Appraisal

The manuscript "Neurological Dissociations as Interface Architecture" presents an ambitious, sweeping, but ultimately fundamentally flawed attempt to reconceptualize a suite of classical neurological anomalies—specifically split-brain syndrome, blindsight, anosognosia, and functional neurological disorder (FND)—as empirical evidence for a non-reductive, interactionist dualist "interface" model of consciousness. The author's central thesis asserts that phenomenal consciousness interacts with the physical world through the brain functioning as a transmitting and receiving interface. Consequently, the manuscript argues that localized neurological damage disrupts this interface rather than destroying the localized neurobiological seat of consciousness itself. By treating clinical dissociations as strictly defined "fractures" in a mind-brain channel, the manuscript attempts to insulate phenomenal experience from the prevailing winds of structural reductionism and computational functionalism.

However, a rigorous audit of the manuscript’s empirical claims, logical inferences, and engagement with contemporary cognitive neuroscience reveals a text that is entirely unmoored from the current scientific consensus. While the author displays a superficial familiarity with recent neuroscientific vocabulary, the manuscript systematically begs the question in favor of its a priori dualist metaphysics by framing routine scientific underdetermination as definitive evidence for non-physicality. More critically, the text relies on a heavily curated, often outdated, and explicitly gerrymandered selection of empirical literature. It routinely elevates minority interpretations of specific phenomena to the status of settled fact, while utterly ignoring devastating, peer-reviewed rebuttals from the 2021–2025 literature.

Furthermore, the manuscript misinterprets recent structural neuroimaging data—most notably the 2025 findings by Santander et al. regarding the connectivity of the corpus callosum—using raw functional connectivity matrices as a stand-in for phenomenal unity, without addressing the rigorous counterarguments regarding the unity of agency versus the unity of experience. The manuscript also fails to logically assimilate the dominant computational frameworks of the 2020s, specifically Active Inference and Predictive Processing, which elegantly and exhaustively explain the very dissociations (like FND and anosognosia) that the author claims require a dualist interface. Finally, the manuscript’s invocation of Integrated Information Theory (IIT) to support its "binding" arguments completely ignores the recent, paradigm-shifting adversarial collaborations published in 2025, rendering its theoretical landscape hopelessly obsolete.

This report will systematically deconstruct the manuscript’s handling of these clinical phenomena. It will demonstrate how its empirical inaccuracies, persistent omission of competing frameworks, and logical equivocations categorically disqualify it from publication in any top-tier philosophy of mind or cognitive science journal.

## 1. The Epistemic Framework and the Immunization of the Dualist Presupposition

The foundational methodological error of the manuscript lies in its overarching epistemological approach to clinical pathology. The author treats the brain not as the biological generator of phenomenal experience, but as a biological transceiver for a non-physical, independent subject. Consequently, whenever local damage produces a bizarre subjective state—such as the denial of paralysis (anosognosia) or the ability to navigate a room without conscious visual experience (blindsight)—the manuscript claims that the "channel" has degraded, but the fundamental "signal source" remains intact.

This approach constitutes a profound failure of the philosophy of science, specifically regarding the falsifiability of empirical theories of consciousness. The manuscript demands that materialist frameworks cross the "explanatory gap" for specific clinical syndromes, yet it shields its own "interface" construct from corresponding empirical scrutiny by deploying an immunizing strategy. By defining the interface as that which merely filters or routes pre-existing phenomenal consciousness, the author creates a conceptually sterile and entirely unfalsifiable paradigm.

The manuscript ignores the stringent criteria established in the contemporary literature for what constitutes a valid, testable theory of consciousness. Doerig, Schurger, and Herzog (2021) in *Cognitive Neurodynamics* outline the "hard criteria for empirical theories of consciousness," specifically noting the requirement that any valid theory must be capable of surviving the "unfolding argument" and providing a non-trivial, empirically testable metric to distinguish a conscious system from a functionally identical, non-conscious system.

If consciousness is a unified, non-physical entity utilizing the brain as an interface, the manuscript owes the reader a rigorous, mathematically or biologically grounded definition of what specific neural mechanism constitutes this "coupling" boundary. Instead, the author engages in perpetual boundary-substitution. When structural neuroimaging reveals physical pathways for cross-hemispheric communication (as in partial callosotomies), the author claims this is evidence of the interface's "resilience." When communication breaks down entirely, the author claims the "receiver is damaged" while the mind remains whole. A hypothesis that accommodates all possible neurological outcomes explains nothing.

It merely retrofits neuroscientific vocabulary onto Cartesian dualism, ignoring how modern computational neuroscience has dissolved the very paradoxes the author claims are intractable for physicalism.

## 2. Split-Brain Pathologies, the Misappropriation of fMRI Synchrony, and the Conflation of Agency with Phenomenal Unity

The manuscript’s treatment of split-brain syndrome serves as the primary empirical pillar for its argument regarding the "resilience of unity." The author asserts that if consciousness were identical to the brain’s integrated physical activity, surgically severing the corpus callosum should produce a clean, unmistakable bifurcation of the subject. Because clinical observation supposedly does not yield a perfectly clean split, the manuscript concludes that phenomenal unity belongs to consciousness itself—as a fundamental property of the interface signal—rather than its physical substrate.

To support this ambitious claim, the manuscript leans heavily on a highly specific and remarkably distorted interpretation of recent data, most notably the October 2025 *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences* (PNAS) study by Santander et al.. Santander and colleagues examined six adult callosotomy patients (five male, one female), four of whom had complete transections and two of whom had partial preservation of the splenium. Utilizing functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) resting-state functional connectivity (FC) matrices, they demonstrated that "full interhemispheric integration [can be] sustained by a fraction of posterior callosal fibers," showing that even minimal structural connectivity can maintain complex network architecture and synchronized BOLD signals across the hemispheres.

### The Phenomenal versus Agentive Equivocation

The manuscript weaponizes this specific neuroimaging finding to argue that physical connectivity does not cleanly dictate phenomenal integration, thereby suggesting a non-physical locus of unity. The text argues that the "2025 findings complicate connectivity-based readings: minimal callosal connections produce full neural synchronization," which is then explicitly mapped to the preservation of a unified conscious subject.

This represents a catastrophic misreading of both the Santander 2025 study and the broader, highly nuanced split-brain literature of the current decade. The manuscript completely conflates *functional synchrony* (the statistical co-fluctuation of blood-oxygen-level-dependent signals across cortical hemispheres over time) with *phenomenal binding* (the subjective, first-person experience of a unified visual field or singular selfhood). Santander et al. explicitly limit their claims to network dynamics and macroscopic whole-brain organization. They point to polysynaptic routing, subcortical pathways (such as the superior colliculus and thalamic relays), and the synchronization of external sensory inputs during the resting-state scan as the physical mechanisms responsible for maintaining functional coherence. They do not, at any point, claim that a severed cortex maintains a single phenomenal mind via non-physical entanglement or an immaterial interface.

More egregiously, the manuscript utterly ignores the definitive philosophical and empirical framework established by Schechter and Bayne (2021) in *Neuropsychologia*, which directly addresses this exact debate over split-brain unity. The manuscript clings to the outdated and heavily criticized Pinto et al. (2017) model of "divided perception but undivided consciousness", failing to recognize that Schechter and Bayne completely dismantled this model's theoretical foundations in the 2020s.

Schechter and Bayne demonstrate conclusively that while split-brain subjects may maintain a "unity of agency"—the ability to orchestrate unified behavior through subcortical cross-cueing, ipsilateral motor pathways, and continuous environmental feedback—they robustly exhibit a breakdown in *phenomenal unity*. A patient may act like a single agent to navigate a room or coordinate their hands, but they fundamentally lack the conjoint phenomenal experience of stimuli presented simultaneously to opposite visual hemifields. Schechter and Bayne advance a "layered unity model," noting that while global unity of agency might be preserved, local phenomenal unity is demonstrably shattered.

By willfully ignoring the distinction between the unity of behavioral agency and the unity of subjective experience, the manuscript commits the exact functionalist reductionism it claims to despise. It looks at the unified behavior of a split-brain patient, looks at the fMRI synchrony in the Santander study, and erroneously concludes that a single, indivisible subject must still be operating the machine. This ignores the vast literature showing that a disconnected cortical hemisphere can possess its own isolated experiential stream, rendering the manuscript's thesis on the absolute "indivisibility" of the subject empirically bankrupt. If phenomenal unity can be split by a scalpel, the interface hypothesis fails. The manuscript circumvents this failure only by ignoring the literature that documents the split.

## 3. Blindsight, Perceptual Degradation, and the Misappropriation of Signal Detection Theory

The manuscript’s handling of blindsight is similarly compromised by highly selective citation, an evasion of mainstream scientific consensus, and a profound misunderstanding of contemporary psychophysics. Blindsight—the well-documented phenomenon where patients with lesions to the primary visual cortex (V1) can accurately guess the location, orientation, or movement of visual stimuli despite claiming to be completely blind in the affected contralesional hemifield—is traditionally viewed as the ultimate clinical dissociation between performance (unconscious visual processing) and awareness (phenomenal consciousness).

To salvage its interface theory, the manuscript must eliminate the possibility of truly unconscious, highly accurate visual processing. If complex, goal-directed visual processing can occur entirely in the dark, without any phenomenal accompaniment, it implies that the brain can perform complex cognitive tasks without the dualist "interface" being active, rendering consciousness functionally epiphenomenal or entirely redundant. To avoid this fatal implication, the manuscript adopts the heterodox position advanced by Ian Phillips (2021), arguing that blindsight is not unconscious vision at all, but rather "qualitatively degraded conscious vision" (QDC).

According to Phillips's view, which the manuscript adopts wholesale, blindsight patients actually *do* have conscious experiences of the stimuli in their blind fields. However, because these experiences are so dim, hazy, and degraded compared to normal vision, the patients adopt a highly conservative "response bias," refusing to label the degraded experience as "seeing." The manuscript uses this to argue that consciousness is a fundamental, graded property of the interface, rather than an emergent threshold, and that no true dissociation between performance and awareness actually exists.

### The Ignored Mathematical Rebuttals in Signal Detection Theory

While Phillips's (2021) argument represents a known minority position in the literature, the manuscript presents it as a settled empirical triumph for non-reductive theory while entirely ignoring the devastating, mathematically rigorous rebuttals published immediately thereafter. Specifically, the manuscript fails to address Michel and Lau's (2021) critique in *Psychological Review*, which systematically dismantled the QDC hypothesis using the very Signal Detection Theory (SDT) framework that Phillips attempted to leverage.

Signal Detection Theory strictly distinguishes between a subject's perceptual sensitivity to a signal (d′d'd′) and their response criterion or bias for reporting that signal (ccc). Michel and Lau mathematically demonstrate that the response-bias explanation for blindsight is completely implausible in documented cases of "performance matching". In tightly controlled psychophysical experiments, researchers can match a blindsight patient's performance (d′d'd′) in their blind field to their performance in their sighted field (for example, by heavily degrading the contrast of the stimulus presented to the intact sighted field).

When perceptual sensitivity is exactly matched across the two visual fields, the patient *still* reports conscious awareness in the sighted field but explicitly denies it in the blind field. If blindsight were merely degraded consciousness masked by a globally conservative response criterion, matching the objective signal-to-noise ratio and task performance should equalize the subjective reports of awareness. It emphatically does not. The dissociation remains robust even when sensitivity is controlled, proving that the lack of awareness is not a mere artifact of a conservative decision threshold.

By omitting this fatal counterargument, the manuscript builds a theoretical house of cards. If genuine blindsight exists—if the brain truly processes and acts upon complex visual information completely independent of phenomenal awareness—then the manuscript’s "Capability Division Problem" is entirely an artifact of its own misreading of the literature. The manuscript conscripts Phillips to fight a metaphysical war against physicalism, but ignores the empirical reality that blindsight patients exhibit feature-specific discriminatory abilities (e.g., navigating physical obstacles, accurately catching thrown objects) that simply cannot be explained by the mere "feature-agnostic" dim conscious salience proposed by the QDC hypothesis. The interface hypothesis collapses when confronted with actual, performance-matched psychophysical data.

## 4. Active Inference, Functional Neurological Disorder, and the Redundancy of the Interface

The manuscript attempts to assimilate anosognosia for hemiplegia (the persistent denial of paralysis following a stroke) and Functional Neurological Disorder (FND, formerly known as conversion disorder) into its dualist framework. To do this, the author posits an architecture comprising "ascending" and "descending" interface channels. For anosognosia, the manuscript hypothesizes a "reversible routing failure on the ascending leg," where the self-monitoring channel fails to deliver error signals to the conscious subject. For FND, it posits a functional override of the descending motor output channel, where the interface is effectively hijacked.

While the manuscript correctly identifies that modern conceptualizations of FND involve an "abnormally precise motor prior that actively generates the symptom" (citing older literature like Edwards 2012 and Voon 2010), it fails catastrophically to recognize that the contemporary iterations of these very models explicitly eliminate the need for any dualist interface. The manuscript is seemingly oblivious to the massive, field-wide consolidation of Predictive Processing (PP) and Active Inference models of FND and anosognosia in the 2022–2025 literature.

### The Exhaustive Explanatory Power of Active Inference

In the modern Predictive Processing framework (e.g., Edwards, 2022; Hallett et al., 2022, *The Lancet Neurology*), the brain operates as an advanced Bayesian inference engine. Its fundamental imperative is to minimize prediction error by either updating its internal generative models to match sensory input (perception) or moving the body to alter sensory input to fulfill the prediction (active inference). Under this paradigm, FND and anosognosia are not "fractures" in an interface between a non-physical mind and a physical brain. They are elegant, entirely mechanistic pathologies of *precision weighting*.

In Functional Neurological Disorder, a highly weighted (hyper-precise), abnormal prior expectation regarding movement (e.g., an expectation of a severe tremor or total limb paralysis) is generated and sent down the cortical hierarchy. Because this prior is assigned immense precision, the ascending sensory evidence that contradicts it (the actual, healthy position of the limb) is down-weighted and ignored by the system. Through active inference, the brain literally forces the body to move (or paralyzes it) to fulfill the aberrant prediction. Crucially, because this movement is driven by a non-conscious prior rather than a deliberate, high-level intention, the subject experiences the movement as entirely involuntary or "alien".

Similarly, in anosognosia, damage to the specific right-hemisphere networks responsible for interoceptive and proprioceptive precision-weighting means the brain simply cannot update its prior belief that the limb is healthy and capable of movement. The ascending prediction errors indicating paralysis are dismissed as irrelevant noise. The patient is not "cut off" from a dualist monitor; their physical Bayesian updating system is mathematically broken.

The manuscript’s attempt to overlay a dualist "interface" onto this computationally complete physicalist framework is entirely superfluous and violates the principle of explanatory parsimony (Occam's razor). The PP model already explains *why* the patient feels the way they do, *how* the symptom is mechanically generated, and *why* it can be reversible (e.g., by shifting attention or precision weights via third-person observation or physical therapy). By failing to test its interface hypothesis against the contemporary, computationally rigorous Active Inference models of these disorders, the manuscript commits a fatal sin of omission. It invents a metaphysical problem where a perfectly functional, empirically verified computational mechanism already exists. The "interface" does no explanatory work; it is mere homuncular residue.

## 5. Integrated Information Theory (IIT 4.0) and the Evasion of the Cogitate Falsification

In its attempt to explain how spatially distributed neural processes bind together to form a unified conscious subject (the binding problem), the manuscript borrows heavily from Integrated Information Theory (IIT). The author references the mature mathematical formalism of IIT 4.0 (Albantakis et al., 2023), correctly noting its emphasis on a "maximally irreducible cause-effect structure" (Φ\PhiΦ). The manuscript attempts to appropriate IIT's formalism as the physical correlate of its non-physical interface, suggesting that physical integration is the necessary material condition for the dualist mind to "couple" with the brain.

However, the manuscript’s treatment of IIT betrays a staggering ignorance of the most significant empirical event in consciousness science of the current decade: the 2025 publication of the Cogitate Consortium's adversarial collaboration in *Nature*.

### The Adversarial Falsification of Spatial and Temporal Predictions

The Cogitate project (Melloni, Ferrante, et al., 2025) was a massive, pre-registered, multi-modal (fMRI, MEG, and intracranial EEG) adversarial collaboration pitting IIT directly against its primary rival, the Global Neuronal Workspace Theory (GNWT). The experimental design was unprecedented: proponents of both theories agreed in advance on specific, incompatible predictions, and independent laboratories collected the data to prevent confirmatory bias.

IIT specifically predicted that conscious experience corresponds to sustained, short-range connectivity within a "posterior cortical hot zone" (encompassing occipital, temporal, and parietal areas), and that this specific cause-effect structure should be maintained for the exact duration of the conscious percept. Conversely, GNWT predicted that consciousness involves a late, frontoparietal "ignition"—a sudden, all-or-nothing broadcasting of content across a global workspace.

The results, published in *Nature* and widely debated in the 2025 literature, provided severe empirical challenges to *both* theories, but proved particularly devastating for the specific axioms of IIT the manuscript relies upon. Crucially for the manuscript's thesis, IIT's specific prediction regarding sustained, highly synchronized activity in the posterior cortex for the full duration of a conscious percept was not cleanly confirmed by the neuroimaging data. The structural and temporal boundaries of the hypothesized Φ\PhiΦ-structure proved far more fluid, transient, and task-dependent than the rigid "cause-effect structure" axioms demanded. While posterior regions were heavily involved, the exact mechanisms of integration required by IIT 4.0 were challenged by the temporal decay of the signal during sustained perception.

The manuscript’s failure to even mention the Cogitate Consortium's findings is unacceptable for an article purporting to map the "functional anatomy of the mind-brain channel." The author relies on IIT's theoretical architecture to ground the "resilience of unity" argument and explain perceptual binding, yet the very neurobiological markers IIT uses to define that unity have been subjected to intense, highly publicized empirical friction.

You cannot build a sprawling metaphysical argument upon a specific neuroscientific theory while completely ignoring the 2025 *Nature* paper that explicitly tested—and partially soft-falsified—that exact theory's core physiological predictions. Furthermore, the manuscript entirely misrepresents the GNWT perspective. It treats "illusionism" and reductive functionalism as monolithic strawmen, ignoring how GNWT's requirement for global broadcasting elegantly accounts for the distinction between conscious reportability and localized, unconscious sensory processing (such as the processing that occurs in authentic blindsight). By sheltering its dualist interface behind the unverified axioms of IIT while actively suppressing the messy, contradictory reality of the Cogitate empirical data, the manuscript reveals itself as an exercise in philosophical confirmation bias rather than rigorous scientific inquiry.
