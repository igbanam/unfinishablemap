# Audit: “Miswanting: When a Forecast Error Changes the Choice”

## Overall assessment

The article has a **strong conceptual core but two materially overstated empirical conclusions**. Its most valuable contribution is to distinguish:

1. an inaccurate affective forecast;
2. a choice associated with that forecast;
3. experimental evidence that changing the forecast changes the choice; and
4. a choice that is demonstrably worse by a specified later-experience or welfare criterion.

Much of the affective-forecasting literature establishes only the first level. The article is right to demand more before calling something a demonstrated *mischoice*. But it sometimes moves without warning between the very strict fourth criterion and the much broader second or third criteria. That scope shift produces its two central errors.

The main verdicts are:

- **The claim that Hsee and Zhang’s Study 3 is the sole forecast-driven case is false under the ordinary meaning of “a forecast error changes choice.”** Dorison, Minson and Rogers supplied a five-study programme in which faulty forecasts were measured and forecast-correcting interventions changed actual information-exposure choices, with a large preregistered replication and a further conceptual replication.
- **Hsee and Zhang may still be the only located paradigm meeting the article’s much narrower criterion of a forecast-associated choice followed by a reversal in experienced hedonic ranking.** That claim needs to be stated explicitly and modestly.
- **Calling Hsee and Zhang Study 3 simply “unreplicated” is incomplete.** There appears to be no independent published replication of its choice–experience result, but Hsee and Zhang themselves reported an unpublished strategic replication using audiovisual stimuli.
- **The claim that nobody measured felt anticipation at choice is false.** Schlösser, Dunning and Fetchenhauer measured option-directed immediate emotions immediately before real choices; one study also measured emotional response after the realised outcome. Young, Shuster and Mikels later measured immediate option-directed affect before choice, and other recent work has measured pre-choice affect or closely related anticipatory states.
- **The narrower causal question remains open:** I found no study that cleanly shows that consciously felt anticipatory valence, independently of learned value, beliefs and attention, misranks two options, causes the choice, and is then contradicted by later experience.
- The article’s included citations are generally represented carefully. Its chief problems are **literature omission, over-broad negative claims, an incomplete replication account, and an over-neat alignment of Gilbert–Wilson with Berridge**.
- The article is transparent that its value-sensitive position is retained on prior commitments rather than supported by this evidence. That is commendable. Nevertheless, some passages protect the position by stipulating an unusually demanding evidential test and then describing the existing evidence as warranting exactly no update.

The changelog explains how the problem arose. The research pass consulted only 25 sources, was explicitly not systematic, left some sources at abstract level, did not reach one relevant article, and read another only through a summariser. ## 1. The “single forecast-driven case” claim

The first challenged passage is:

> “only reversal shown to be carried by a forecast” [[miswanting|concepts/miswanting]]

### What Hsee and Zhang actually establish

The article accurately reports the basic pattern in Study 3. Most participants preferred the unpleasant-task/large-chocolate combination, while both experimentally assigned experiencers and self-choosers reported greater happiness with the pleasant-task/small-chocolate combination. A separate prediction group produced forecasts matching the majority choice. Hsee and Zhang themselves called the self-choice comparison a less direct test because participants selected themselves into conditions and had seen both alternatives. [Warrington College of Business+1](https://bear.warrington.ufl.edu/brenner/mar7588/Papers/hsee-zhang-jpsp2004.pdf)

That is unusually close to the article’s desired chain:
forecast ranking → choice ranking ≠ experienced ranking.

But it still does not prove that the forecasts *caused* the choices:

- Forecasts and choices were measured in different groups for the cleanest comparison.
- The self-choice comparison is observational after self-selection.
- No forecast was experimentally shifted independently of the option descriptions.
- Choice itself may have altered later experience through dissonance or justification; Hsee and Zhang noted evidence suggestive of this.
- Only the selected option was experienced by each self-chooser, so the personal counterfactual remained unobserved. [Warrington College of Business+1](https://bear.warrington.ufl.edu/brenner/mar7588/Papers/hsee-zhang-jpsp2004.pdf)

The Map therefore overstates matters with “carried by a forecast.” A more accurate formulation would be **“the choice ranking matched an independently measured forecast ranking that reversed the later between-condition experience ranking.”**

### It is not wholly unreplicated

The statement that there is no independent replication may be right, provided “independent” and “published” are retained. Anvari and colleagues replicated only Hsee and Zhang’s first two misprediction studies, not Study 3’s choice–experience comparison.

But the original paper reports a strategic replication attributed to Hsee and Zhang (2003). It used two audiovisual episodes differing in picture pleasantness and noise intensity. Choosers preferred one episode while experiencers were happier with the other, reproducing the choice–experience inconsistency. Because the authors conducted it themselves and appear to have reported it only within the 2004 article, it is neither independent nor a satisfactory modern replication. It nevertheless means that “unreplicated” without qualification is inaccurate. [Warrington College of Business](https://bear.warrington.ufl.edu/brenner/mar7588/Papers/hsee-zhang-jpsp2004.pdf)

The article should say:

> No independent published replication of Hsee and Zhang’s Study 3 choice–experience result was located. The original authors reported an unpublished strategic replication with different stimuli.

### Dorison and colleagues are a major omitted counterexample

Dorison, Minson and Rogers conducted five studies on selective exposure to opposing political views. Studies 1–3 compared forecasted with experienced affect. Studies 4–5 tested whether correcting faulty affective forecasts changed actual exposure choices; Studies 2, 3 and 5 were preregistered, and Study 4 received a large preregistered replication. [CHARLES DORISON](https://www.charlesdorison.com/uploads/1/2/4/4/124452321/dorisonminsonrogers_cognition.pdf)

In Study 4, participants expected opposing political speech to feel substantially more negative than it subsequently did. A forecast-correction treatment made forecasts less negative and changed which senators’ material participants selected. Study 5 used information about previous participants’ forecasted and experienced reactions and again reduced own-party selective exposure; the authors reported a 34% reduction relative to the distance between the control result and even-handed selection. They explicitly presented Study 5 and the preregistered Study 4 replication as converging evidence that reducing faulty affective forecasts reduces selective exposure. [CHARLES DORISON+2](https://www.charlesdorison.com/uploads/1/2/4/4/124452321/dorisonminsonrogers_cognition.pdf)

This directly contradicts any broad claim that Hsee and Zhang provide the only demonstrated case in which forecast error changes choice.

It is **not**, however, a complete counterexample to the article’s strict welfare criterion:

- Watching opposing content is not automatically better in experienced-utility terms.
- Participants could rationally value information, epistemic diversity or civic engagement despite a somewhat unpleasant experience.
- The interventions changed information and beliefs about other people’s experiences, not an isolated internal forecast variable.
- In Study 4 the intervention also changed reported experienced affect, leaving demand effects or broader construal changes as possibilities.
- The studies establish a change in selective exposure, not a revealed reversal between the hedonic ranking of two fully experienced options.

The correct conclusion is therefore two-tiered:

> **Broad causal claim:** Hsee and Zhang are not alone; Dorison and colleagues provide replicated intervention evidence that correcting affective forecasts can change real choices.
> **Strict welfare-reversal claim:** Hsee and Zhang may remain the only located paradigm combining forecast–choice correspondence with a reversed later hedonic ranking of the options.

### The wider intervention literature also matters

A 2018 meta-analysis examined 37 publications, 72,020 participants and 133 independent effects from interventions targeting anticipated regret or anticipated affect. The interventions changed immediate behaviour, with a pooled effect of d = .29, as well as intentions and anticipated regret. The construct pool is heterogeneous and generally does not establish forecast *error*, later experience or welfare reversal. It therefore cannot replace Hsee and Zhang, but it makes the article’s depiction of forecast-guided behaviour as resting on one isolated study misleading. [OUP Academic](https://academic.oup.com/abm/article/52/2/157/4801248)

## 2. Felt anticipation has been measured at choice

The second challenged passage is:

> “measured the felt anticipation present at choice” [[miswanting|concepts/miswanting]]

The article says no located study did this, and later strengthens the claim to no study measuring the felt state at choice alongside later experience. That is factually wrong. [[miswanting|concepts/miswanting]]

### Schlösser, Dunning and Fetchenhauer

Their article defines immediate emotions as the hot, visceral feelings experienced while contemplating a particular option “at the cusp” of deciding, and reports four studies in which immediate emotions predicted risky choices over and above anticipated emotions and subjective outcome probabilities. It was first published online in 2011 and appeared in the 2013 journal volume. [Wiley Online Library+1](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/bdm.757)

The detailed Study 4 procedure is especially damaging to the Map’s negative claim:

- 108 participants made a real incentivised Ellsberg-style choice.
- Before choosing, they rated their immediate emotional states concerning each urn.
- They also rated anticipated emotions associated with possible outcomes.
- The outcome was then realised.
- They reported their emotional state after learning the outcome.
- Immediate emotion, rather than anticipated outcome emotion, mediated part of the ambiguity effect. [Universität zu Köln+1](https://kups.ub.uni-koeln.de/2770/1/Dissertation_T_M_Schloesser_2009_Deciding_Emotions_revised300609.pdf)

This is not merely a global mood rating. It measures option-directed present affect immediately before the decision and later outcome-related affect in the same design. It therefore directly refutes the literal sentence that no design measured felt state at choice alongside later experience.

It does **not** establish the proposition the Map ultimately needs:

- It did not identify a welfare-reversing mischoice.
- Immediate emotion was measured, not independently manipulated.
- Mediation is not proof that the phenomenally felt component caused the decision.
- Immediate emotion may have reflected uncertainty, attention or an incipient choice rather than an autonomous evaluative input.

Those are reasons to qualify its significance, not reasons to omit it.

### Young, Shuster and Mikels

Young and colleagues presented immediate-affect probes before risky choices, asking how participants presently felt about each option, alongside separate anticipated-affect questions. In their second experiment the order of immediate and anticipated probes was counterbalanced. Immediate affect toward the sure option mediated the framing effect; anticipated affect did not. The samples were small—only 24 participants in Experiment 2—and there was no later experienced-utility comparison, so the result is suggestive rather than decisive. But it plainly measures option-directed present feeling at choice. [DePaul Emotion and Cognition Laboratory+1](https://emocoglab.depaul.edu/publications/journals/Young_Shuster_Mikels_2018.pdf)

### Asutay and Västfjäll

In an incentivised risky-choice task with 101 participants, self-reported arousal before choice was associated with greater loss aversion, risk aversion and choice consistency. The relevant signal was mainly arousal rather than option-specific valence, and the evidence was model-based and correlational. It is therefore not a test of the Map’s precise valence hypothesis, but it further invalidates any general suggestion that present felt affect has not been measured around the point of choice. [Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-022-14810-w.pdf)

### Grimani and colleagues

Two 2024 studies, including an incentivised moderate-stakes experiment and a US representative sample, separately measured anticipatory and anticipated emotions and found that both statistically mediated effects of value and framing, with anticipatory emotion accounting for a larger share in several analyses. [Springer](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s00426-024-02001-3.pdf)

This study does **not** rescue the Map’s literal moment-of-choice claim, because participants answered the emotion questions after each decision. Its authors label the questions “anticipatory” because they concern how participants presently feel about choosing the options, but the temporal order permits choice to produce or rationalise the report. That limitation should itself become part of the article’s methodological discussion. [Springer](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s00426-024-02001-3.pdf)

### The defensible residual claim

After correcting the literature, a narrower and important gap remains:

> No located study cleanly demonstrates that a *misranked consciously felt anticipatory valence* causes a choice, independently of learned value, beliefs and attention, and is subsequently contradicted by a lower experienced-utility outcome.

That is substantially different from saying felt anticipation has not been measured. The first is an unresolved causal and ontological question; the second is an incorrect literature claim.

## 3. The forecast’s causal role is not “everywhere inferred”

The third challenged passage is:

> “forecast’s causal role is everywhere inferred” [[miswanting|concepts/miswanting]]

Dorison and colleagues randomised forecast-correcting information and observed changed behavioural choices. That goes beyond purely observational inference. Their studies do not identify a perfectly isolated latent forecast mediator, but they are causal interventions directed at forecast error. [CHARLES DORISON+1](https://www.charlesdorison.com/uploads/1/2/4/4/124452321/dorisonminsonrogers_cognition.pdf)

The article’s stronger formulation—no study changed a forecast while holding every other input fixed—may still be defensible, but it requires clarification. A forecast is constituted by beliefs, simulations, remembered experiences and present affect. Manipulating it while literally holding all these fixed may be psychologically incoherent. A practical test should instead require:

- random assignment of forecast-relevant information or imagery;
- an observed change in the forecast;
- no material change in objective incentives or outcome distributions;
- measured checks on present affect, attention, perceived probability and learned value;
- a behavioural choice endpoint;
- later experience;
- causal mediation analysed with appropriate assumptions stated.

Even then, randomising the intervention does not automatically randomise the mediator. Unmeasured mediator–outcome confounding remains possible. The article is right not to equate mediation with proof of a phenomenal causal vehicle, but wrong to describe the entire literature as devoid of causal intervention evidence.

## 4. What the post-2015 literature changes

### Forecasts can influence behaviour without producing strict hedonic mischoice

Beyond Dorison and the health-intervention meta-analysis, recent longitudinal evidence connects forecast accuracy with behaviour. A 2025 study followed 857 people across eight health behaviours for three months. More accurate anticipated affect was associated with greater behaviour performance and later motivation after adjustment for intentions, habit and related variables. Because forecast accuracy was defined retrospectively from the difference between anticipated and experienced affect, the result remains observational and vulnerable to third variables; it is evidence of ecological association, not causal miswanting. [White Rose Research Online](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/234615/)

The proper conclusion is not “only one study links forecasts to choice.” It is that **only a much smaller subset connects forecasts, choices and later experience strongly enough to support a welfare-reversal interpretation**.

### Forecast accuracy is component-specific

Carlson and colleagues’ four studies found that people reported relying more on forecast emotional intensity than on frequency or duration in decisions about residency ranking, university applications, voting and travel. Where accuracy was assessed, intensity forecasts were more accurate than frequency or duration forecasts. This matters because a forecast can be inaccurate in duration but still correctly rank live options on the dimension actually driving choice. [Springer](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s11031-023-10007-4.pdf)

The neighbouring forecasting article already recognises this point, but the miswanting article does not import it. The latter therefore presents forecast error as more uniformly choice-threatening than current evidence warrants. [[affective-forecasting-gap|concepts/affective-forecasting-gap]]

### Everyday forecasts are often relatively accurate

Moeck and colleagues’ 2026 experience-sampling and diary studies found that people were generally able to predict which days or events would feel better or worse than usual, while making comparatively small errors in absolute affect level. This distinction between **relative accuracy** and **absolute level accuracy** is crucial for the Map: a selector needs the correct ordering of options, not exact magnitude calibration. [Springer](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s42761-026-00364-x.pdf)

The article’s strict rank-reversal standard is therefore theoretically sound. What is unsound is treating the absence of many rank-reversal demonstrations as if it also implied an absence of evidence that forecasts influence choices.

### Recent joint-versus-separate evaluation evidence should be included

Issa and colleagues’ 2026 experiment randomised 1,500 participants across anticipated versus experienced warm glow, joint versus separate evaluation and helping one versus six children, using real donations. It found overestimation of warm glow, but also showed that scope sensitivity depended on the evaluation format and that participants sometimes accurately anticipated their own scope insensitivity. This is directly relevant to Hsee and Zhang’s joint-/separate-evaluation mechanism and demonstrates that the modern picture is not simply “joint prediction exaggerates a difference that separate experience erases.” [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42330892/)

### The loss-aversion discussion is too binary

The article contrasts Kermer’s forecast-error interpretation with Charpentier’s decision-weighting interpretation. That is a useful distinction, and the summaries are broadly faithful. But newer evidence indicates at least three separable contributors:

- anticipated outcome emotion;
- immediate or anticipatory option-directed affect;
- the decision rule that weights those signals.

Young and colleagues found immediate affect, rather than anticipated affect, explaining their framing effect. Grimani and colleagues found both measures statistically involved, although their post-choice measurement prevents clean temporal interpretation. Asutay and Västfjäll found pre-choice arousal related to loss and risk parameters. The current literature therefore does not support a simple Kermer-versus-Charpentier fork; the location of the asymmetry varies with task, timing and measurement. [DePaul Emotion and Cognition Laboratory+3](https://emocoglab.depaul.edu/publications/journals/Young_Shuster_Mikels_2018.pdf)

## 5. Gilbert and Wilson: mostly accurate, but over-formalised

The article correctly reports Gilbert and Wilson’s stipulative definition: their “wanting” is a prediction of later “liking.” Their discussion also explicitly combines imagined details, beliefs about reactions and present gut responses. The article accurately recognises the latter later in its discussion of misattribution. [[miswanting|concepts/miswanting]]

Three qualifications are needed.

### “Experienced utility” is the Map’s translation

Gilbert and Wilson describe liking in terms of how something affects well-being, satisfaction, happiness and contentment. Mapping that to Kahnemanian experienced utility is reasonable, but it is an interpretive translation rather than a source-level identity. Their discussion is not committed to the Map’s more exact distinction between instantaneous phenomenal valence, temporal-integral utility and remembered assessment.

The text should therefore say **“roughly corresponds to experienced utility”**, not that it simply *is* consciously felt experienced utility.

### Their account is not a purely cool prediction model

The opening currently makes Gilbert–Wilson wanting look like a propositional forecast placed neatly on a predicted-utility row. But their own model is hybrid: imagery, beliefs and present affect all contribute. The later section acknowledges this, creating an internal tension with the opening. A present “gut reaction” may be both an occurrent affective state and an ingredient in the forecast, so the Map cannot sharply divide cool forecast judgement from felt vehicle without an operational rule for mixed cases.

### The reference conflates editions

The bibliography gives the original 2000 chapter and notes its 2006 reprinting, but attaches the DOI belonging to Chapter 30 of *The Construction of Preference*, whose print publication year is 2006. This is not a false citation—the text is a reprint—but it should be represented as either two references or as a 2000 original followed by a separately identified 2006 reprint DOI. [[miswanting|concepts/miswanting]]

## 6. Berridge: not the “reverse” of Gilbert and Wilson

The article’s contrast is directionally useful but taxonomically misleading.

Berridge and Robinson use quoted “wanting” for incentive salience: cue-triggered motivational attraction supported by mesolimbic systems. They explicitly distinguish it from ordinary cognitive wanting, which has a declarative goal. Ordinarily the two align, but incentive salience can oppose conscious goals or occur without reportable subjective desire. Their “liking” means hedonic impact, operationalised through behavioural and neural reactions, and is not automatically identical to conscious pleasure. [LSA Technology Services](https://sites.lsa.umich.edu/berridge-lab/wp-content/uploads/sites/743/2019/10/2016-Berridge-Robinson-Liking-wanting-IS-theory-of-addiction-Am-Psychol.pdf)

Gilbert–Wilson wanting and Berridge “wanting” are therefore not reversals of one another. They answer different questions:

- Gilbert–Wilson: what does a person predict will improve later affect?
- Berridge: what motivational process makes a cue attractive and energises pursuit?
- Kahneman: what predicted, decision, instant and remembered utility variables are being distinguished?
- The Map: which, if any, is consciously felt and causally operative in selection?

Berridge incentive salience can be influenced by predictions and normally cohere with ordinary desire, even though it can dissociate from them. Calling the taxonomies “reverse” suggests a symmetric inversion that does not exist.

The neighbouring wanting/liking article is much more precise. It calls Berridge’s liking an operational hedonic reaction rather than necessarily felt pleasure, distinguishes ordinary cognitive wanting from incentive salience, and explicitly warns that the dissociation does not support dualism. That terminology should be made canonical across the cluster. [[wanting-liking-and-the-value-in-mechanism-fork|topics/wanting-liking-and-the-value-in-mechanism-fork]]

## 7. Missed counterarguments

### Anticipatory emotion may be an output of deciding

The article treats the main alternative as an unfelt learned-value system that runs alongside feeling. It misses a more specific causal alternative: anticipatory emotion may be generated by the emerging decision rather than supplied as its input.

Davis, Love and Maddox explicitly contrasted emotions-as-input with emotions-as-outcome. In their task, anticipatory physiological responses tracked response uncertainty rather than stimulus value, supporting the possibility that such responses arise from decision processing or attentional preparation. This does not settle the matter for self-reported valence, but it is a direct countermodel to the Map’s temporal picture. [PubMed Central (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2735832/)

### Measuring feeling can change the choice

Repeatedly asking participants how they feel about each option may:

- focus attention on affect;
- promote joint evaluation;
- cause deliberation;
- alter imagery;
- induce demand characteristics;
- itself create or amplify the affect being measured.

Young counterbalanced the order of immediate and anticipated probes, which helps with order effects but does not provide a no-probe control for measurement reactivity. Any proposed “felt currency” experiment needs passive measures, sparse experience sampling or separate measurement-validation cohorts.

### Mediation is not causal identification

Several recent studies report that emotion statistically mediates framing or value effects. Unless the mediator itself is randomised—or very strong sequential-ignorability assumptions are defensible—this does not show that feeling caused choice. Learned valuation, attention, response preparation or an incipient choice could generate both the report and behaviour.

The article is right to resist causal overinterpretation, but it should explain this standard rather than implying that the relevant states have never been measured.

### Later feeling is not automatically the correct welfare standard

The article acknowledges revealed preference but leaves the objection unexamined. That omission is load-bearing.

Different standards can reverse the verdict:

- temporal-integral pain favours the shorter cold-water episode;
- remembered experience may favour the longer episode with the better ending;
- willingness to repeat may track remembered rather than total utility;
- the extended colonoscopy can improve later health behaviour;
- political cross-exposure can serve epistemic or civic values despite unpleasantness;
- a choice can rationally sacrifice affect for duty, achievement, knowledge or another person.

A choice is not “miswanting” merely because later affect is lower. The article needs to distinguish at least:

1. **hedonic forecast error**;
2. **hedonic rank reversal**;
3. **all-things-considered preference error**;
4. **welfare loss under a defended normative theory**.

Hsee and Zhang demonstrate something close to the second. They do not by themselves establish the fourth.

### Only one realised option is usually observed

For self-choosers, later experience of the selected option is observed but personal experience of the forgone option is not. Between-participant comparisons substitute population averages for individual counterfactuals. Individual heterogeneity in tastes can therefore generate an apparent group reversal even when many individuals chose correctly for themselves.

Hsee and Zhang considered one version of this objection, but their response does not eliminate all treatment-effect heterogeneity. A modern design should use repeated choices, crossover exposure where ethically possible, or hierarchical models estimating person-specific treatment effects.

### Forecasts can be wrong in magnitude but right for choice

The Map understands this in the neighbouring forecasting article but does not fully propagate it here. Carlson and Moeck reinforce the point: the dimensions people report using most may be comparatively accurate, and everyday forecasts can preserve relative rankings despite absolute bias. [Springer+1](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s11031-023-10007-4.pdf)

## 8. Dualist tenet protection and evidential bracketing

### What the article handles well

The article is unusually candid that the value-blind account has a parsimony advantage and that the Map retains value-sensitive selection because of prior phenomenal-value commitments, not because the miswanting literature supplies independent evidence. It also gives P-VS1 low confidence and concedes that existing behavioural reversals are compatible with mechanism-only models. [[miswanting|concepts/miswanting]]

That is better than presenting affective forecasting as evidence for interactionist dualism.

### The operational standard is asymmetrical

The article accepts post-outcome rating scales as evidence of experienced utility while dismissing pre-choice rating scales as potentially cool judgements rather than felt anticipation. But Schlösser and Young explicitly asked how participants felt *now* about the alternatives. If self-report can operationalise experienced valence after an outcome, comparable present-tense self-report must at least count as evidence of anticipatory valence before choice.

Neither establishes ontological irreducibility or causal efficacy. The problem is the asymmetric evidential treatment, not that either measure is decisive.

### “The rival predicts every reversal” is too strong

Without a quantitative value-blind model specifying when reversals should occur, their direction and magnitude, the rival **accommodates** the findings rather than predicts each one. The same applies to the value-sensitive account. A generic learned-value mechanism and a generic felt selector are both flexible enough to fit almost any observed choice unless their inputs and transformation rules are specified.

The parent article correctly admits that no datum yet dissociates felt valence from computed value. That remains the central issue. Direct measurements of immediate feeling add relevant data, but they do not produce the required dissociation. [[valence-and-conscious-selection|topics/valence-and-conscious-selection]]

### “No confidence update” is insufficiently argued

Evidence that is equally likely under two specified models has a Bayes factor near one and need not update their relative odds. But the article has not specified likelihoods for either model. It instead declares all results shared and then fixes the confidence band.

There are at least three distinct updates that should not be conflated:

- The evidence increases confidence that forecasts and immediate affect can be behaviourally relevant.
- It does not establish that phenomenal feeling, as opposed to its neural implementation or correlate, is causally necessary.
- The continued success of mechanism-level accounts may increase their comparative plausibility, depending on prior complexity penalties.

Tenet 5 may reject a simplistic “fewer entities always wins” rule, but it does not license assigning the mechanistic account no evidential advantage. The Map needs a positive reason to expect observations that the mechanistic model does not already expect.

### The claimed “cost of the driver” is premature

The article calls miswanting the demonstrated cost of presently felt anticipation as a driver. Yet its own preceding analysis says the studies did not establish that feeling drove the choice. The proper conditional is:

> If presently felt anticipatory valence is the selection currency, forecast-induced misranking would be one cost it must inherit.

That is a framework-internal implication, not a demonstrated cost of an empirically established driver.

### The Many-Worlds passage is extraneous and overcompressed

Miswanting remains a coherent branch-relative question under Everettian interpretations: an agent anticipates future branch-relative experiences, has preferences over weighted outcomes, and can forecast them badly. What disappears is fundamental single-outcome selection, not the psychological distinction between forecast, choice and experienced affect. The article briefly acknowledges branch relativisation but then treats the psychological question as lacking a referent. That imports the Map’s separate anti-Everett argument rather than deriving anything from the miswanting literature. [[miswanting|concepts/miswanting]]

This material would be better placed in a small framework note rather than the empirical conclusion.

## 9. Integration with neighbouring articles

### The Affective-Forecasting Gap

This article is better calibrated than “Miswanting.” It distinguishes anticipated judgement from presently felt anticipatory emotion, recognises that choice requires ranking rather than precise magnitudes, cites component-specific accuracy and recent everyday-life evidence, and says that only rank reversals threaten the proposed selection currency. [[affective-forecasting-gap|concepts/affective-forecasting-gap]]

The integration problem is that “Miswanting” does not inherit that article’s current evidence base. It should explicitly separate:

- forecast error without choice evidence;
- forecast-associated choice;
- experimentally forecast-sensitive choice;
- immediate-affect-associated choice;
- later hedonic rank reversal;
- welfare loss.

Dorison belongs in the third category; Schlösser in the fourth and partly the later-experience category; Hsee in the fifth; none cleanly establishes the full phenomenal-causal chain.

### Wanting, Liking, and the Value-in-Mechanism Fork

This neighbouring article should govern the terminology. It correctly says that:

- incentive salience is not predicted utility;
- Berridge “liking” is not automatically conscious pleasure;
- motivation, hedonic reaction and conscious pleasure are distinct;
- the empirical dissociation is neutral between dualist and physicalist interpretations. [[wanting-liking-and-the-value-in-mechanism-fork|topics/wanting-liking-and-the-value-in-mechanism-fork]]

“Miswanting” presently compresses this into a supposed reversal of vocabulary. That should be replaced with a crosswalk among non-equivalent constructs.

### Negative-Valence Asymmetry

The weighting article initially says that negative value generally moves choice more strongly, but later concedes that the inference from choice asymmetry to felt-valence asymmetry is interpretive and that even the behavioural generalisation is contested. [[negative-valence-asymmetry-and-the-selection-weighting-function|concepts/negative-valence-asymmetry-and-the-selection-weighting-function]]

The miswanting article should feed newer evidence back into that page. Young, Asutay and Grimani indicate that immediate affect, anticipated affect and decision weighting can all contribute, depending on design. This weakens any suggestion that the empirical literature has located loss asymmetry cleanly on one canonical row.

### Valence and Conscious Selection

The parent article’s most defensible claim is that the value-sensitive theory requires a dissociation: at equivalent computed or learned value, choice must track independently varying felt valence. It admits that no such datum exists. [[valence-and-conscious-selection|topics/valence-and-conscious-selection]]

That verdict remains sound after correcting “Miswanting.” Schlösser and Young show that immediate feeling has been measured and predicts choice. They do not show that it varies independently of computed value or that its phenomenality contributes causal work. The correction should therefore change the Map’s account of the existing experiments, but not upgrade P-VS1.

## 10. Final verdict

The article’s central methodological insight survives:

> Affective forecast error alone is not evidence that forecast error changed a choice, and neither establishes that consciously felt anticipation was the causal selector.

But the current article overreaches in turning that insight into a literature verdict. The empirical record is better represented as follows:

- **Forecast errors are common but heterogeneous and often modest or ranking-preserving.**
- **Forecast-related constructs have repeatedly been associated with choices and behaviour.**
- **Randomised interventions aimed at forecasts have changed actual choices.**
- **Present, option-directed affect has been measured immediately around choice and sometimes predicts behaviour better than anticipated outcome emotion.**
- **Very few studies combine forecast, choice and later experience in a way that supports a hedonic rank-reversal interpretation.**
- **No located design completes the full causal chain required by the Map: consciously felt anticipatory misranking → choice, independently of computed value and attention → demonstrably worse later experience.**
- **Nothing in this literature independently supports interactionist dualism or phenomenal valence as a distinct selection variable.**

The article therefore needs **major empirical revision but not conceptual replacement**.
